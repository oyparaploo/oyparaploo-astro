*Lead-in:*
Written on the back of absence slips, found scattered in a Baltimore classroom after the school closed. Different handwriting on each slip. Someone had tried to put them in order, gave up. The janitor kept them because his daughter was in that class. Said it was the only thing that helped after his wife died.

Branch Holloway wasn't supposed to be teaching this. The real counselor—Ms. Williams, good salary, good insurance—walked out Tuesday. Left fifteen kids who'd all lost someone that year.

Baltimore City Public Schools. Metal detectors work. Counseling budget doesn't exist.

"I don't have a degree," Branch said. Same cardigan from yesterday. E.M. on the pocket. "But I know what it's like when your brain breaks and everything looks different."

"Different how?" Marcus asked. His father died at the Amazon warehouse. Wrong place, they said. As if there's a right place to die for $15.50 an hour.

"Like—" Branch touched between her eyebrows. Had a scar there. "You know how sometimes you can smell your dead grandmother making coffee? At 3 AM? You know she's dead but you smell it anyway?"

The kids looked at each other.

"That's not crazy," Branch said. "That's just your brain holding two true things at once. She's dead. She's making coffee. Both true."

The principal would've stopped this. But Branch was basically invisible. Substitute teachers make $13.47 an hour in Baltimore. Cheaper than real help.

"Close your eyes," she said. "Think about the person you lost. Are they gone or still here?"

"Gone," Jennifer said. Her mom overdosed. Fentanyl in fake Percocet.

"But you still talk to her."

"That's different—"

"No. That's the same. She's gone and she's there. Your brain knows both things."

Jennifer started crying. But nodding too.

Branch drew on the whiteboard. Marker barely worked.

"There's this thing. When you lose someone. Your brain can't pick one truth. So it picks both. They're dead. They're alive. They're nowhere. They're everywhere."

"That's insane," Troy said. His brother was killed by police. Traffic stop. The settlement was $3.2 million. He'd trade it for one more fight about cereal.

"Yeah. It is. But it's also how you survive. By being insane in the right way."

She taught them her method. Not from books. From her own shit—daughter in Virginia with her ex, marriage dead, career as a dancer extremely dead, her teeth dying without insurance.

One kid's grandfather just stopped waking up. No drama. Just done. Another's cousin drowned in the harbor. Someone's aunt had a heart attack at Dollar General. Not all deaths are symbolic. Some are just stupid.

"It's like this," Branch said. "After my daughter left—not dead, just gone—I kept making her lunch. Every day. PB&J, apple, juice box. Put it in the fridge. Threw it out at night. Made it again next morning."

"That's crazy," Pearl said. Lost her baby sister to SIDS while babysitting. Hadn't talked until now.

"Is it? Or is it the only sane response to insane loss?"

Pearl looked at her hands. "I still check the crib."

"How long?"

"Six months."

"That's not crazy. That's love with nowhere to go."

Week three, the principal showed up.

"What are you teaching them?"

"That dead people are still here."

"That's not in the curriculum—"

"Neither is their dead parents."

He had a photo on his desk. His son. Leukemia. Even good insurance couldn't save him.

"You can't teach them that—"

"I'm teaching them what they already know. That their dad is both at that warehouse and at home. That their mom is both overdosed and making dinner. That the brain doesn't pick one truth when both are true."

The kids started calling it "double-seeing." Not a third eye. Just using both eyes to see opposite things.

Marcus still saved half his sandwich for his dead father. Gas station sandwich. $3.99.

"That's stupid," someone said.

"No," Branch said. "That's his dad being dead and eating lunch. Both true."

Jennifer taught her sister. "Mom's gone but smell her cleaning supplies. She's there. In the Pine-Sol."

"That's not real—"

"Real is broken. We're all just picking which broken to believe."

Test scores went up. Nobody knew why. State sent someone to study it. They left confused. Something about grief making kids better at holding multiple answers, at seeing problems from opposing angles. They wrote a report. Nobody understood it.

Principal tried to fire Branch.

The kids protested by sitting in the hallway, eyes closed, seeing their dead people.

"This isn't helping them," he said.

"Your son," Branch said. "Is he gone or do you still see him?"

He walked away.

She got fired anyway. But the kids kept meeting. Band room. Teaching each other to double-see.

"See them as gone but here. Dead but talking. Nowhere but everywhere. Don't pick one. Hold both."

Marcus found Branch months later. Different school. Same cardigan.

"Still teaching the double-seeing?"

"What else would I teach? Math?"

"Is it real?"

"Real as that sandwich you're holding. Real as your dad being dead and eating lunch. Real as anything else we tell ourselves to get through Tuesday."

He offered her half the sandwich. Three days old.

They ate in silence. Tasted like grief and $3.99 and love with nowhere to go.

It was terrible. Which made it perfect. Or just Tuesday.

*Coda:*
The brain doesn't choose between opposing truths after loss. It holds both—they're gone, they're here, they're dead, they're making coffee at 3 AM. This isn't mystical. It's just Thursday in Baltimore, where substitute teachers make $13.47 an hour teaching kids that insanity might be the sanest response to an insane world. The test scores went up because broken minds might work better than whole ones. Nobody wants to study why.
