## Part I: The Question

I stand at Bdote at 4 AM, where the Minnesota and Mississippi rivers meet, because my Dakota grandmother's ashes were scattered here without ceremony, without permission, without me. I am documenting sacred thresholds—places where transformation becomes inevitable—for a dissertation that will never capture what I'm actually searching for: proof that some ground remembers everything, even what we try to forget.

My qualifications: Twenty-three years of inherited silence about being Dakota in a family that chose survival over identity. A Masters in Religious Studies focused on liminal spaces. Three months sleeping in my car at various sacred sites, recording what the land says when no one's listening. 

The paradox driving this: How do you honor ground you're not sure you have the right to stand on?

At Bdote, the rivers don't simply meet—they recognize each other, folding together like hands in prayer. The Mdewakanton Dakota call this the center of the earth, the place of genesis. The Army called it Fort Snelling, built a fort on top of creation itself. Both truths exist simultaneously, pressing against each other in the soil.

## Part II: Gethsemane Journal

*Jerusalem, three weeks earlier. Mount of Olives, midnight.*

The tour group left hours ago. I'm alone among olive trees older than Christianity, pressing my palms against bark that might have witnessed that night—the one every painting tries to capture, where divine met human in the ultimate threshold moment.

My phone buzzes: another message from my mother. *Why are you wasting your education on this? Come home.*

I don't tell her I'm tracing the geography of surrender, mapping places where people faced impossible choices and the ground held their weight anyway. I don't tell her about finding grandmother's journals after the funeral, filled with Dakota words she never spoke aloud, prayers to land she never returned to.

A security guard finds me, flashlight sweeping. "Garden's closed," he says in accented English.

"I'm researching liminal spaces," I explain.

He laughs, not unkind. "Lady, everything in Jerusalem is liminal. We build on top of our own bones here."

He lets me stay another hour. Tells me his grandfather was Palestinian, used to tend these trees before 1948. "Land remembers," he says. "Even when we're told to forget."

## Part III: The Elder

*Bdote, sunrise. Marcus Greengrass, Dakota elder, arrives as promised.*

"You're Elizabeth's granddaughter," he says. Not a question.

I nod, though I never knew her Dakota name.

Marcus doesn't lecture about cultural appropriation or bloodlines. Instead, he kneels, places tobacco on the ground, speaks in Dakota—words I recognize from grandmother's journals but can't translate.

"Your grandmother left," he finally says. "1952. Married that Norwegian farmer, never came back. Some called it betrayal. I called it survival."

He shows me photos on his phone—not ancient history but last week's water ceremony, young Dakota people standing where we stand, reclaiming what was never truly lost.

"Garden of Gethsemane," I say. "Do you know it?"

"Christian story. Man facing death, asking for another way."

"It's about accepting transformation," I say. "Even when it means everything changes."

Marcus nods toward the rivers. "Dakota have a word—*bdote*. Means where two waters meet. But also means throat, like the earth speaking. Your grandmother knew this word. Wrote it over and over in those journals you found."

He knows about the journals. Of course he does.

## Part IV: Wild Mind Fragment

*3 AM, convenience store parking lot, somewhere between sacred sites.*

My phone notes, typed while I can't sleep:

*One thing you can always rely on is that the times are always changing—grandmother's favorite saying, I now realize was code for: we had to change to survive*

*Root science: DNA test shows 47% Dakota, 53% Norwegian—math of erasure*

*Music for benefit of all wild senses: pow wow videos on YouTube at 3 AM, volume low so no one hears me trying to learn*

*Centuries to our story but I only know 70 years*

*Shop for toys till you drop—Mom's December strategy, buying American Girl dolls instead of answering questions about why we don't know our relatives*

*Branding culture: how to be Native enough? How to be anything enough?*

*Forgotten about ethics vs. forgotten about relatives*

I delete it all. Start over. Delete again. The wild mind won't settle into academic prose, won't behave for the dissertation committee. It wants to scream: *The garden waits but I don't know what for.*

## Part V: The Research Scene

*University library, Indigenous Studies section. Dr. Sarah Crow Dog pulls book after book.*

"You're writing about Gethsemane and Bdote together?" She raises an eyebrow. "Bold. Foolish maybe, but bold."

She shows me maps—Fort Snelling, 1820, built directly on top of Bdote. Shows me paintings—Jesus in Gethsemane, always depicted in European gardens, never Middle Eastern terrain.

"Sacred sites get overwritten," she says. "New story on top of old story. But land doesn't forget. It just waits."

She pulls up archaeological reports. Bdote: 10,000 years of continuous indigenous presence. Gethsemane: Crusader churches built over Byzantine churches built over first-century Jewish graves.

"Your grandmother," she says suddenly. "Elizabeth Greengrass. She was my teacher's cousin."

The world shrinks.

"She left to save her children from boarding schools. Married white, spoke English only. Let everyone think she was Latina. Survival strategy of the 1950s."

She hands me a photo I've never seen: grandmother as a young woman, standing at Bdote, wearing traditional dress I never saw her wear again.

## Part VI: Night Watch

*Gethsemane, second visit. 2 AM.*

I've bribed the security guard—same one from before—with good coffee and American cigarettes. He lets me sit among the trees while he makes his rounds.

"You're looking for something specific," he observes.

"I'm looking for what happens in the waiting. That moment between prayer and acceptance."

He points to a gnarled olive tree, trunk split but still growing. "This one's maybe two thousand years old. If Jesus prayed here, this tree heard it. But tree doesn't tell stories. Just keeps growing."

I think about Bdote's cottonwoods, how they sound like water when wind moves through them. How grandmother wrote: *Trees remember what we make ourselves forget.*

"My family fled in 1948," the guard says suddenly. "From a village near here. We still have the key to our house. House is gone, but we have the key. You understand?"

I do. I have grandmother's journals but not her language. Her recipes but not her stories. Her face in the mirror but not her name.

## Part VII: The Convergence

*Bdote, dawn. I've been here all night, recording.*

The rivers sound different in darkness—louder, more insistent. I play back my recordings: water, wind, occasional car from the distant highway. But underneath, something else. A rhythm like breathing, like the earth's pulse.

Marcus arrives with coffee, finds me surrounded by notebooks.

"Figured you'd still be here," he says. "Gethsemane girl trying to understand Dakota ground."

"They're connected," I insist. "Places where transformation becomes inevitable. Where the only choice is surrender or breaking."

"Your grandmother broke," he says gently. "But breaking isn't always failure. Sometimes it's how light gets in."

He teaches me a Dakota word: *wanikiya*—to live in spite of. To save one's life. What grandmother did.

"She saved you," he says. "Saved you from forced assimilation but also from complete disconnection. You're here, aren't you? Asking questions. That's not nothing."

## Part VIII: The Dissertation Committee

*University conference room. Three professors, one confused graduate student.*

"You're comparing Gethsemane to Bdote?" Dr. Harrison frowns. "The theological implications alone—"

"I'm not comparing," I interrupt. "I'm documenting how sacred sites hold human transformation. How ground becomes witness."

Dr. Crow Dog supports me: "It's innovative. Risky, but innovative."

Dr. Martinez, the outside reader, surprises everyone: "I see it. Colonial overlay of sacred indigenous sites parallels Christianity's relationship to Jerusalem's older histories. The personal thread—your grandmother's story—that's what makes it work."

They approve my research, conditionally. I need more concrete evidence, less meditation.

But meditation is evidence. The garden waiting *is* the story.

## Part IX: What Grandmother Knew

*Her journal, final entry, 1987:*

*The garden waits. I learned this from the missionaries who tried to save us—their Jesus in his garden, sweating blood, accepting transformation. But I already knew about gardens waiting. Bdote waits. Has been waiting since I left. Will wait long after I'm gone.*

*I chose survival over ceremony. Chose my children's safety over my own belonging. This too is sacred. This too is threshold.*

*Tell my granddaughters: The land remembers even what we forget. The rivers still meet. The garden still waits. And waiting is not passive—it's the active choice to remain, to endure, to hold space for return.*

*Even if return takes generations.*

## Part X: Return to Bdote

*Six months later. Water ceremony organized by Marcus and the youth group.*

I stand with thirty others—some full-blood, some like me, fractional, finding our way back. We offer tobacco to the rivers. Someone drums. Someone sings a song that sounds like coming home.

A young woman next to me whispers, "First time?"

"My grandmother was Elizabeth Greengrass," I say.

"Oh!" Recognition floods her face. "My grandfather talks about her. Says she was brilliant. Says leaving was the bravest thing she did."

The rivers meet and meet and meet—endless convergence, endless recognition. I think of Jesus in his garden, sweating blood but not running. Of grandmother in her Norwegian farmhouse, writing Dakota words in secret. Of the Palestinian guard at Gethsemane, holding his grandfather's key to a house that no longer exists.

All of us in our gardens, waiting. All of us at our thresholds, choosing.

## Part XI: The Final Recording

*Bdote, 4 AM, one year after I started.*

I set up my recording equipment one last time. The rivers sound exactly the same—eternal meeting, eternal recognition. But I hear differently now.

The sound isn't just water. It's every prayer spoken here. Every ceremony. Every loss. Every return. The Fort Snelling cannon fire and the Dakota songs beneath it. My grandmother's footsteps leaving and mine arriving.

A blue heron lands nearby—unlikely, Marcus would say, this time of year. It stands in the shallow water where the rivers meet, perfectly still, waiting.

I think of the olive trees in Gethsemane, holding two thousand years of prayers. Of this heron, holding the morning. Of grandmother's journals, holding words she couldn't speak aloud.

The dissertation is finished. The committee calls it "groundbreaking scholarship on sacred liminal spaces." But what I've really written is simpler: a map of return for those who got lost surviving.

## Part XII: What the Garden Knows

*Final dissertation defense. I break protocol, begin with a prayer.*

"The garden waits," I say. "Not for us to be worthy, but for us to arrive. However broken. However many generations it takes."

I present my research: archaeological data, theological analysis, ethnographic interviews. But also: recordings of rivers at dawn, photographs of olive trees older than empires, grandmother's journals translated by Dakota speakers who wept reading her words.

Dr. Harrison challenges: "You're suggesting landscape holds memory? That's not scientifically provable."

"I'm documenting what exists beyond proof," I respond. "Every sacred site I studied—Gethsemane, Bdote, the Ganges ghats, Uluru—they all share this quality of active waiting. Of holding space for human transformation."

The Palestinian guard's words echo: *Land remembers, even when we're told to forget.*

Marcus's teaching returns: *Bdote means throat—the earth speaking.*

Grandmother's journal whispers: *Even if return takes generations.*

They grant my PhD. But more importantly, Marcus invites me to the next water ceremony as a relative, not a researcher.

## Epilogue: The Garden Speaks

*Bdote, sunrise, present day.*

I bring my daughter here—she's three, asks endless questions. "Why is the water talking?" she wants to know.

"It's meeting another water," I explain. "They're saying hello."

She accepts this, throws leaves into the current, watches them swirl at the confluence.

Marcus is now an elder elder, moves slower but still comes for ceremonies. "Your grandmother would be proud," he says. "You found your way back."

"She never really left," I realize. "She just sent her return forward through time."

My daughter starts singing—nonsense words, but the rhythm is Dakota, learned from the YouTube videos I play while cooking. She doesn't know she's singing her great-great-grandmother's language. Doesn't know she's completing a circle.

The garden waits. Has always waited. Will always wait.

Not passively, but with the active presence of a place that knows: every leaving contains its return, every breaking its healing, every silence its eventual song.

The rivers meet. The child sings. The elder smiles.

And somewhere, in whatever garden she now tends, grandmother's waiting is finally over.

*Author's Note: Bdote remains a sacred site for the Dakota people, accessible to all who approach with respect. The Garden of Gethsemane still stands in Jerusalem, its ancient olive trees bearing witness to prayers in every language. Both places remind us that transformation doesn't happen in isolation—it happens where earth meets heaven, where rivers converge, where gardens wait for us to arrive at our own readiness. This essay is dedicated to all who are finding their way back to places their families had to leave to survive.*
