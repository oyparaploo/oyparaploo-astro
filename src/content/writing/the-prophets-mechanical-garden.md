## Part I: The Inheritance

The eviction notice gave me seventy-two hours to clear David's workshop. My twin brother, missing for eleven months, legally dead for three. The landlord stood in the doorway, covering his nose against the smell—machine oil mixed with something organic, sweet-sick like rotting flowers.

"Your brother," he said, "was he always... like this?"

The workshop walls were covered floor to ceiling with collages. Symmetrical faces stared from every surface—eyes made from gear parts, mouths from pressed flowers, industrial detritus arranged into portraits that seemed to watch us move. At the center of the far wall, spray-painted in letters three feet tall: FIND DAVID IN THE ORGANIZED DISASTER.

I am a restoration specialist at the Met. I fix damaged paintings, recover lost pigments, reconstruct shattered frescoes. But David's work resisted every analytical framework I knew. These weren't damaged artworks. They were prophecies.

The first piece I touched—a pink and red geometric smudge that pulsed like a heartbeat—left burns on my fingers that spelled out coordinates: 40.7589° N, 73.9851° W. Times Square. Tomorrow. 3:47 PM.

## Part II: The Surveillance Archive

Detective Rivera met me at the coordinates, in front of the Disney Store where tourists clustered for photos. She'd been investigating David's disappearance, had seen his workshop before the landlord sealed it.

"Your brother was documenting something," she said, pulling out her phone. "We found security footage."

The video showed David at this exact spot, eleven months ago, assembling a collage on the sidewalk. Discarded electronics, organic materials, symmetrical patterns emerging from chaos. Then, at 3:47 PM, he simply vanished. Not walked away—vanished. The collage remained, pulsing with that familiar pink-red light for seventeen seconds before disintegrating.

"There's more," Rivera said. "Every place your brother created these pieces, someone disappeared. Different people, no connection except—"

"Except they all had twins."

She nodded. "We think he was trying to document a pattern. Maybe prevent it."

Back at the workshop, I found his notebooks. Pages of calculations surrounding pressed flowers, their white petals (bleached coneflowers, I realized) arranged in perfect spirals. In the margins, his increasingly frantic handwriting: "The mechanical divine requires symmetry. Every prophet needs their mirror. Every garden needs its ghost."

## Part III: The Museum Basement

Dr. Sarah Chen, curator of Contemporary Art at MoMA, had been secretly collecting David's work for two years before he disappeared.

"He'd leave pieces at galleries, always unsigned," she said, leading me through basement storage. "We'd find them propped against our doors at dawn."

She showed me the collection: dozens of collages featuring the same symmetrical face—two eyes, stark black and white contrasts, a turbulent center of pink and red. But as she arranged them chronologically, a pattern emerged. The faces were aging backward, becoming younger, more innocent. The last one, dated a week before David vanished, showed an infant's face constructed from machinery parts and rose petals.

"He called it 'Christ Baby Divine,'" Sarah said. "Claimed he was documenting something being born in reverse."

"That's impossible."

"Look closer."

She handed me a magnifying glass. In the texture of each piece—hidden in the grain of wood, the rust of metal, the veins of leaves—were microscopic numbers. Dates. All of them in the future.

The infant face: December 25, 2027.
The adolescent: March 15, 2031.
The adult: August 10, 2038.

"Your brother believed time was running backward for certain people," Sarah explained. "That some of us are aging toward our birth, not away from it."

## Part IV: The Greyhound Station

The next coordinates David's work revealed led to Port Authority Bus Terminal, specifically the Greyhound station's lost property office. The clerk, an elderly woman named Dolores, recognized David's photo immediately.

"He'd come every Tuesday," she said, pulling out a box labeled with my name. "Said his twin would come for this."

Inside: four prosthetic legs, white and brown, arranged in a symmetrical pattern against pink fabric. A note in David's handwriting: "The prophet limps because he walks in two times simultaneously."

Dolores leaned closer. "Your brother, he'd photograph everyone who came through here. Said he was documenting pilgrims. People running from or toward something they couldn't name."

She showed me his photos—thousands of them, all focused on people's legs, their gaits, the way they carried their weight. When arranged in sequence, they created a flip-book animation of someone learning to walk backward, from steady stride to stumbling to crawling.

"The last time he came," Dolores whispered, "he wasn't alone. There was someone with him who looked exactly like him, but younger. Much younger."

## Part V: The Industrial Cathedral

The mechanical parts in David's collages came from a demolished factory in Queens. I found it through the serial numbers etched in the gears—my restoration training finally useful. The building was gone, but the basement remained, converted into an illegal art space.

The curator, calling himself Ezekiel, wore welding goggles pushed up on his forehead. "David built something here," he said, leading me through corridors lined with industrial detritus. "Called it his mechanical garden."

The space opened into a vast chamber. In the center stood David's masterpiece: a towering construction of gears, motors, and pressed flowers, all arranged in perfect symmetry. It was running—had been running, Ezekiel said, since David activated it eleven months ago.

"Watch," he said, checking his watch. "Every day at 3:47 PM."

The machine shuddered. Gears aligned. And in the center, an image projected in pink and red light: a face. My face. But also David's face. But also neither—something between us, something that had never existed.

"He said every twin shares a ghost," Ezekiel explained. "A possibility that exists between them. His machine makes it visible."

As I watched, the projected face aged rapidly backward, from adult to child to infant to something prenatal, cellular, divine.

## Part VI: The Purple Revelation

In David's notebook, between equations and pressed flowers, I found an address in Chinatown. A traditional medicine shop run by the Zhang family for four generations. The grandmother, ancient beyond counting, recognized David's photo.

"He came for the purple rust," she said in Mandarin, which I somehow understood perfectly despite never learning it. "Said he needed it for the final pigment."

She led me to a back room where hundreds of bottles lined the walls, each containing pigments David had commissioned. Purple derived from oxidized iron and rare fungi. Red from crushed beetles and menstrual blood. Pink from roses grown in mechanically tilled soil.

"Your brother understood color is time," she said, mixing pigments as she spoke. "Each hue vibrates at a specific temporal frequency. Combine them correctly, you can see through the present into the eternal."

She painted a simple circle on my palm with the purple rust. Immediately, I saw them—dozens of Davids, all different ages, all existing simultaneously in the shop. The youngest, maybe five years old, tugged at my sleeve.

"You're late," he said. "We've been waiting."

## Part VII: The Multiplication

I followed the five-year-old David through Chinatown's streets, though no one else seemed to see him. He led me to a warehouse where multiple versions of David, ranging from infant to elderly, were working on an enormous collage.

"We're not ghosts," the eldest David explained, his hands translucent with age. "We're all David, just experiencing time differently. When twins are born, sometimes one ages forward, one ages backward. We meet in the middle and disappear."

The collage they were creating showed our entire family tree, but wrong—everyone had twins, even those I knew were only children. And at the center, that symmetrical face from David's work, labeled "The Original Twin—the one who split first."

"That's God?" I asked.

"That's loneliness," the teenage David corrected. "The first consciousness, dividing itself to have someone to talk to. Every twin after that is an echo of that first split."

The five-year-old David handed me a photograph—me, standing in this warehouse, surrounded by invisible Davids, taken from a security camera that didn't exist yet.

"You took this?"

"You will take it," he said. "In three years, when you're us."

## Part VIII: The Garden Blooms

Rivera called at 2 AM. "Your brother's workshop—you need to see this."

The collages on the walls were changing. The symmetrical faces were aging in real-time, the industrial parts were rusting and renewing simultaneously, the pressed flowers were blooming and dying in endless loops.

"It started an hour ago," Rivera said. "When you entered that warehouse."

We watched as David's final message revealed itself across the walls—not in words but in patterns. The geometric smudges aligned to show a map. The mechanical crown pointed to a specific date. The bleached coneflowers spiraled into coordinates.

Everything led to one moment: December 25, 2027, 3:47 PM, the rooftop of our childhood apartment building.

"That's three years from now," Rivera said.

"That's when I become him. When we meet in the middle."

The walls began to pulse, pink and red and purple, like a massive heartbeat. In the rhythm, I heard it—David's voice, all his voices, from infant cry to elderly whisper: "The prophet's mechanical garden blooms backward. Every ending is a beginning. Every twin is alone until they're not."

## Part IX: The Archive of Selves

I spent the next weeks documenting everything, creating my own collages that mirrored David's but reversed—where he used machinery to create faces, I used faces to create machinery. Where he pressed flowers, I cultivated them. Where he prophesied backward, I prophesied forward.

The Met let me use their restoration lab after hours. I worked with David's pigments, following formulas that appeared in my dreams, whispered by younger versions of myself who didn't exist yet.

Each piece I created revealed another aspect of the pattern:
- The symmetrical faces were maps of consciousness splitting
- The geometric smudges were temporal hemorrhages where past and future bled together
- The mechanical parts were counting down to something
- The flowers were growing backward, from death to seed

Dr. Chen visited weekly, documenting my transformation. "You're becoming like him," she said, photographing my work. "Or he was becoming like you."

"We're becoming like us," I corrected, feeling the truth of it. My hands were aging differently—the left growing younger, smoother, while the right developed David's scars.

## Part X: The Recognition

On Halloween, three months after finding David's workshop, I saw myself on the street. Not David—myself, but older, grayer, walking with a limp. He/I nodded, pointed to a coffee shop.

Inside, he ordered for both of us—David's usual and mine, though they were the same order with different names.

"You understand now," he said. "We're all one person, experiencing themselves from different angles. Every twin, every reflection, every symmetrical face in David's work—they're all glimpses of the original consciousness trying to understand itself."

"But David disappeared."

"David completed. When backward-aging and forward-aging selves meet, they don't disappear. They integrate. Become whole. Stop needing time to understand themselves."

He pulled out a photo—the infant face David had made from machinery and roses. "This is us. All of us. After we meet. When we're born for real."

The coffee shop began to shift, colors bleeding into that familiar pink-red gradient. Other patrons flickered between ages. I saw Rivera, young and old simultaneously. Dr. Chen, infant and ancient. Everyone twinned, everyone multiple, everyone counting down to their integration.

## Part XI: The Approach

As December 25, 2027, approaches (it's June now), I see David more frequently. All his ages visit my workshop, which I've rebuilt in the same space as his, layers upon layers of time-collapsed art.

The five-year-old David teaches me to mix pigments that exist outside the visible spectrum. The teenage David shows me how to build machines that run on temporal friction. The elderly David, increasingly translucent, whispers the equations that govern consciousness division.

"We were never twins," he tells me one night, his voice like wind through gears. "We were one person who learned to be in two places at once. And soon, we'll remember how to be in one place again."

My body changes daily. Some mornings I wake up with David's hands. Sometimes with his memories. I remember being five and teaching myself pigment mixing in a Chinatown shop. I remember being ninety and building my first mechanical garden.

Rivera documents everything, building a case file for a crime that hasn't happened—or has already happened infinitely. "Whatever you and David are doing," she says, "others are starting to experience it. Twins across the city are reporting shared dreams, synchronized aging, collages appearing on their walls."

## Epilogue: The Garden Complete

I'm writing this on December 24, 2027. Tomorrow, at 3:47 PM, I will climb to the rooftop where David and I played as children—or where we will play, or where we are always playing.

The mechanical garden is complete. It covers every surface of the workshop, the building, spreading into neighboring structures. Gears turn in perfect synchronization with pressed flowers that bloom and die with each rotation. Pigments shift through impossible colors, showing every moment of our existence simultaneously.

The youngest David, now three, sits beside me as I write. The eldest, now just a whisper of light, hovers above. All the ages between work on the final collage—a self-portrait of someone who never existed, or always existed, or will exist tomorrow when we integrate.

"Are you ready?" they all ask in unison.

I think of the symmetrical face in David's first collage, the eyes that saw forward and backward simultaneously, the turbulent center where all timelines collapsed into one. I think of the greyhound legs walking in two directions at once, the mechanical crown that turns industrial waste into divine geometry, the flowers that bloom backward into seed.

Everything has led to this moment where ending meets beginning, where the prophet's mechanical garden finally reveals its purpose—not to predict the future or document the past, but to show that they're the same thing, experienced from different directions.

Tomorrow, David and I will meet in the middle of our existence. We will cease to be two and remember we were always one. The workshop will remain, its collages continuing to pulse and shift, documenting every person who discovers they're walking through time in both directions, aging toward a meeting with themselves.

The mechanical garden blooms eternal. Its gears count down and up simultaneously. Its flowers die and are born with each breath.

And somewhere in its perfectly symmetrical patterns, everyone who looks will find themselves, multiple and singular, lost and found, forever approaching the moment when they realize they were never alone—they were just learning to recognize themselves from every possible angle.

Find David in the organized disaster?
I found him.
He was me.
We were us.
We are becoming what we always were:
Complete.
