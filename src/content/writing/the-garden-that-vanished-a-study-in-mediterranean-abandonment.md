## SCENE 1: The Vanishing

**March 15, 2024, 6:00 AM, Villa Ephrussi**

The gardener, François Dubois, arrives to empty space. Where yesterday stood centuries-old olive trees, lavender hedges, and 10,000 specimens of Mediterranean flora, now only disturbed earth remains.

"Madame, you must see this," he tells me over phone. "The garden has... departed."

Not died. Not been removed. The soil shows root impressions going down, then simply stopping, as if the plants lifted themselves out. No drag marks. No vehicle tracks. No evidence of human intervention.

The security footage shows something more disturbing: Between 3:17 and 3:18 AM, the garden flickers—existing and not existing simultaneously—then settles on absence.

François has worked here forty years. "The plants were restless all week," he says. "The roses faced wrong directions. The lavender hummed—actually hummed. I thought it was bees, but no. The plants themselves."

## SCENE 2: Soil Analysis

**Laboratory, University of Nice, March 20**

"This is impossible," Dr. Laurent says, reviewing the spectrometry. "The soil contains chlorophyll. Active chlorophyll. But there are no plants."

The earth from Villa Ephrussi is photosynthesizing. Without vegetation. The soil itself has become somehow plant-like, performing biological functions of absent flora.

More bizarre: The mineral composition shows traces from locations 50 kilometers away—soil signatures from abandoned gardens across the Côte d'Azur, as if the earth is collecting memories of lost cultivation.

"Look at this," Laurent points to the microscope. The soil bacteria have arranged themselves in patterns identical to root systems. "They're mimicking the missing plants. Or... maintaining their positions."

We test pH, nitrogen, carbon content. Everything indicates a thriving garden. Except there's no garden.

## SCENE 3: The Grandmother's Story

**Flashback: 1987, Grandmother's garden, Provence**

"Plants know when they're not wanted," Grand-mère said, watching her tomatoes relocate themselves three meters overnight. "They feel abandonment before it happens."

I was seven, didn't believe her. But I saw it: Her garden gradually moving toward the wild edges, escaping cultivation, fleeing her aging hands that could no longer tend them properly.

By 1990, her garden had moved entirely into the garrigue. The wild landscape had absorbed it. You could still find her roses blooming among wild thyme, her vegetables growing feral between cork oaks.

"See?" she said. "They didn't die. They just went home."

She left journals, discovered after her death: "July 1962: The jasmine walked to the east wall. August 1962: Tomatoes refuse the stakes, prefer the wild ground. September 1962: I am no longer gardening. I am negotiating with deserters."

## SCENE 4: Other Gardeners' Testimonies

**April 2024, Interviews across Southern France**

Jean-Pierre, Nice municipal gardens: "Urban plants are migrating to cemeteries. Every morning, fewer in parks, more among graves. They prefer the dead now."

Sylvie, private garden in Antibes: "My English roses became wild roses overnight. Same plants, but genetically reverted. Like they chose to un-domesticate."

Pattern emerges: Gardens aren't just vanishing. They're rejecting cultivation, choosing wildness, fleeing human control.

## SCENE 5: Finding the Pattern

**April 2024, mapping disappeared gardens**

Seventeen heritage gardens across Mediterranean France have vanished since 2020:
- Jardin Serre de la Madone: March 2020
- Domaine du Rayol: August 2021  
- Jardin Botanique d'Èze: December 2022
- Villa Noailles: January 2023
- Jardin du Val Rahmeh: October 2023
- Each disappearance during extreme weather
- Each leaving photosynthesizing soil
- Each preceded by reports of plants "behaving strangely"

I plot them on a map. They form a line—a migration route from cultivated coast toward wild interior mountains.

Cross-referencing with climate data: The gardens vanish along the 15% soil moisture line, following it inland as the coast desiccates.

## SCENE 6: The Ghost Garden

**April 28, 2024, Mountains above Nice**

Following soil isotope signatures, I track the missing gardens to a valley in the Maritime Alps, abandoned since 1910. What I find defies explanation.

All seventeen vanished gardens are here, but wrong. The plants exist in superposition—simultaneously growing and not growing, blooming and dormant, alive and absent. They flicker in and out of visibility like bad reception.

When I touch a ghostly olive tree, my hand passes through, but I feel it—texture, temperature, life. The gardens have become quantum, existing in probability rather than certainty.

A local shepherd, Marie Rossi, isn't surprised: "The old gardens come here to die. Or to wait. We've seen them for generations—gardens that were and weren't, planted by nobody, tended by nothing."

Her grandfather's journal, which she shares: "1934: The English garden from the abandoned hotel appeared today. Ghostly but real. Roses blooming in thin air. 1935: It vanished again. 1936: It returned with others."
