## Part One: Mumbai

The vendor at Crawford Market kept wiping his hands on his pants, even though they weren't dirty. Maya knew the feeling—sometimes your body just needs something to do while your mind catches up.

"LUCKY LAUGHER," she read from her phone screen, where she'd been collecting these phrases for three years. The screen was greasy with Mumbai humidity, that particular wetness that makes everything feel slightly alive. "HUNGRY FLIES."

He looked at her the way people look at modern art they don't understand but suspect might be expensive. "For dogs?"

"For humans," Maya said. Then, because that sounded wrong: "For me."

The engraving machine made a sound like a dentist drill falling in love. DUST BUZZ appeared in the aluminum, permanent as regret, light as hope.

Her brother Arjun had worn the first one. WAILING LOVE, stamped on military-grade metal, which their mother treated like evidence of his general failure to be serious about life. She'd grab at it during family dinners, usually right when he was reaching for the pickle.

"At least tell me what it means," she'd said once, exasperated.

"It means I saw it on a wall and it made me laugh," he'd said, which was Arjun's way of saying it meant everything.

After the accident—a Tuesday, 3:47 PM, the kind of specific time that brands itself into your brain—Maya started collecting them. Not consciously at first. She'd just see these word combinations spraypainted or scratched into Mumbai's surfaces and photograph them. EGGS COCK on a construction barrier. ROACH CRAWL on a bus stop. STREET HAIKU on, ironically, a government building. SOFT CONCRETE carved into a train station bench.

Now she wore eight tags. They made a noise like she was smuggling spoons.

"You're going to set off metal detectors," Priya said that evening. They were eating takeaway Chinese from boxes, sitting on their balcony that was technically too small for two people but they made it work. Below them, Mumbai did its Mumbai thing—honking, selling, living, arguing, all at once.

"Good," Maya said. "I want to be detectible."

Priya chopsticked a piece of manchurian and studied the newest tag. "TENDER CHAOS. That's very... us before coffee."

Maya snorted. Actually snorted, which made Priya laugh, which made the old woman watering plants on the opposite balcony look over and smile, which made the whole evening feel like it was worth saving.

## Part Two: Detroit

The Detroit pawn shop smelled like WD-40 and broken promises. Earl, the owner, had apparently decided sometime in 1987 that mustaches were forever, and who was Maya to argue?

"My friend collects these," she told him, holding out the tags. This was true-ish. Lily was sixty-eight, had retired from Ford's River Rouge plant with hands that could diagnose an engine by touch, and now made sculptures from car parts in her garage. They'd met when Maya knocked on the wrong door looking for an art residency that had closed two years ago.

Earl held HUNGRY FLIES between fingers that looked like they'd fixed every engine in Michigan at least twice. Metal shavings from his engraving machine scattered across the counter like silver dandruff. Maya pressed her thumb into them, feeling the tiny bites of metal embedding in her skin. "This supposed to mean something?"

"Everything means something," Lily said, appearing with a carburetor she'd found in the back. "Or nothing does. Either way, we're all just making it up."

Earl laughed. Not a polite laugh, but the kind that comes from your belly when someone says exactly the thing you've been thinking for forty years but couldn't put into words.

His engraving machine was older than Maya's entire life plan. It made sounds that belonged in a museum of extinct mechanical birds. While he worked, he told them about Dolores, his wife, who'd collected salt and pepper shakers.

"Four hundred pairs," he said, guiding the metal through the machine. "I asked her once why she needed so many. She said, 'Earl, the question is why doesn't everyone?'"

He made an extra tag without asking: STILL HERE. Handed it to Maya with a shrug that wasn't really a shrug.

"After Dolores died, I started saying it every morning. Like, checking. Still here? Yep, still here. Becomes a habit."

In Lily's garage that night—which still had oil stains shaped like continents on the concrete floor—they arranged the tags on a workbench scarred by forty years of problem-solving.

"We should hide these around the city," Lily said suddenly. "Like easter eggs, but for adults who've given up on easter."

Maya picked up Arjun's tag. WAILING LOVE. She'd kept it in a tin that once held Danish butter cookies, the kind every Indian family repurposes for sewing supplies or secrets. The metal was worn smooth where his thumb used to worry it during exams.

"He would have liked you," she told Lily.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. He collected terrible jokes. Like, aggressively terrible. He had a notebook full of them."

She clipped his tag onto her chain. It settled against the others with a small sound that wasn't quite music but wasn't quite noise either. Something in between. Like most true things.

Outside, Detroit was doing that thing where abandoned buildings look beautiful at sunset, which shouldn't work but does. Somewhere in Mumbai, it was already tomorrow, and Priya was probably having her morning tea, wearing the TENDER CHAOS tag Maya had left on her pillow.

"Hey," Lily said, looking at Maya's collection of tags. "You know what this is? It's a poem. A really loud, clanky poem."

Maya touched the tags, feeling their edges, their weight, their terrible beautiful names. LUCKY LAUGHER. HUNGRY FLIES. DUST BUZZ. ROACH CRAWL. EGGS COCK. STREET HAIKU. TENDER CHAOS. SOFT CONCRETE. STILL HERE. WAILING LOVE.

Each one a little life raft. Each one a small fist raised against the forgetting.

"Yeah," she said to Lily, to Earl's empty shop, to Arjun's ghost, to the city outside that kept insisting on existing despite everything. "A poem."

The tags sang their tinny song against her chest.
