## Stories I Should Have Written Down Sooner

---

I knew Marina for thirty-seven years, though knew isn't quite right. You don't know someone like Marina—you witness them, you orbit them, you collect pieces of them like beach glass and try to make sense of the whole from the fragments.

She kept finches. That's what I remember first, before everything else. Four of them in a Victorian cage she'd rescued from an estate sale, and she'd whistle their language back to them every morning while making Turkish coffee in that ridiculous copper pot. The birds would tilt their heads, considering her attempts, then correct her gently. She'd laugh and try again. This went on for years.

---

## The Workshop Years

Her father had been a carpenter—the real kind, who could tell you which tree the wood came from by its smell. Marina inherited his workshop but not his patience. Where he'd been methodical, she was explosive. She'd attack a piece of oak like it had insulted her mother, then suddenly go gentle, running her fingers along the grain like reading braille.

"Wood remembers everything," she told me once, showing me the rings in a cross-section of pine. "This thin ring here? Drought year. This wide one? Someone loved the tree that summer. Trees grow differently when they're loved."

I thought she was being metaphorical. Marina was never metaphorical.

In those early years—we were both twenty-something, both convinced we'd invented rebellion—she made furniture for people who couldn't afford it. Not charity, she insisted. Trade. She'd build someone a table in exchange for three months of piano lessons, a bookshelf for Spanish tutoring, a bed frame for someone teaching her to can preserves. Her apartment filled with skills instead of money.

---

## When She Caught Fire

Then came what I call her burning years. She discovered politics the way some people discover religion—suddenly and completely. Started with the school board trying to ban some books. Marina showed up to the meeting with a tower of banned books she'd built into a functional lamp. Plugged it in right there in the middle of her speech. "See?" she said as it illuminated the room. "Dangerous books giving light. Imagine that."

She ran for city council and lost spectacularly. Got eleven votes, including mine and her mother's. Didn't matter. She kept showing up, kept speaking during public comment periods, kept making everyone uncomfortable with her questions about where exactly the money went and why the mayor's brother got all those contracts.

The newspaper called her "difficult." Men called her worse things. She had their words printed on business cards: "Marina Volkov: Difficult, Hysterical, and Unreasonably Loud." Handed them out at farmer's markets.

But here's what they didn't see—the nights after the confrontations when she'd sit in her workshop, hands shaking, sanding the same piece of wood over and over until it was nothing but dust. She wasn't fearless. She was terrified and did it anyway.

---

## The Accident

Nobody talks about this part, but I was there, so I'll tell it.

Highway 49, the stretch where it curves like a question mark. She'd been at a demonstration at the processing plant—something about groundwater contamination. Driving home, exhausted, she hit a deer. Or the deer hit her. These things are mutual.

The car flipped. When I found her (following an hour behind because someone had to document the protest), she was sitting on the embankment, covered in glass diamonds, holding the deer's head in her lap. It was still alive, barely. She was singing to it in Russian—lullabies her grandmother had taught her. The deer died just as the ambulance arrived. Marina didn't cry until three days later, and then she cried for six hours straight.

After that, she was different. Not broken, but rearranged. Like someone had taken her apart and put her back together with a few pieces in new places.

---

## The Toad Summer

This is embarrassing to admit, but there was a summer when Marina got... well, ugly isn't the right word. Reduced, maybe. She stopped washing her hair, lived on saltines and cherry tomatoes, spoke to no one for weeks at a time. Her mother called it depression. Marina called it "research."

She was studying decay. Literally. Had specimens all over her workshop—leaves in various stages of decomposition, fruit growing elaborate molds, a bird skeleton she was watching return to dust. "Everything falls apart," she said when I finally forced my way in with groceries. "But look—" she showed me time-lapse photos she'd been taking, "—it falls apart into other things. Useful things. Beautiful things, if you adjust your definition of beautiful."

That was the summer she met Thomas. He was living under the overpass, had been since the hurricane took his house and his mind went sideways from the trauma. Marina didn't try to fix him. She just sat with him, two broken people comparing notes on falling apart. They'd share these elaborate meals she'd make from dumpster diving—she called it "rescue cuisine." 

He taught her something about endurance, I think. How to be reduced to almost nothing and still be something.

---

## The Years of Plenty

Then, like a switch flipped, Marina expanded. Started teaching woodworking to kids from the housing projects. Married a storm chaser named David who drew weather maps like other people write poetry. Had three kids in four years—bang, bang, bang—like she was racing against time.

This is when she got big. Not fat—dimensional. Like she was taking up all the space she'd been denied before. She'd nurse the baby while running a planning meeting, her older kids playing under the table. She painted her house seven different colors because "children need to know the world isn't monotone." 

Her workshop became a commune of sorts. There was always someone there—teenagers learning to joint wood, elderly men who just wanted to smell sawdust and tell stories, women who needed to hit something with a hammer and have it be productive rather than destructive.

"I'm growing backwards," she told me, covered in paint and baby spit. "Getting younger as I get older. More possible instead of less."

---

## The Divorce

David left on a Tuesday. Said he loved her but couldn't compete with her causes, her workshop, her infinite capacity to care about everything except their marriage. Marina didn't fight it. Signed the papers in the workshop, sawdust in her hair, baby on her hip.

"Some things are meant to be temporary," she said, surprisingly calm. "We were a summer storm. Intense, necessary, brief."

But I saw her after, when she thought no one was looking. She'd stand in the workshop holding pieces of wood, not working them, just holding them like they might explain something. The kids would find her there at dawn, asleep standing up, still gripping a plank of cherry or maple.

---

## What Came After

The children grew like weeds, wild and sideways. Marina taught them to be unfaithful to her expectations—her exact words. "I'm your mother, not your map," she'd say. "Find your own way. I'll be here when you need supplies."

She dated occasionally—a photographer who lasted three months, a baker who made it six, a social worker who became a friend instead. "I'm not built for partnership," she concluded. "I'm built for adjacency. People beside me, not with me."

The workshop evolved. She started making coffins—beautiful ones from reclaimed wood. "Death furniture," she called it cheerfully. "Everyone needs a good box eventually." She'd carve stories into them, things the families told her about the departed. Turned grief into something you could touch.

---

## The Migration

At fifty-three, Marina developed something the doctors couldn't name. Not quite MS, not quite lupus, something that made her bones feel like glass some days, like steel others. She adapted, as she always did. Built herself mobility aids that looked like art—a cane that was also a measuring stick and a weapon, if needed. A walker that collapsed into a backpack.

She started traveling, but not the way people usually do. She'd go to disasters—floods, fires, tornadoes—and set up a temporary workshop. Teaching people to rebuild, but more than that. Teaching them that destroying and creating were part of the same motion.

"After my house burned," one woman told me, "Marina taught me to make a table from the charred beams. My whole life, I eat breakfast on my tragedy transformed into furniture."

---

## Last Things

Marina died building something. Of course she did. Heart attack in the workshop, chisel in hand, working on a cradle for her newest grandchild. Fell forward onto the piece, left a perfect imprint of her hand in the soft pine.

Her children kept the imprint. They run the workshop now, teaching what she taught but in their own languages. The eldest makes protest furniture. The middle one builds tiny houses for the unhoused. The youngest documents everything, says they're writing a book about transformation.

I still have the first thing Marina ever made for me—a box for keeping important things. It's ugly, joints all wrong, wood stained with what might be tears or coffee. Inside, she carved: "For Paula, who sees me when I cannot see myself."

---

## What I Learned

Marina taught me that we're all multiple people wearing the same name. That transformation isn't magic—it's labor. That you can be a different person at breakfast than you were at dinner and both are true.

She never said "go away" to any version of herself. She said "make room." And she did, expanding and contracting like lungs, like hearts, like the universe itself—constantly becoming while constantly being.

Sometimes I dream about her workshop. It's full of all the things she never got to build, all the people she never got to be. In the dream, she's there, sanding something impossible—a door that opens both ways, a chair that's also a boat, a table that remembers every meal ever eaten on it.

"Still working," she says without looking up.

"Still watching," I reply.

And we continue like that, maker and witness, until I wake up and remember she's gone. Except she's not. She's in every piece of reclaimed wood, every difficult woman at a microphone, every badly made beautiful thing.

That's the thing about carpenters' daughters—they know how to build things that outlast them.
