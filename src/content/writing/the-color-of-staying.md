The paint arrived on a Tuesday, three gallons of what the hardware store called "Marigold Supreme" but what Elena knew was closer to the color of her daughter's laugh before everything changed. She'd driven from her apartment in Oakland to the old house in Scranton, Pennsylvania, where her sister Margaret still lived, carrying the paint cans like precious cargo through two airports.

"You could have bought paint here," Margaret said, watching Elena pry open the first can in what used to be their mother's sunroom.

"Not this color," Elena said, though she couldn't explain why it mattered. How some yellows were merely yellow, while this one held light like a promise.

Margaret understood anyway. She'd been the one to find their mother that morning six months ago, still holding the jade plant she'd been watering.

The sisters worked in companionable silence, spreading the paint with wide trowels Elena had also brought from California. The texture was important - not smooth like a wall, but rippled, catching shadows and light differently depending on where you stood. Elena thought of the therapy sessions where she'd learned to shape clay, pressing her whole palm into the material until it held the heat of her hand.

"Remember the swing?" Margaret asked suddenly.

Of course Elena remembered. Their father had painted it this same golden shade every spring, long after they'd outgrown it. Even now, twenty years after he'd died, she could feel the chains warming under her grip, see the faint shimmer where their bodies had worn the paint thin.

By the third day, they'd covered most of the sunroom walls. The color transformed the space, made it feel like standing inside a paper lantern. Margaret's teenage daughter, Iris, home from school with a fabricated stomachache, wandered in and stopped.

"It's so... loud," she said, but not unkindly.

"Good," Elena said. "Quiet colors are for people who have nothing to protest."

Iris ran her finger along one section where the paint had pooled and dried in tiny ridges. "What are you protesting?"

Elena and Margaret exchanged glances.

"Forgetting," Margaret said finally. "We're protesting forgetting."

That night, unable to sleep, Elena found herself back in the sunroom. The moonlight turned the yellow walls into something softer, like the inside of a shell. She pressed her palm against a section that was still slightly tacky, held it there until she could feel her pulse transferring through her hand into the wall.

Margaret found her there at dawn, curled on their mother's reading chair, palm still pressed to the wall as if she were taking its temperature, or it hers. Without speaking, Margaret placed her own hand beside Elena's. They stood that way as the sunrise gradually warmed the room, the gold paint beginning to glow like something charging up, gathering strength for the day.

Later, when Elena was packing to return to California, Iris appeared with a small canvas she'd painted the same shade of yellow. 

"For your apartment," she said shyly. "So you don't forget either."

Elena held the canvas carefully, feeling its edges, still sharp with newness. This was simpler but no less profound: a teenage girl's hands moving across canvas, insisting that brightness matter, that color could be a form of memory, a way of saying: we were here, we felt joy despite everything.

On the plane home, Elena kept the canvas on her lap. The businessman beside her glanced at it, then looked away, as if the color were too much for the muted palette of public spaces. Good, Elena thought. Let it be too much. Let it demand attention, take up psychic space, refuse to apologize for its luminous insistence on being seen.

In Oakland, she hung the painting where morning light would hit it first. Some days, she pressed her palm against it, imagining she could feel through the canvas and paint all the way back to that sunroom in Scranton, where golden walls held the heat of two sisters' hands.

Margaret texted her a photo three weeks later: Iris in the sunroom, doing homework at their mother's desk, surrounded by those golden walls like a meditation on light itself.

"She says it helps her think," Margaret wrote. "Says the color makes her feel brave."

Elena smiled, touching the canvas on her own wall.
