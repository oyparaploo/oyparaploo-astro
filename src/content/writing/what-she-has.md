She's washing the fork again, the one with the scratch down the middle tine. Tuesday morning, 7:12. She knows it's a scratch now, has known for months, but her thumb still finds the groove, still rubs along it. The morning light through the kitchen window makes the metal shine except where the scratch is. The scratch swallows light.

In the apartment below, someone else is scrubbing a pan that will never come clean. She can hear the scraping through the floor. Both of them washing things that won't get cleaner.

The milk went bad yesterday. She discovered this after pouring it into her coffee, watching it separate into small islands. She drank it anyway. Not from poverty but from stubbornness, a refusal to let the milk make her late. Now, Tuesday morning, 7:14, she writes "buy milk" on the envelope of her gas bill. The same gas bill she'll throw away unopened. The milk won't get bought. This is her third morning drinking coffee black.

The refrigerator hums. All refrigerators hum at the same frequency, a fact she learned from a documentary about industrial design that she watched instead of sleeping. The hum is designed to disappear into the background. It works. She only notices it when it stops, that sudden silence that makes her think something has broken.

She opens the door. The light inside shows: three kinds of mustard, no bread. A container of something in foil that might have been lasagna once. Half an onion wrapped in plastic, sprouting green shoots like it's trying to escape. She closes the door.

At 7:20, she checks her phone for the first time. She'd promised herself no phone until after breakfast, but she needs to check the time, and checking the time means seeing the notifications, and seeing the notifications means the day has already started wrong. Seventeen emails. Five from the same company about the same sale. She unsubscribes. She unsubscribed last month. The emails keep coming.

Her neighbor is leaving for work. She hears his door, his keys, his footsteps on the stairs. They've lived next to each other for three years, separated by drywall and different schedules. She knows he takes his trash out on Wednesdays, that his alarm goes off at 6:45, that he talks to his mother on Sunday afternoons. She can hear the rhythm through the wall, though not the words. He probably knows things about her too—when she showers, when she can't sleep, when she opens and closes the refrigerator at midnight looking for something that isn't food.

The scratch on the fork happened at a dinner party in 2019. Someone trying to use it to open a wine bottle when the corkscrew broke. She can't remember who. The party feels like it happened to different people in a different world, back when sharing food from common bowls didn't require an act of faith. The fork survived. The friendships mostly didn't. But every morning, the scratch reminds her that once she had a kitchen full of people laughing, someone improvising with cutlery, wine flowing despite obstacles.

Now, Tuesday morning, 7:25, she puts the fork in the drawer. Tonight she'll take it out again, see the scratch, wonder if it's dirt, try to clean it. She does this every day.

At lunch, she watches the utility crew through her office window. They've been fixing the same section of pipe for four days. One person digs. Three watch. She's seen this everywhere—Tokyo, Berlin, the suburb where she grew up. Always one digging, three watching.

Her coworker texts: "Did you see that article about—"
She responds: "Please no."
"Fair. Someone's making banana bread with mayo."
They spend ten minutes discussing this banana bread. Neither of them knows the person making it well enough to ever taste it. It doesn't matter. The banana bread is theoretical, like most things they discuss to avoid discussing what they're actually thinking.

That evening, she waters the plant. It's dying on one side, thriving on the other, like it can't decide. She bought it to make the apartment feel more alive, but it's only made death more visible. Still, the new leaves keep coming, bright green and hopeful, unfurling next to the brown ones that crackle when touched.

The plant doesn't care about consistency. It just keeps going, half-dead, half-alive, fully itself.

At 10:45, she checks tomorrow's weather. Partly cloudy, high of 72. The same weather as today, which wasn't actually partly cloudy or 72. But the prediction is comforting, proof that tomorrow exists.

Her neighbor's TV murmurs through the wall. News or sports, she can't tell. The rhythms sound the same—urgent peaks, lulling valleys, commercials every eight minutes. She opens her refrigerator again. The light comes on, reliable as always. Nothing has changed since morning except the onion, which has grown another millimeter of green, pushing toward something.

She takes out her phone to add "buy milk" to her grocery app. There are already three instances of "milk" on the list from previous weeks. She adds a fourth. Tomorrow she'll forget to check the list, buy yogurt instead, come home to find four yogurts already in the refrigerator, no milk.

Wednesday morning arrives. She washes the fork, finds the scratch, rubs it with her thumb. The morning light through the kitchen window falls exactly as it did yesterday, showing everything except what needs to be seen. The refrigerator hums its engineered frequency. The milk is still unbought. The coffee is black. The plant has dropped another brown leaf, grown another green one.

Her neighbor leaves for work at 7:20. His door, his keys, his footsteps. She knows this rhythm like breathing, these lives running parallel, never touching except through shared walls and schedules. Today she'll see him at the mailboxes. They'll do the thing with their faces that isn't quite a smile. Neither will speak. Both will wonder if they should.

This is what days are made of—not events but repetitions, each one slightly different, bleeding into the next. The same fork, the same scratch, the same forgotten milk, everyone standing before their refrigerator's light, looking for something they forgot to buy, closing the door, opening it again because what else is there to do.

The light stays on when she opens the door. She trusts it to be there, and it is, showing her what she has, which is never what she came for, which is what she has.
