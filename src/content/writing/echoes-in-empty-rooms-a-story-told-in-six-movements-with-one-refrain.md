## Prelude

I met her in spaces where sound travels honestly—an abandoned textile mill where pigeons nested in the rafters, the basement of St. Augustine's where the youth group used to meet before the flood. These places held truth differently than crowded rooms. Every word bounced back threefold, cleaner somehow.

She moved through life like a string quartet warming up: two violins conversing, viola humming underneath, cello keeping time. The pattern never changed—A minor sliding into F major, breathing through D minor, lifting to E seventh. Never quite landing, but never needing to.

---

## Movement One: Recognition (A Minor)

She was translucent when I knew her. Not fragile—clear. Like those singing bowls made of quartz, where you can see the mallet moving inside even as the note rises. Her voice carried that same transparency, each word distinct as plucked strings marking time. Twelve beats, then eight. Rain on metal roofs.

Her apartment was deliberately spare. Three rooms, seventeen plants, each one named and thriving. She'd sit at her small table—reclaimed pine, she'd found it on Crescent Street—and drum out rhythms with her fingertips. Always the same pattern. Question, comfort, heartbeat, possibility. She called it her thinking song.

"Coffee?" But she meant *tell me who you are when no one's watching.*

The grinder hummed at exactly 107 rotations per minute. I counted once. She smiled when I told her—said that was the tempo of remembering without rushing. We'd sit while the French press steeped, four minutes precisely, letting silence do what words couldn't.

---

## Movement Two: The Lesson (F Major)

At home, she became more herself—like a photograph developing in reverse, from sharp details back to essential light. She was everyone's constant while they spun through chaos. Her presence had weight without heaviness, like bass notes you feel more than hear.

She moved with deliberate ease. Folding laundry became meditation. Washing dishes turned into water music. Everything had its rhythm—unhurried but never lazy. She'd hum while cooking, this low thrumming that made the walls vibrate sympathetically.

Her teaching was oblique:
- *Monday:* She showed me how sage leaves curl when they're ready for harvest (violins entering soft as dawn)
- *Wednesday:* We sat through an entire thunderstorm without speaking (viola adding its voice to the rain)  
- *Saturday:* She played recordings of empty concert halls, just the ambient sound (cello grounding everything)

"Listen to what's not there," she'd say. "That's where the music lives."

---

## Movement Three: History (D Minor)

She was twenty-two when she learned about resonance (she told me this in pieces, like feeding birds). Started collecting sounds instead of things:

- *First collection:* Her grandmother's breathing machine, recorded on cassette (strings barely audible)
- *Second:* Subway trains at 3 AM, when they sing differently (adding layers, complexity)
- *Third:* Her daughter's first cry, then her last phone call, seventeen years apart (full orchestra of grief and joy)

She spoke about her younger self like describing someone in an old photograph—fond but distant. "I was learning to generate my own light," she'd say, then pause to let the words find their shape in the air. The melody would climb just slightly, like she was still discovering what that meant.

Her internal world had texture—folk songs meeting jazz theory meeting that underground electronic pulse she'd loved in the '90s. Like someone playing acoustic guitar through a delay pedal, traditional but transformed.

---

## Refrain: The Instruction (E Seventh)

*Move, move, move.*

Not away—*through.* 

She'd say it walking. Say it cooking. Whisper it when I got stuck on a problem. Sometimes soft as breathing, sometimes clear as church bells. Always with that seventh note underneath suggesting the next chord, the next step, the next revelation.

The beat lived under everything else—restrained but insistent, like a heart that won't quit.

*Move through, not around. Move through, not over. Move, move, move.*

---

## Movement Four: Courage (A Minor)

Out in the world, she took up exactly the space she needed. No more, no less. Like a cello note in an empty warehouse—you couldn't ignore it, but it never overwhelmed.

I watched her navigate the hardest conversations with this strange grace. Never deflecting, never attacking. Just present. She'd teach seventh-graders at the community center, these kids everyone else had written off, and they'd lean in when she spoke. Not because she was loud—because she was real.

"Follow what makes you curious," she'd tell them. "But slowly. Let it develop."

---

## Movement Five: The Journey (F Major)

She carried two books always: *Be Ought To Will* by some philosopher I'd never heard of, and a field guide to bird songs she'd annotated until you couldn't see the original text. "Bird roots," she'd laugh, showing me her notes. "Songs that grow into trees if you plant them right."

Her curiosity had patience built in. She'd touch old walls to feel their stories—limestone from ancient seas, brick fired in vanished kilns. She'd stop mid-sentence to catch music in construction noise, sirens, arguments from upstairs. Everything was material.

"This is the real work," she said once, recording the sound of ice melting in her whiskey. "Finding what everyone else ignores."

---

## Movement Six: Transcendence (D Minor)

She aged like wood—deeper grain, richer tone. Not illness, just time doing what time does. The fullness came through then, all those years of listening concentrated into presence.

She spoke in bridges by the end, connecting things I hadn't seen were separate:

"I keep thinking about my daughter. Not the one I raised—the one I could have been. Sometimes I hear her in the space between heartbeats. She says 'keep going' but means 'stay present.' Do you understand?"

(When she died—yes, in my arms, the cello dropped to its lowest note and held us both suspended—it wasn't ending. It was modulation. The volume dropped to almost nothing, but the song continued.)

She told me this over dinner. Candles, bread she'd baked that morning, wine from a bottle she'd saved for no occasion except this one. The simplicity held everything—steady rhythm supporting a magnificent soul.

---

## Final Refrain: The Echo

*Move, move, move* becomes *moved, moved, moved* becomes *moving, moving, moving.*

The story doesn't end—it cycles like her chord progression, each return softer but more certain.

In her apartment (I have the key still, though strangers live there now), I swear the walls remember. A minor to F, D minor to E seventh, around and around, never resolving because resolution was never the point.

---

## Coda: What Remains

She was:
- 4 AM light through dusty windows (beginning in whispers)
- The rest that makes music possible (space for breathing)
- Soup simmering on Sunday (range from silence to symphony)
- The rhythm underneath all rhythm (that patient electronic pulse)

I carry her tempo now—that unhurried flow with underground bass notes, that swaying measurement that makes time negotiable. When I'm very quiet in empty spaces, I hear her in the natural reverb, in how certain light falls through abandoned windows, in the way plants grow toward sun they can't see yet.

*Move,* she says through memory, *move, move forward.*

Translation: *Follow what fascinates you, but with patience. Be real without forcing reality on others. Let your truth echo until it fills the space naturally. This is advice from someone who learned it frequency by frequency—not shouted wisdom but whispered understanding, urging you toward honest, patient discovery.*
