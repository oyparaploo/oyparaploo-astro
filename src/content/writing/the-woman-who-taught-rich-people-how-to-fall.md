*Lead-in:*
Found this typed on company letterhead, crumpled in a trash can outside Morrison & Associates after they went bankrupt. Coffee rings on every page. Someone had written "She was right" in the margins, then crossed it out, then written it again. Security camera footage shows a janitor reading it at 3 AM and crying.

Branch Holloway showed up in a cardigan from Goodwill. Someone's initials on the pocket—E.M. Probably dead.

The executives were expecting someone who looked like success. Not someone who took two buses because the subway was $2.90.

"You're the falling expert?" Jennifer Martinez asked, checking her phone. She made $450,000 a year. Branch had googled it.

"I teach dance. Taught. Before my studio got priced out."

They'd hired her after three executives had breakdowns. HR found her through some article about dancers with career-ending injuries. They were paying her $500. Most money she'd seen in months.

"Tea?" Branch pulled out a thermos.

"We have coffee—"

"This is different."

It tasted like grass. Like disappointment. Dandelion root from Prospect Park.

"It's terrible," said the CEO, Margaret Chen. Her watch cost $89,000.

"Yeah. Keep drinking."

Then Branch said: "Now we fall."

"What?"

"You hired me to teach grace during failure. Can't do that standing."

She folded to the floor. Not dramatically. Just stopped holding herself up. Her right knee clicked—old injury, no insurance for surgery, got addicted to painkillers, got clean, now it just clicks.

"We're not dancers—"

"You're falling anyway. Stock price. Marriages. That thing you call work-life balance." She stood, knee twinging. "You're just doing it ugly."

Jennifer stood to leave. "This is ridiculous—"

"You haven't slept in six days. Your daughter won't return your calls."

"How did you—"

"I don't know things. You just look like I did after my knee gave out. Like someone drowning while trying to smile."

Something in how she said it—flat, factual—made Jennifer sit.

Branch turned on her speaker. Duct tape and prayer holding it together. The music had gaps, downloaded badly from YouTube.

"We're learning to fall. That's it. Just fall better."

She taught them to soften impact. To protect themselves while still moving through the collapse. Her knee clicked with each demonstration.

"Stop thinking," she told Jennifer. "Fall like everything's already broken."

"I don't—"

"Your daughter already hates you. Your marriage is done. Now what? Now you choose how you land."

Jennifer fell. Badly. Then better. Started crying. Kept falling.

Three hours on the conference room floor. Italian marble. Cold. Expensive.

"My company's failing," Margaret said, mascara running.

"I know. Saw someone crying over spreadsheets in the bathroom."

"My marriage too."

"Wedding ring's been off two weeks."

"But I feel… lighter?"

"That's just exhaustion. Or maybe acceptance. Same thing sometimes."

They met weekly. Branch teaching them to fall. The company still tanked. Stock dropped 47%. But something shifted. They stopped fighting the collapse.

"You're like a meditation teacher," someone said.

"I'm a dance teacher who can't afford dance studios. Teaching rich people to fall for $500 a session."

"What you've given us—"

"Is what poverty taught me for free. How to fall because you have no choice. How to get up because you have no choice. How to fall again."

Last session, same terrible tea.

"Are you religious?" Margaret asked.

"My grandmother was. Said God lived in broken things. But she died alone because I was too high to visit. So what do I know about God?"

She left a note: "Invoice attached. Net 30 days."

Jennifer kept it taped to her monitor next to her daughter's photo. The one from before everything started falling.

The company went under six months later. Some people found other jobs. Others didn't.

But they fell differently. With something like intention. Like Branch with her clicking knee, falling and rising and falling again.

"What was she?" Margaret asked Jennifer at the bankruptcy hearing.

"Someone who charged us $500 to learn what she learned from losing everything."

"Did it help?"

Jennifer thought about her daughter, who still wasn't calling. Her husband, who'd filed for divorce. The job interviews that weren't going well.

"I don't know. But I fall better now."

They never saw Branch again. Heard she was teaching somewhere else. Or stopped teaching. Or fell and didn't get up. Different stories.

But sometimes in meetings, when everything was collapsing, someone would make that gesture—letting go without drama—and everyone would remember: 

You fall anyway. Might as well fall with technique.

Branch's knee clicking through every demonstration. That sound was the lesson. Not the words about grace or transformation. Just the click of damaged bone, still moving, still teaching, still falling for $500 a session because rent was $1,800 and the subway was $2.90 and her daughter wasn't calling and falling was the only thing she knew how to teach.

*Coda:*
Everyone's falling. The ones teaching grace are just the ones who've fallen enough times to develop technique. Not wisdom. Not transformation. Just technique. How to hit the ground without shattering. How to get up when getting up seems pointless. How to fall again because the ground's still there and gravity hasn't changed its mind and the knee still clicks but you need the $500 and these rich people need to believe falling can be graceful, even when it's not, even when it's just falling.
