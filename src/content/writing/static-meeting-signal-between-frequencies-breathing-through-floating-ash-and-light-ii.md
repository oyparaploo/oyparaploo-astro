The smoke rises like a whisper unanswered, and I think of the old archivist in her room, recording the city's sounds before the dust storms arrive. Everything will be buried in silt, she knows, but still she works—her steady hands transcribing what remains. This is determination, perhaps: continuing when the ash clogs the atmosphere, when even breathing becomes an act of will.

I drive east, ahead of the megafire wall, and remember my grandmother's stories of sandstorms in the old country. How she would stuff cloth under the doors, cover the windows, wait. Now the climate reporter beside me films everything, turning disaster into data, trauma into contemporary art. But I understand—we each find our own way to bear witness.

The insulin shortages worry me. My neighbor, diabetic and pragmatic, counts his remaining vials like precious stones. The ticking clock of his condition mirrors the larger countdown we all face. In the odd-numbered diary I keep, I transcribe the alerts that interrupt our days, these interludes that have replaced our ordinary rhythms.

Can you still hear us? This refrain follows me. The elliptical sentences that end mid-thought remind me of conversations with my mother, how her voice would fade on the telephone, distance swallowing words. Now it's smoke that swallows sound, carbon particulate levels that determine when we can speak clearly to one another.

Here's the thing about writing climate disaster fiction—everyone wants the elevator pitch until they realize it's literally about elevators stopping mid-floor when the power grids fail. High-level lens hooks, my editor says. Make it sellable. As if eco-grief could be packaged into a neat three-act structure.

So I'm driving east, ahead of a megafire wall, and I've picked up this climate-reporter hitchhiker who won't stop filming. "This is content," she says, pointing her phone at the ash-clogged atmosphere. "This is data versus contemporary art." I want to tell her: honey, we're all just gig-economy artists navigating disaster logistics now. Even storytelling is a hustle.

The character constellation arcs are obvious: pragmatic diabetic protagonist (insulin shortages = ticking clock), the old archivist recording city sounds before dust storms bury everything, her childhood friend turned air-filter smuggler. Classic moral foil setup. But here's what publishers don't understand—the real tension isn't in the plot. It's in watching language suffocate under smoke.

Can you still hear us? That's the refrain that editors want me to cut. Too repetitive, they say. But repetition is the point—air-quality index numbers as heartbeats, rhythmic reminders that we're still alive, still breathing particulate matter.

The megafire wall advancing from the west measured 47 miles across its leading edge when the climate reporter first noticed the ash beginning to fall. This was Tuesday, 3:47 PM, air quality index 367—hazardous. The particulate matter wasn't just smoke; spectrographic analysis would later reveal traces of vinyl siding, roofing tar, and the distinctive chemical signature of burned insulation. Someone's home, atomized and airborne.

The driver, heading east on Highway 20, understood the mathematics of evacuation: fuel capacity minus consumption rate multiplied by distance to safety. His passenger, the reporter, filmed everything—the apocalyptic light, the way smoke moved like a living thing. She kept calling it "data versus contemporary art," but he knew better. This was physics. Convection columns reaching 40,000 feet, generating their own weather systems, creating winds that could throw burning debris miles ahead of the main fire front.

Carbon particulate levels peaked at 2.5 micrometers—small enough to penetrate deep into lung tissue, to cross the blood-brain barrier. The tape hiss that replaced clear communication wasn't metaphorical; it was the literal degradation of audio quality as ash interfered with electronic signals. Music stored on cloud servers crashed not from heat but from power grid failures cascading through data center cooling systems.

"Can you still hear us?" The question appeared in emergency broadcasts, in texts between separated families, in the final transmissions from fire crews overrun by flame fronts moving at 50 miles per hour. Sound versus silence became a binary indicator of survival.

We all eventually oscillate between zeal and nihilism—unfortunately, this psychological pattern has been documented in disaster response research. Initial adrenaline-fueled action gives way to exhaustion, to the recognition that individual effort cannot match systemic failure. Hope fatigue sets in like a chronic condition.

Pour sand slowly onto hot metal—this is the sound of time passing, of memory fading. The tape hiss that replaces dialogue when the air quality drops too low. But even in this suffocated language, with its numerous line breaks where breath should be, we find ways to speak.

This is why it matters: it humanizes wildfire statistics, shows us ourselves in the storm-chasers and activists, the lobbyists and TikTokers. We are all part of this panoramic saga, following the ricochets through escalating disaster, learning to live with smoke as metaphor for unprocessed trauma.

Clear skies versus particulate opacity—this is how we measure honesty now. In the end, we return to the simple question, gentle but persistent: Can you still hear us?
