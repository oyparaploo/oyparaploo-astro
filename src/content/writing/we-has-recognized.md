*The night janitor at the city's contemporary art museum begins creating unauthorized exhibitions from what artists throw away, discovering that the labor of maintaining art might be more important than making it—and that a cornucopia of necessary and useful things already exists in what everyone else calls garbage.*

Wrack Lumière had been cleaning the Contemporary for seven years. Every night, emptying trash from artists' studios. Every night, seeing what got thrown away. Sketch after sketch. Broken frames. Paint tubes squeezed to death. Failed attempts worth more than her annual salary. All this... shit that was actually...

"What are you building, woman?" the security guard asked, finding Wrack in Gallery C at 3 AM, garbage everywhere—no, not everywhere, arranged, sort of, maybe—

"Something about value... labor... I don't—practice? Fuck, I don't know."

She'd started just keeping things. A sketch here—Brendan Malone's discarded portrait, he made $300,000 per painting. A broken frame there—Maria Santos, represented by Gagosian, threw it out after her opening. Then sorting but not really. Then arranging but badly. Now Gallery C (closed for renovations since 2019, no budget to actually renovate) was overflowing with excess—with and without luxury, with more than enough of what nobody wanted, spilling into Gallery D, the hallways, the bathroom even.

"This is..." the guard started.

"Cornucopia of necessary and useful things. Or trash. Or... both? Neither?"

"Does the title reframe abundance?" he asked, then laughed. "Is the meaning profound or... or not? Sorry, I sound like—"

"Like you get it," Wrack interrupted.

"I see you every night. Eleven dollars fifty cents an hour. You're proposing different abundance. In abundance... wait, that's not... recognizing richness in what we need?"

His name was Hinge Beaulieu. Used to make art. MFA from RISD. $95,000 in debt. Now watched empty galleries fill with Wrack's... whatever these were. Watched rich people look at million-dollar paintings while he made less than Manhattan parking costs.

"Culture says we don't have enough," Hinge said, picking up a crushed can—Red Bull, artist's helper, $3.49 at the museum cafe, 89 cents at the bodega. Adding it to a pile that might be sculpture or might be pile.

"Need more newer better—" Wrack agreed.

"But look at all these essential things we already—"

"HAVE!" They both shouted, then laughed like lunatics. Like people who stayed awake all night for money, not art.

They started working together. After midnight, building or destroying or both. Beautiful aesthetic value emerging from literal garbage, or maybe it was still garbage but beautiful garbage or—

"Is it worth contemplating?" Wrack would ask, holding something broken—piece of a $50,000 sculpture that broke during install, insurance paid out, artist already forgot.

"Or not?" Hinge would add.

"Garbage or deemed worthless—"

"Why is it art—"

"Or anti-art—"

"Should this be good enough for museum?"

They'd crack up. They were IN a museum. Institution requiring $47 million annual budget while they made... this... for free, for nothing, for everything. The museum's electric bill: $380,000 a year. Wrack's annual salary: $23,920 before taxes.

Peal Dubois found them week three. Data entry, spreadsheets, crying over funding cuts that meant her $28,000 position might be eliminated while the director's $450,000 salary remained untouched.

"That's..." she started, seeing their chaos. "Artsy Assemblage or... act of bringing disparate... disparate elements..."

"Into brand new configurations!" Wrack finished.

"The assemblage is beautiful because we has—" Peal stopped. "Did I say we has?"

"We has!" Hinge confirmed. "We does! We is!"

"Grammar's breaking—"

"Everything's breaking—"

"Good!"

They became three. Then seven when gift shop workers joined—$12 an hour, no benefits, selling $45 t-shirts that cost $3 to make. Then fifteen, twenty, who knows, people kept coming, leaving, coming back. All museum workers. Invisible. Essential. Underpaid. Seeing millions in art while making nothing.

"Recycling makes garbage useful—" someone said. Mo from shipping. $15 an hour to handle art worth more than he'd make in ten lifetimes.

"No!" Wrack interrupted. "Garbage already has its own gorgeous... use... gorgeousness... just needs recognizing and carefully or—"

"HAPHAZARDLY!" everyone yelled.

"Arranged!" 

They threw things. Placed things. Threw them again. The violence necessary. The arrangement essential. Both at once.

"Reproductive labor," Peal said one night, covered in dust from crawling through the museum's storage—90% of the collection never displayed, rotting in climate-controlled rooms. Something sticky—leaked fixative from a conservation project, probably toxic.

"Work of maintaining life—"

"Caring for others—"

"Sustaining daily—"

"What is presently unseen and—"

"UNACKNOWLEDGED!" they all screamed. The scream echoed through galleries where a single painting's insurance was more than their combined lifetime earnings.

This became their thing. Call and response but broken. Overlapping. Interrupting. Like their lives—fragments stealing time from sleep to make something from nothing.

"Reinterpreting routine—"

"Resulting repetition—"

"Doing something over and—"

"OVER AND OVER AND—"

"Artificial Essentials!" Mélodie Scraps shouted. She'd joined from the gift shop after her third wage theft complaint was ignored. Brought the unsold postcards—thousands, Monet and Basquiat becoming wallpaper, becoming something else.

"Society literally cannot function—"

"Without these things!"

"Without us!"

"Pollution!"

"Non-perishable garbage undervalued in—"

"All market economies!"

"INSANE HOPE AS TRANSFORMATION!"

Wrack held up a broken frame filled with cafeteria napkins people had cried into. Visitors' tears. Workers' tears. Her own tears from the day she'd found out her daughter couldn't visit because Wrack worked nights, courts called it "unstable environment." The napkins formed a pattern. Like a map of grief. Like a $2 million Rothko but honest.

The museum director found them week eight. Dr. Catherine Pierce. Yale PhD. Salary package worth $650,000 including housing allowance. Gallery C was... impossible to describe. Garbage art? Anti-art? Just garbage? But somehow...

"What is this?"

"Everyone has own truth isn't relativism," Wrack said. Exhausted. Making $11.50 an hour to speak philosophy to someone who'd never cleaned anything.

"Quite the contrary—"

"Truth is fluidity—"

"Truth is... messy? Moving?"

"Transformation!"

"Continuous becoming!"

"Truth is we doesn't know but we doing it anyway!"

The director stood surrounded by her museum's waste made into... into...

"This can't be here—"

"Things become present at hand!" Wrack interrupted.

"Through attention—"

"We create importance—"

"Through practice!"

"Where there was none!"

"Or was there always?"

They were all talking at once now. The director couldn't follow. Good. Let her feel what confusion was like. What working nights for seven years straight did to your brain. What seeing millions in art while making poverty wages did to your soul.

"All types languages shape thoughts—"

"And feelings—"

"When we remove—"

"When we blend—"

"We training visitors—"

"We TRAINING!" Wrack emphasized. "Not ARE training! We training because we has recognized!"

"Think in flows not segments!"

"Teaching another way of—"

"Perceiving reality!"

A donor wandered in. Or maybe janitor. Or artist. Hard to tell at 4 AM covered in garbage juice and glory. Everyone looked the same covered in the museum's excess.

"The reproductive labor in each work—"

"Searching sorting cleaning—"

"Arranging rearranging—"

"Joining stringing—"

"Countless hours of maintenance—"

"Making evident the hidden—"

"Behind walls—"

"Or out in open—"

"LABOR!"

The museum tried stopping them. Failed. Tried officializing them—offered to make it a "community engagement project" with no pay but "exposure." They refused. Kept working days, creating nights. Or was it creating days, working nights? The boundary dissolved then reformed then dissolved messier.

"We're the museum," Hinge said one dawn, Gallery C through F now infected with their assemblages.

"We always was," Wrack corrected.

"Were?"

"WAS. We was. We is. We has been. We has recognized what was always true."

"Museum isn't art on walls—"

"It's labor of making something important—"

"Happen!"

"Daily!"

"Repeatedly!"

"Without acknowledgment!"

"For eleven fifty an hour!"

Someone suggested documentation. They documented. Then threw away the documentation. Then made art from the thrown away documentation. Then threw that away. Then rescued it. Cycle cycle cycle. Like their shifts. Like their paychecks that came and went without anything leftover.

"Beautiful aesthetic value," Wrack would say, holding actual trash. Someone's lunch wrapper. A visitor's used tissue. A pink slip from HR.

"Worth contemplating?" Hinge would ask.

"Or not?" Peal would add.

"Yes!" someone would shout.

"No!" someone else.

"Both!" everyone.

The work spread. Not just galleries. Bathrooms where they'd cried during shifts. Offices where they'd been disciplined for being three minutes late. The parking garage where they slept in cars between doubles. Garbage art or art garbage or just necessary useful things everywhere. The museum became unrecognizable. Better. Worse. Both.

"This is our cornucopia," Mélodie said one night, crying or laughing. Both. Always both.

"Of necessary—"

"And useful—"  

"Things!"

"That nobody needs!"

"That everyone needs!"

"That we needs!"

"That we has!"

They never stopped. Never got permission. Never got stopped. Just kept maintaining and making, making and maintaining, until nobody could tell which was which, until the museum was garbage was art was labor was life was—

"We has recognized," Wrack would say, mopping floors that would never be clean enough, arranging garbage that would never be art enough, living a life that would never be valued enough.

"We has," everyone would answer.

Or not answer. Just keep working. Keep making. Keep recognizing the gorgeous in the discarded. Keep creating importance where there was none. Or where there always was but nobody saw until the janitors started saving everything, arranging everything, making the reproductive labor visible by making such a beautiful necessary useful mess that you couldn't ignore it anymore.

You couldn't ignore them anymore.

We has recognized.

We is recognizing.

We continues.

Always for $11.50 an hour.

Always in galleries worth millions.

Always knowing the garbage is more honest than the art.

Always.

*We are all maintaining something while destroying something while creating something, recognizing that labor is transformation is garbage is art, that importance emerges through practice and mess and repetition, and that the real cornucopia isn't what's displayed but what sustains and disrupts the display—the endless, chaotic, reproductive labor of making something important happen without permission, without acknowledgment, without ever stopping to ask if it's worth contemplating or not, all for $11.50 an hour in buildings full of millions we'll never touch except to clean.*
