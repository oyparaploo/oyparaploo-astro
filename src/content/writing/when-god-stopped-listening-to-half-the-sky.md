Someone I loved once told me that wisdom is like water—it finds its way through any crack, around any stone, into any vessel that will hold it. I think about this when I walk into churches, mosques, temples, and notice how the light falls through windows designed by men, onto floors where women kneel but do not stand to speak.

There was a woman once, Julian of Norwich, who sat in a cell while the Black Death ate through Europe like a terrible hunger. She wrote that she saw God as Mother, birthing the world in blood and beauty, holding all creation the way a woman holds a child—fierce and tender at once. All shall be well, she wrote, and all manner of thing shall be well. The church fathers built cathedrals that scraped against heaven, but her small room held truths too large for their stone walls to contain. They remember Augustine, who hated his body. They remember Aquinas, who built God from logic. But Julian, who saw divine love as a mother sees her child? She becomes a footnote, a curiosity, a sweet mystic—never mind that she reimagined everything.

This is what I want to tell you: every religion that claims to hold the whole sky gives women only a window to look through. Do you see it? This strange mathematics where the infinite divine speaks only in bass tones, where eternity has a gender, where half of creation is invited to listen but not to speak?

Let me tell you about beginnings, because they matter. They always do. Every sacred story starts with breaking—breaking rules, breaking expectations, breaking open. Jesus sits at a well, dusty and thirsty, speaking theology with a Samaritan woman as if she too could understand the holy. His disciples return from buying bread and stand frozen, shocked that their teacher discusses living water with someone so unclean, so female, so other. But there he is, and there she is, and between them flows a conversation about truth that ignores every boundary their world has built.

Muhammad, shaking with terror in a cave, runs home to Khadija. Cover me, he whispers. She does more than cover him. She believes him when no one else will. Her wealth keeps him alive when others want him dead. Her faith makes Islam possible. I'm not being poetic here—I mean without her money, without her connections, without her absolute belief, there would be no Islam. The religion exists because a woman said yes, I believe you.

Even Buddha, that prince who left everything, watches women weep for their dead children and recognizes suffering as suffering, salt as salt, tears as tears. Finally, reluctantly, he opens the door to women seeking enlightenment. Though he predicts his teaching will now last only half as long. Why, do you think? What was he afraid would grow in the garden where women were also gardeners?

Even in these wild moments of beginning, there's this hesitation, like a held breath. The door opens, but only partway. Light enters, but through a crack. As if too much illumination might reveal something that cannot be unseen. And perhaps they were right to be afraid. Perhaps they knew what every gardener knows—that life wants to grow wild, that order is always temporary, that what you plant is never quite what rises from the earth.

You know what's dangerous for revolutions? Success. I mean it. Watch any wildflower that became a garden, any stream that became a canal, any movement that became a monument. When the tents become temples, when the wanderers become rulers, when the gold arrives—watch what happens to the women who were there at the beginning. They become Mary Magdalene, transformed from apostle to prostitute though the Bible never says this. They become Aisha, remembered for her marriage bed but not her two thousand hadiths, her military leadership, her sharp intelligence that challenged men who thought themselves scholars. They become Gargi Vachaknavi, who asked questions so profound about the nature of reality that the sage warned her head would shatter if she continued—but the thing is, the text records her questions anyway, as if even the scribes knew that truth once spoken cannot be entirely unheard.

The institutions develop a technology—no, that's too clean a word. They develop a system, messy and brutal and effective. First, they lock the language itself. Sanskrit becomes male property, Latin too, classical Arabic as well—like putting the stars behind glass where you can see them but never touch. Women can listen to God's words but only in translation, only through the filter of male interpretation, only as others decide they should be understood.

Then they build separate gardens, telling women they are roses too precious for the rough world outside. You are the heart, they say, while we are the head. You are the private devotion while we are the public prayer. You are the feeling while we are the thinking. A pedestal, it turns out, is still a cage—just prettier, just harder to escape because who can complain about being called precious, special, pure? Who can argue with being lifted up, even if lifting up means being lifted away from power, from choice, from voice?

And then, with surgical precision, they interpret selectively. When Paul mentions women apostles, centuries of scholars explain why apostle must mean something different in their case. When texts show women leading, teaching, prophesying, elaborate theories emerge about special circumstances, never to be repeated. Like cutting branches that grow toward light, like training a vine to grow only where you want it, like turning a forest into a garden where everything wild has been tamed or removed.

But here's the thing about life—it wants to live. And wisdom? It wants to be spoken. Truth wants to be true. Rabia al-Adawiyya, the slave girl who became Islam's greatest mystic, said she loved God neither from fear of hell nor hope of paradise but for love itself, pure and purposeless as sunlight. The scholars couldn't erase her because her words had already taken root in too many hearts, already bloomed in places they couldn't reach.

Julian wrote in English, the people's tongue, while the church locked its truths in Latin. She saw God as Mother nurturing creation, as lover longing for union, as friend walking beside us through shadow. The church fathers could dismiss her as a simple anchoress, but her visions spread like seeds on wind, finding soil in souls that had been waiting for exactly this version of divine love.

Gargi asked the sage what was the foundation of water, of fire, of wind, of space itself. Her questions went deeper and deeper until she touched the groundlessness of everything, the void that holds existence. Stop, he said, or your head will shatter. But the questions were already asked. The text, despite itself, records them. As if truth, once spoken, creates its own gravity, draws witnesses, demands preservation even by those who wish it silenced.

They were not erased—that would be too obvious, too violent, too hard to justify. Instead, they were diminished, made small, called exceptional. That terrible word that means not like other women, as if other women, given the chance, wouldn't also shake foundations, wouldn't also see God differently, wouldn't also offer medicines the world desperately needs.

Fear teaches us things if we're willing to learn from it. The church, the mosque, the temple fear women's wisdom the way winter fears spring, not because it is weak but because it changes everything. You don't need laws against what's impossible. No religion forbids humans from breathing underwater or flying without wings—we simply can't. But thousand-page theological arguments about why women cannot be priests? Detailed laws about what women cannot touch, cannot read, cannot say? Centuries of scholarship explaining why God's voice drops an octave when speaking authority? This is fear building walls against a flood that's already seeping through.

Every time a barrier falls, something extraordinary happens. Protestant churches ordain women and suddenly there are female ministers everywhere, as if they'd been waiting just beneath the surface. Women gain access to Islamic education and female scholars emerge, reinterpreting everything, finding Khadija the businesswoman, Aisha the scholar, Fatima the fierce. Buddhist women regain ordination rights and build monasteries that thrive like gardens that were always waiting for the right gardeners. The fear was never that women would fail. The fear was that women would succeed, and in succeeding, reveal that male monopoly on religious authority was never divine mandate but human choice, never cosmic law but earthly power grabbing what it could while it could.

I'll be blunt—these institutions chose power over truth. They chose to control women's bodies, whom they marry, how they dress, when they speak. They chose to control wealth through inheritance laws that favor sons, work permissions that require male approval, property rights that pass from father to husband to son but never rest in female hands. They chose to control the story itself, who gets remembered, whose wisdom counts, what God really meant to say.

The Virgin Mary gets venerated not for her yes to mystery, not for her courage in accepting an impossible calling, but for impossible purity—motherhood without sexuality, the ultimate male dream of reproduction without female desire. The devoted wife gets praised for submission, not for the thousand ways she holds the world together, not for the wisdom she whispers to children who will grow up to lead nations. The silent saint becomes acceptable only when her ecstasy doesn't translate to authority, when her visions don't challenge the existing order, when her mysticism remains safely personal, never political.

What gospels were never written because the apostles couldn't hold pens? What suras were never revealed because the prophets were home with children? What vedas disappeared because the sages were told their questions would break their heads? We lost theologies written from bodies that bleed and heal in rhythm with the moon, that understand creation from the inside, that know birth as resurrection and nursing as eucharist and menopause as sacred transformation. We lost ethics shaped by those who create life but are kept from taking it, justice that repairs rather than punishes, power that flows rather than hoards. We lost half the conversation with the divine.

Imagine a Christianity where Mary Magdalene's gospel survived, where her witness carried the same weight as Peter's denial. Imagine an Islam where Aisha's interpretations stood beside Muhammad's, where her questions shaped law as much as his answers. Imagine a Hinduism where Gargi's questions were pursued to their beautiful, terrifying end, where the void she touched became the foundation for understanding everything else. That's not a different religion—that's the same religion finally telling the truth.

Heresy means choice in Greek. Women choosing to read, to interpret, to speak, to lead. The very act of choice becomes heretical when you were never meant to choose. But wildflowers know something gardens forget—life finds a way. Seeds blow over walls. Roots crack foundations. What you bury becomes fertilizer for what grows next.

In every generation, in every tradition, the voice returns. A woman in Saudi Arabia gets behind the wheel and drives toward a horizon that was always hers. A girl in Pakistan goes to school despite death threats because education is light and she was born for illumination. A nun performs mass in a forest because the church won't let her near the altar but the trees don't care about canon law and God, she suspects, is more present in pine sap than in prohibition. A daughter asks her father why she can't read from the Torah, and he has no answer that doesn't sound like fear.

And increasingly, they don't wait for permission. They know what the sparrow knows—song is song, flight is flight, the sky belongs to everyone or no one. The machinery creaks and groans, the old interpretations crack like ice in spring. Seminaries fill with women who read Greek and Hebrew and Arabic for themselves, who discover that the original texts say things the translations tried to hide, who find God speaking in feminine verbs and inclusive pronouns and metaphors that have nothing to do with thrones and armies and everything to do with birth and bread and breath.

What happens when the conversation finally includes everyone? Will these religions survive the return of their missing half? Can Christianity embrace the Magdalene's wisdom and remain Christianity? Can Islam hear Aisha's voice as clearly as Muhammad's? Can Hinduism let Gargi's questions shatter what needs shattering? Or will they become museums—beautiful, intricate, historically significant, but empty of living truth, visited only by tourists who marvel at what humans once believed when they thought God cared about gender?

The heresy of her voice isn't that it speaks falsehood. The heresy is that it speaks truth—truth that power cannot acknowledge and remain unchanged. But power never understood what every seed knows: what you bury becomes food for what grows next. And what's growing now cannot be stopped by walls or words or centuries of careful forgetting.

I keep thinking about that sound—not thunder, not ending, not catastrophe. Maybe it's harmony, finally complete. Maybe it's the conversation with the divine finally including everyone who was always meant to speak. Or maybe it's just God, remembering how to listen to the whole sky.
