In what used to be her mother's garage in Davis, Marla discovered the last project: a military surplus ammunition box transformed into an incubator, lined with raw silk and moss. Inside, cocoons hung like prayers, each one labeled with her mother's careful script: "Luna - found 7/15," "Polyphemus - emerged 8/2," "Cecropia - waiting."

The house still smelled like her mother's gardening gloves - dirt and lavender, promise and completion. But the garden itself was gone, bulldozed for the new development, along with the milkweed stands her mother had cultivated for thirty years.

Her sister Beth arrived that evening, still in her park ranger uniform from Yosemite. She stopped when she saw Marla sitting cross-legged on the floor, the ammunition box cradled in her lap like an infant.

"Mom was keeping them," Marla said. "The last ones."

Beth knelt beside her. The cocoons were various stages of development - some papery and spent, others still full with transformation. In the corner of the box, their mother had tucked a note: "When they have nowhere else to go, we become their forest."

That night, a Luna moth emerged. They watched it unfold in the kitchen, its pale green wings spreading impossibly wide against the backdrop of packed boxes. It was perfect and doomed - no mates for miles, no trees of its kind left in the county. Marla extended her finger, and the moth climbed on, weighing almost nothing.

"We can't save them," Beth said quietly.

"Mom thought we could."

"Mom thought a lot of things."

They sat in silence as the moth tested its wings. Marla remembered the previous summer, finding her mother in the garden at dawn, transferring a newly emerged Cecropia from her cupped palms to a wild cherry branch. "Hold it like you're holding water," her mother had said. "Firm enough that it feels safe, loose enough that it knows it can leave." Those same hands had taught Marla to braid her hair tight for soccer games, warrior-strong, then loosen it at night, tender as lullabies.

Over the next week, as they sorted through the house, more moths emerged. Each morning brought new wings - Io moths with their startling eyespots, delicate Rosy Maples, a single Imperial moth that filled Marla's entire palm. The sisters created a routine: Beth would prepare sugar water and overripe fruit, while Marla would hold each moth, letting it memorize flight in the safety of cupped hands before releasing it into the screened porch their mother had built specifically for this purpose.

"It's not sustainable," Beth said one evening, watching a Cecropia moth circle the porch light. "They need wild spaces, not human terrariums."

"I know," Marla said. But she kept holding them anyway, understanding for the first time why their mother had.

The estate agent, a efficient woman named Chen, stopped by to assess the property. She paused when she saw the porch full of moths, their wings catching sunset light like stained glass windows.

"Those need to go," she said. "Buyers will think it's an infestation."

"They're not pests," Marla said, surprised by the sharpness in her voice. "They're refugees."

Chen's expression softened slightly. "My grandmother used to keep silkworms," she said. "Said they were the last memory of her village in China. But memories aren't enough to sustain a species."

That night, Marla dreamed of her mother's hands releasing thousands of moths into a forest that no longer existed. She woke to find Beth on the porch, a Polyphemus moth perched on her braided hair.

"I've been thinking," Beth said. "There's a recovery program at the park. For endangered lepidoptera. We could transport the cocoons there."

"All of them?"

"All but one," Beth said. "You should keep one. For mom."

They spent the next day creating individual carriers from their mother's collection of tea tins, each one cushioned with moss from the ghost of the garden. As they worked, Marla noticed Beth's hands had become like their mother's - rough but impossibly gentle, warrior and gardener both.

The chosen keeper was a Cecropia cocoon, the one their mother had marked "waiting." Marla fashioned a nest for it from her old soccer jersey, the one with the number her mother had sewn on crooked but with love. She placed it in the ammunition box, this strange armor protecting impossible fragility.

On the flight back to Pittsburgh, she kept the box on her lap. The businessman beside her glanced over once, curious, but said nothing. How could she explain that she was carrying the last of something, that her lap was a forest, her hands a habitat?

Three weeks later, in her apartment overlooking the Allegheny River, the moth emerged at dawn. Marla was ready with sugar water, with torn pieces of peach, with her cupped palms. The moth was perfect - russet and cream, with eyespots like tiny windows. It climbed onto her finger, wings still damp with becoming.

She thought of her mother, who'd spent her last years creating an ark for things that shouldn't need saving. Of Beth, driving cocoons through the night to safer forests. Of all the hands throughout history that had become reluctant sanctuaries when the wild world contracted.

The moth's wings dried slowly. Marla held perfectly still, feeling its tiny grip on her finger, the weight of being needed in ways that broke her heart. When it finally flew, it circled her apartment three times before landing on the window, wings spread against the glass like a prayer for spaces it would never find.

She opened the window anyway. The moth hesitated at the threshold between sanctuary and whatever waited beyond. Then it lifted into the Pittsburgh morning, carrying with it something Marla couldn't name - not hope exactly, but not surrender either. Something more like tenderness, fierce and futile, the kind that makes you keep trying even when you know you're holding the last of something in your insufficient hands.

That evening, Beth called. "Did it emerge?"

"This morning. I let it go."

"Into Pittsburgh?"

"Into the world. Whatever's left of it."

Beth was quiet for a moment. "Mom would have done the same."

Marla touched the empty ammunition box, still lined with moss and memory. Tomorrow she would drive to the botanical gardens, volunteer for their butterfly house, learn the names of other vanishing wings. She would braid her hair tight, warrior-strong, then loosen it to hold whatever fragile things still needed holding.
