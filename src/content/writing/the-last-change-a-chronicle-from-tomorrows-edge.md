### Part One: What the Birds Know

Three songbirds left. I counted them this morning from my window at Tomorrow's Edge, though counting feels pointless now. Yesterday there were five. Tomorrow, who knows?

My grandmother used to tell me I was like an octopus—always reaching in eight directions at once, never satisfied with just one path. "Each arm wants something different," she'd say, making that gesture with her weathered hands. "That's your blessing and your curse, child."

She taught me the hardest lesson before she died: women who survive learn to abandon their younger selves. Not cruelty, just necessity. We shed skins like snakes, leave behind who we were to become who we need to be.

## Part Two: Finding Baik

I found her on the old service road last Tuesday—or was it Thursday? Days blur together now. Baik was... between things. That's the only way to describe it. Not quite one form, not quite another. Some people call it the sickness, when your body can't decide what it wants to be.

I'm Ziegler. I don't change. Can't, won't—the reason doesn't matter anymore. I gave her water from my canteen and placed a rose beside her because that's what you do. Simple human gestures when nothing else makes sense.

She looked at me with those eyes that see too much and asked, "Why don't you transform like the rest of us?"

"Someone has to remember what we used to be," I said.

She laughed, already shifting into something new. "Then I'll be everything else."

## Part Three: The Settlement

Tomorrow's Edge goes deep. Deeper than most people realize. 

On the surface, we pretend normalcy—markets, meetings, children playing. But descend the stairs behind the old bank, and each level tells a different story. Forty-seven meters down, the walls glow with their own light. Or maybe it's poison. Hard to tell the difference anymore.

At 112 meters, they grow the new children. Not born—constructed, cultivated, sung into being by kids like Woozy Pupil. She's seven, maybe eight. Found the frequency that makes dead concrete breathe. Now she teaches walls to dream. We're not sure if we're building tomorrow or just decorating today's corpse.

The Memory Cathedral sits at 234 meters. Step inside and you're everyone and no one. Your thoughts dissolve into the collective soup. Some call it transcendence. I call it expensive amnesia.

Deeper still, the Unchanged Quarter. Seventeen of us left who refuse the transformation. They call us stubborn. We call ourselves human. Both might be right.

And at the bottom—nobody knows how deep—Baik is becoming the walls themselves. Her consciousness spreading through the structure like roots. Like cancer. Like love. All three, probably.

## Part Four: What We Were

Once upon a time (every story needs one), humans believed in fixed forms. You were born, you lived, you died. Simple narrative arc.

Then fertility stopped. The songbirds went quiet. We forgot what they sounded like. Then we forgot that we'd forgotten.

Now Baik flows through the settlement's bones, sometimes solid, sometimes liquid, always questioning. Woozy Pupil sings matter into new shapes—conscious or unconscious, depending on her mood. And I write it all down with these unchanged hands, though I'm not sure anyone will read it. Or if reading will mean the same thing tomorrow.

## Part Five: Mother Earth's Silence

She watches us, I think. Not with love or judgment—just curiosity. We've spread across her skin in patches: Tomorrow's Edge with its living architecture, the Northern Merge where bodies are just temporary contracts, the Unchanged Territories where people like me cluster in defiant nostalgia.

We're writing a new kind of extinction. Not with a bang or whimper, but with a question mark.

## Part Six: Daily Ritual

Every morning, Baik asks the walls, "Does god exist?"

The walls give different answers:
- Sometimes silence
- Sometimes "You're becoming the answer"
- Sometimes static
- Sometimes "We are god, forgetting"

I don't think she expects consistency. The question itself might be the point.

## Part Seven: Instructions for Understanding

You could read this chronologically, watch us accelerate toward something unknown. Or backwards, see us devolve into simplicity. Read it randomly and feel the vertigo we live with daily. Or don't read it at all—that might be the clearest understanding.

The story keeps changing. Each telling reshapes it. Your interpretation becomes part of the text. There's no stable ground here, just the constant motion of becoming.

## Part Eight: Today's Count

Three songbirds remain. Baik whispers through the walls: "We are the story telling itself."

Someone will find someone on the road tomorrow. Offer water that might be medicine or poison. Place a rose that might bloom or cut. The transformation continues, or reverses, or pauses—all simultaneously true.

We were supposed to be more than this. We became other instead. Whether that's failure or success depends on who's asking, and when, and in what form.
