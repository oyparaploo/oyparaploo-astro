*Lead-in:*
Found in a stenograph machine left at Brooklyn Criminal Court. Someone had typed this between trials, off the record. The janitor who found it said court reporters leave all the time—burn out, can't take it. This one left her machine still running, typing nothing into nothing.

Véronique Spool typed other people's words for seventeen years. $31,000 a year. Brooklyn rent: $2,100. The math didn't work.

"You type everything?" New prosecutor. Columbia Law. Shoes worth two months of Véronique's rent.

"Everything said."

"Nothing else?"

She looked at her steno machine. Held together with rubber bands. "I type what people say. Not what they mean."

But she saw both.

This trial: Mother killed her baby. Or baby died. Depends who's talking.

Baby was four months. Born addicted, 4 pounds 3 ounces. Mom was using throughout pregnancy. Also been prostituted since twelve. Baby died—suffocation or SIDS or inevitability.

Véronique typed: "The defendant willfully and deliberately—"

Saw: Mother was seventeen when she gave birth. Still had acne. Still watched cartoons.

Typed: "With malice aforethought—"

Saw: Mother's other kids in back row. Oldest maybe eleven. Same eyes. Already broken.

Troy Rousseau, public defender, cornered her after. Guilty verdict. Twenty-five to life. Mother collapsed quiet, bones giving up.

"You see different," he said.

"See what?"

"Like you're typing one thing but watching another."

"I type what's said."

"But you see—"

"Your suit's too big. Your dad's?"

He stopped. "What?"

"Nothing. I just notice things. You keep looking at the jury like they're going to ground you."

"That's—"

"You defend her or defend yourself from becoming her? Different things."

They kept running into each other. Halls, vending machines. Véronique eating crackers for lunch. $1.75.

"That mother's other kids," she said once. "You see them?"

Troy hadn't.

"Three kids. Foster care now. Watching mom get twenty-five years. Oldest will be in this courtroom in six years. Different charge, same story."

"How do you know?"

"I don't know. I just see patterns. Kid already has the look."

"What look?"

"Like he's guilty of something that hasn't happened yet."

Véronique started adding stuff to transcripts. Not officially. Just brackets: [defendant's child crying] [prosecutor checking phone] [judge takes antacid].

"What's this?" Judge asked once.

"Accuracy."

"Stick to words."

"Words lie."

"That's not your job."

"My job is $31,000 a year to watch families get destroyed by people making six figures."

"Your job is typing."

She typed: "Yes, Your Honor."
Thought: You make $174,000 to ruin lives.
Typed: [Judge's third antacid]

Jennifer from DA's office—$95,000 but drinking boxed wine—found Véronique crying in bathroom after child abuse case.

"You okay?"

"Kid still loves his dad. Dad who beat him. Love and terror mixed. You see that?"

"That's fucked."

"That's everyone. We're all both things. Victim and perp. Guilty and innocent. Court pretends it's one or other."

"That's how verdicts work."

"That's how verdicts fail."

Marcus the bailiff got it next. Just from watching. Twelve years, $44,000.

"You see through things," he said.

"I'm tired."

"No, like—you see the real crime."

"Which is?"

"Poverty."

She looked at him. "Every case—look, it's always the same. Poor person, desperate thing, jail, poorer, more desperate. Circle."

"That's—"

"Look. That dealer. Seventeen. Selling fentanyl. Making maybe $50 a day. Prosecutor makes $190,000 prosecuting him. Judge makes $174,000 sentencing him. His public defender makes $65,000 defending him badly. I make $31,000 typing it. The prison will make $62,000 a year housing him. Everyone profits except him."

"Jesus."

"No, just math."

Troy started defending different. Not claiming innocence. Just context.

"Your Honor, my client sold drugs. Also his school had forty kids per class. Also his mother works three jobs at $7.25. Also his life expectancy is 61 in this zip code versus 85 six miles north."

Judges hated it.

Sometimes juries got it.

Véronique typed faster. Like she was trying to catch what wasn't being said. The pauses. The looks. The way mothers mouthed "I love you" to kids getting twenty-year sentences.

"Slow down," judge said once.

"Can't."

"Why?"

"This is the speed of what's really happening. You send someone away for twenty years in twenty minutes. I'm just trying to keep up."

She started typing everything. Even silence. [Seventeen-second pause while mother realizes son won't see sunlight until 2044]

Day Véronique quit, she left her machine running. Just clicking at nothing.

Troy found her in parking garage. Her car—1997 Honda, 267,000 miles, door broken.

"Where you going?"

"Away from this."

"You changed how I see."

"No. You always saw. Everyone sees. Just nobody wants to look at what they see."

"The way every verdict is wrong?"

"Every verdict is both right and wrong. That's the problem. Mother did kill baby. Mother was also child herself. Both true. Court picks one truth. Ignores other."

She left. Machine kept clicking for hours before someone turned it off.

Troy, Jennifer, Marcus—they kept seeing both things. Guilty and innocent at the same time. Criminal who got criminalized first. It's all mixed up.

In court where poor people got sorted into cages, they became the new seers. Not typing but watching. The real record. The one nobody wanted:

Every criminal had been criminalized first.
Every verdict created more crime than it solved.
Every judge had stomach problems from knowing this.
Every prosecutor checked their phone to avoid seeing defendants' families.
Every public defender was defending themselves from their clients' lives.
Every court reporter saw too much for $31,000 a year.

*Coda:*
Court reporters type 260 words per minute but nobody asks about the words between words, the silences that contain verdicts, the pauses where mothers realize their children are gone. The real record is what isn't said: that everyone's guilty and innocent both, that poverty is the only crime being tried, that the system profits from the sorting while the sorted just disappear into numbers nobody reads except court reporters making $31,000 to watch it happen, 260 words per minute, every minute, every day.
