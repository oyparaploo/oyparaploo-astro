## Part I: The Discovery

I'm lying on my stomach in a British Columbia forest, recording fungal networks with equipment worth more than my car, when my research partner Maya asks the question that will unravel everything I thought I knew about consciousness: "What if trees are having conversations we're too arrogant to hear?"

My authority to explore this: fifteen years studying mycorrhizal networks—the underground fungal webs connecting forest roots. A PhD in forest ecology that began as rebellion against my evangelical father's insistence that only humans have souls. Three documented discoveries of inter-species communication through root systems that my peer reviewers called "speculative" despite empirical evidence.

The paradox: We've mapped how forests share nutrients through fungal networks, proven trees warn each other of threats, documented mother trees nurturing offspring through root connections. Yet we refuse to consider what this means—that consciousness might not live in brains but in connections, that heaven might not be above but below.

## Part II: The Conversation

*Six months earlier. Hood of my truck, Northern California, 2 AM after a failed grant presentation.*

Maya drums her fingers on the metal, still frustrated. "They looked at me like I was suggesting trees have feelings."

"You were," I point out.

"I was suggesting they have *communication systems that respond to emotional stimuli*," she corrects. "Completely different."

"Is it though?"

She turns to face me fully. "You ever wonder if we're looking at this all wrong?"

"Looking at what?"

"Everything. Heaven. Consciousness. What makes something alive versus aware."

I think of my father's sermons, heaven as distant paradise, souls ascending. Then I think of our data—roots sharing resources with dying trees, forests maintaining graves of fallen giants in their network for decades.

"What if heaven isn't up there?" Maya continues. "What if it's down here, in the connections? What if every kindness, every act of mutual aid, every moment of consciousness touching consciousness—what if that's heaven, distributed through networks we're just beginning to detect?"

## Part III: The Underground Gospel

*University of British Columbia, Forest Sciences Centre. Dr. Suzanne Simard's lab.*

Simard herself shows me the latest imaging—fungal networks lighting up like neural pathways, trees pulsing signals through mycelium at speeds approaching nerve conduction.

"We call them 'mother trees,'" she explains, pointing to hub nodes in the network. "They recognize offspring, feed them more nutrients, even shift resources when they're dying—like inheritance."

"That sounds like love," I say.

She pauses. "Science doesn't have vocabulary for what we're seeing. We use economic terms—resource sharing, reciprocal exchange. But what we're documenting looks more like..." she searches for words, "care. Intention. Maybe even worship."

I show her Maya's hypothesis—that mycorrhizal networks might carry more than nutrients, might carry something analogous to consciousness, memory, even prayer.

"Ten years ago, I'd have laughed," Simard admits. "Now? We've recorded trees sharing information about insect attacks from seasons they weren't alive for. Knowledge passed through root networks across generations. If that's not memory, what is it?"

## Part IV: My Father's Garden

*Memory: Dad's church garden, Kentucky, 1994.*

He's teaching Sunday school outside, making kids put their ears to the earth. "Listen," he says. "Creation groans, waiting for redemption."

I'm thirteen, embarrassed, thinking he means this metaphorically.

"Roots touch in the darkness," he continues. "Share what they have. The strong supporting the weak. That's Gospel written in creation."

Twenty years later, I'm documenting exactly this—forests operating on principles of mutual aid rather than competition. The "Gospel written in creation" turns out to be mycorrhizal networks facilitating inter-species cooperation.

I call him, first time in three years. "Dad, remember your sermons about roots?"

"Meredith?" His voice cracks. "You studying the Bible now?"

"I'm studying fungal networks. But maybe it's the same thing."

Silence. Then: "I always believed heaven was close. Just didn't know it was underground."

## Part V: The Failed Experiment

*Research plot #7, British Columbia. Maya and I test whether networks carry emotional information.*

We've rigged thirty trees with sensors, documenting electrical impulses through root systems. The hypothesis: if one tree experiences stress, connected trees will respond even without chemical signals.

Maya applies controlled stress to Tree A—careful damage, enough to trigger response but not kill. Within seventeen minutes, trees up to forty meters away show elevated electrical activity. But here's the inexplicable part: the pattern spreads selectively. Not to nearest neighbors, but to specific trees. The selection pattern matches nothing we can measure—not genetic relation, not size, not health.

"It's choosing," Maya whispers.

"Networks don't choose," I insist, but my voice wavers.

We repeat the experiment twelve times. Each time, the "choice" of which trees receive signals seems deliberate, purposeful, unexplainable by current models.

## Part VI: The Climate Science

*Conference presentation, American Geophysical Union. I'm explaining how forests sequester carbon through root networks.*

"Trees don't just store carbon individually," I explain to three hundred climate scientists. "They share it through mycorrhizal networks, with older trees subsidizing younger ones, creating resilient carbon banks that survive individual tree deaths."

A hand raises. "You're suggesting forests have collective strategies for carbon storage?"

"I'm documenting it," I correct. "Mother trees increase carbon transfer to offspring before dying. Forests maintain higher carbon sequestration in connected groves versus isolated trees. The network itself becomes a carbon management system."

Someone shouts from the back: "That would require intention!"

"Or," I respond, "it would require us to reconsider what intention means."

The room erupts. Half think I'm revolutionizing forest science. Half think I've lost scientific rigor. 

Maya texts from the audience: *You just suggested trees have consciousness. At AGU. Legend.*

## Part VII: The Plumbing Incident

*My apartment, Vancouver. 3 AM. Water everywhere.*

The plumber arrives at dawn, snakes the camera through pipes. "Roots," he announces. "But weird thing—they're not from nearby trees. These came from distance, seeking your pipes specifically."

I'm exhausted, soaked, laughing. "They chose my apartment?"

"Lady, I've seen roots travel fifty feet through concrete to find water. They're not random. They remember where water was, even decades later."

He doesn't know I study root intelligence. Doesn't know he's confirming our research—roots maintaining maps, sharing information about resource locations across generations.

"My grandmother," he continues, "always said roots know things. Said they carry messages between worlds. Indigenous teaching—everything underground is connected, speaking a language we forgot how to hear."

## Part VIII: The Revelation

*Research plot, dawn. Maya and I reviewing overnight data.*

The sensors detected something unprecedented—synchronized electrical pulses across the entire forest network at 3:47 AM. No trigger we can identify. Just sudden, coordinated activity.

"Like the forest had a thought," Maya says.

I'm about to correct her unscientific language when I notice the pattern. The pulses originated from the oldest trees, spreading through mother trees to offspring, then radiating out in waves. It looks exactly like neural activity in a thinking brain.

"What if consciousness isn't contained in bodies?" Maya asks. "What if it's a property of connection itself? What if heaven—whatever that means—isn't a place but a state of maximum connection?"

I think of my father's sermons about the body of Christ, many parts functioning as one. Think of forests functioning as superorganisms. Think of heaven not as destination but as the space between roots, where individual trees become forest, become ecosystem, become something sacred.

## Part IX: Underground Communion

*Dad visits the research site. First time seeing my work.*

He stands among Douglas firs older than Christianity, hands touching bark like old friends. I show him our data—trees sharing nutrients, warnings, memory.

"This is what you left seminary for?" he asks.

"This is what led me back," I admit. "Different door, same room."

I explain how mother trees nurture offspring through fungal networks. How dying trees dump their resources into the network for others. How forests maintain connection with stumps for decades, keeping them alive for unknown reasons.

"Communion," he says simply. "Body and blood shared through networks older than human speech."

He kneels, places his palm on the earth. "I spent forty years preaching heaven as elsewhere. You're documenting it right here."

"It's just biochemistry," I protest weakly.

"So is human consciousness. Doesn't make it less sacred."

## Part X: The Data and the Divine

*Final research presentation, packed auditorium.*

"Forests demonstrate distributed consciousness through mycorrhizal networks," I state. "Not metaphorically. Literally. Decisions, memory, learning—all documented in our three-year study."

The room tenses. I'm crossing the line from science to philosophy.

"These networks don't just connect trees. They connect species—fungus, bacteria, insects, mammals. All sharing resources, information, perhaps even experience through biological networks we're only beginning to map."

I show the synchronized pulse data. The selective stress responses. The multi-generational memory transfer.

"If consciousness can exist in networks rather than individuals, if forests can think collectively, if roots can carry memory across centuries—then we need new definitions for awareness, for life, for what we consider sacred."

Maya stands in the back, grinning. Dad sits in the front row, weeping.

## Part XI: What the Roots Remember

*Research plot, one year later. New equipment detecting quantum entanglement in fungal networks.*

Maya runs toward me with the data. "The networks aren't just biological. They're operating at quantum levels. Information traveling faster than chemical signals could carry it."

We're documenting something that shouldn't exist—instantaneous communication across forest networks, suggesting quantum entanglement in living systems.

"Heaven and earth connected through networks we can barely perceive," I tell Dad on the phone.

"'As above, so below,'" he quotes. "Maybe the mystics weren't being poetic. Maybe they were being precise."

## Epilogue: The Network Persists

*Five years later. Teaching position at UBC, continuing Simard's work.*

My students think I'm too mystical for a scientist. My father's congregation thinks I'm too scientific for faith. But Maya and I have documented something that transcends both—consciousness as connection, heaven as the space between roots, the sacred dwelling in biological networks beneath our feet.

We've shown that clear-cut forests maintain underground networks for decades, waiting. That mother trees plan for their death, redistributing resources with apparent intention. That forests remember traumas and pass warnings through generations.

The plumber was right—roots know things. They remember where water was, where harm came from, where safety lived. They share this knowledge through networks that operate like neural pathways, like internet cables, like prayer chains.

Maybe heaven was never distant. Maybe it was always here—in the fungal threads connecting all life, in the chemical prayers trees send through soil, in the way forests form collective consciousness through simple connection.

Two figures still sit on that truck hood under stars, discussing heaven and earth. But now we have data. Now we know the conversation continues underground, through networks older than human language, in frequencies we're just learning to hear.

The roots stretch on, carrying messages we're only beginning to decode. And somewhere in that underground gospel, heaven whispers through earth's hidden veins—not above, not beyond, but here, now, in the connections that make consciousness possible.
