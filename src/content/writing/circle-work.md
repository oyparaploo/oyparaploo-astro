June quilts. Every Thursday. Community center. Been doing this eight years.

She's seventy-one. Widowed. Lives in Eugene.

The group is twelve women. Different ages. Different reasons for being there. All quilting.

They work in silence mostly. Sometimes talk. Usually don't.

June's working on a quilt that's been going for three years. Grandmother's batik fabric. Purple and yellow. She's making shapes. Not squares. Curves. Organic shapes. They look like—

"Are those vulvas?" the new woman asks.

Everyone stops.

June keeps stitching. "Maybe."

"They look like vulvas."

"Good."

The new woman doesn't come back next week.

June keeps stitching. The shapes are deliberate. Birth canals. Female anatomy. All made pretty. All made decorative. So people won't notice what they're looking at.

Her daughter says it's too much. June says it's not enough.

Been making quilts like this for years. Her mother taught her. Her grandmother taught her mother. All of them stitching female bodies into fabric. Hiding it in beauty. Smuggling it past defenses.

Her granddaughter visits. Seventeen. Looks at the quilt.

"Grandma, that's a vagina."

"That's a birth canal."

"Same thing."

"Not really."

Her granddaughter laughs. Takes a photo. Posts it. June doesn't care.

Just keeps stitching.

The group doesn't comment on her shapes anymore. They know what she's doing. Some of them do it too. Stitching female power into quilts. Making it pretty enough to hang in living rooms. To pass as decoration.

Maria makes circles. Breast shapes. Ovaries. All hidden in floral patterns. All obvious if you know what to look for.

Linda makes waves. Menstrual cycles. Tides. All red and purple. All beautiful and terrible.

They work. They stitch. They don't explain.

June's granddaughter starts coming to the group. Wants to learn. June teaches her. How to stitch. How to hide. How to make power look like decoration.

"Why hide it?" granddaughter asks.

"Because if it's just power, people reject it. If it's beautiful power, they let it in."

"That's stupid."

"Yeah. But it works."

They stitch together. June's birth canals. Granddaughter's clumsy first attempts. Both valid. Both necessary.

The community center wants to display their work. Annual show. The group votes. June votes no. Gets outvoted.

The show happens. People come. Look at the quilts. Say they're pretty. Don't notice the vulvas. The breasts. The birth canals. The menstrual cycles. The female power stitched into every piece.

One man notices. Art critic. Writes a review. Calls it "troublingly anatomical." Calls it "aggressive femininity." Calls it "beauty weaponized."

The group loves the review. Frames it. Hangs it in the community center.

June keeps stitching. Her quilt is almost done. Three years. Hundreds of hours. All birth canals. All purple and yellow. All her grandmother's fabric turned into female geography.

Her granddaughter finishes her first piece. Small. Just one shape. Uterus. Simple. Beautiful.

"Is this okay?" she asks.

"It's perfect."

"It doesn't look like yours."

"It's not supposed to."

They hang it with the others. A wall of quilts. A wall of female anatomy disguised as decoration. A wall of power smuggled past defenses through beauty.

June's husband used to say quilting was pointless. Waste of time. What's it for? he'd ask.

This. It's for this. For making things that matter look like things that don't. For stitching power into fabric. For teaching young women how to hide what they need to say until they're strong enough to say it out loud.

Her granddaughter asks: "When do we stop hiding?"

"When it's safe."

"When will that be?"

"Don't know. Maybe never."

"That's depressing."

"Yeah. It is."

They keep stitching anyway. Because what else is there to do? Stop? Give up? Pretend female bodies aren't geography worth mapping?

No. They stitch. They hide. They make it beautiful. They smuggle it through.

June finishes her quilt. Three years. Done. Purple and yellow birth canals. Grandmother's fabric. Grandmother's teachings. Grandmother's power stitched into something people will hang on walls and call pretty.

Good.

She starts another one. This time ovaries. This time red.

In Tulsa, Marie quilts alone. No group. Just her.

She's sixty-three. Teacher. Retired.

Makes quilts about nothing. Or so she says. Just patterns. Just colors. Just fabric sewn together.

Her sister visits. Looks at the quilts.

"Are those—"

"No."

"They look like—"

"They're just shapes."

Her sister doesn't push. Just looks. The shapes are obvious though. Female anatomy. All of it. Stitched into patterns. Hidden in beauty.

Marie's been doing this since her daughter was born. Forty years. Hundreds of quilts. All of them maps of female bodies. All of them hanging in people's houses who don't notice what they're looking at.

Her daughter knows. Has always known. Grew up with these quilts. Learned what they meant before she learned their official names. Learned female anatomy through fabric before she learned it through textbooks.

Now her daughter teaches. Shows the quilts to her students. Women's studies. They get it immediately. See the bodies. See the power. See the hiding.

Marie keeps making them. Alone. No group. Just her and her grandmother's teachings and her mother's teachings and her own hands stitching what matters into what people think doesn't.

Her granddaughter asks to learn. Marie teaches her. The same way she was taught. The same way her mother was taught. The same way her grandmother was taught.

How to hide. How to stitch. How to make power beautiful enough to ignore until you're ready to see it.

They work in silence. Stitching. Creating. Making maps of female geography that people will hang on walls and call decorative.

Good.

That's what they're for.

June stitches. Marie stitches. They don't know each other. Never will.

Just keep doing what they're doing.

Making quilts. Making maps. Making power look like decoration.

Teaching daughters. Teaching granddaughters. Teaching the same lessons their grandmothers taught them.

How to hide what matters. How to stitch it anyway. How to make it beautiful enough to survive.

That's about it.

That's what's happening.
