## When grief transforms a botanist's question about death into a revelation that every act of kindness seeds tomorrow's seemingly impossible gardens

### ( Introduction )

In this haunting exploration of grief and transformation, a botanist's midnight question—"Does photosynthesis continue after we die?"—opens a portal into impossible gardens where the laws of physics bend to accommodate love. When hospital roses bloom at the exact moment of her mother's death, our scientist protagonist encounters mysterious figures: a shape-shifting guide, a silent gardener who plants glowing seeds, and a reporter whose breakdown becomes prophecy. Together they discover that every act of human kindness literally seeds eternal gardens growing inside us all. Part scientific inquiry, part fever dream, part love letter to the persistence of beauty, this work transforms a simple question about plant chemistry into a profound meditation on how death might be just another form of photosynthesis—converting grief into light, sorrow into tomorrow's impossible blooms.

### ( The Story )

Listen, let me tell you about this botanist. Grief does weird things to scientists—suddenly all those years of measuring leaf sweat seem pointless when your mother's dead and flowers are acting like they know something you don't. She's up at 4 AM holding this pressed delphinium, and I swear to you, she's looking at it like it might ring. Sometimes she thinks she hears it humming—a low frequency only the bereaved can detect.

The flower's gone translucent. Blue as those horrible hospital gowns, blue as the veins that popped up on her mother's hands near the end. You know the ones.

Does photosynthesis continue after we die?

Yeah. That's where her brain goes. Not normal grief thoughts like "I should've visited more" or "did she know I loved her." No. Plant chemistry in the afterlife.

Here's the thing about the roses, though. Room 347—she'll never forget that number, it's burned in there with her social security and her first heartbreak. When her mother died (11:23 PM, Tuesday, every detail catalogued), the roses all opened. At once. The nurses freaked. One of them got it on video, because that's what we do now—we film miracles and tragedies with equal dedication. #MiracleRoses trending within hours. 47 million strangers weighing in on her mother's death flowers.

"The question grows roots before we notice."

Guy in her kitchen. Just there. And she should be screaming, calling 911, grabbing the knife from the drawer. But he moves like... like he's always been there, you know? Like when you suddenly notice a tree that's been outside your window for twenty years. His age keeps shifting—old man, young man, old again. Not creepy shapeshifter style. More like your eyes can't quite agree on what they're seeing.

"Your mother asked about the roses."

Six words and her world cracks a little more.

Out the window, there's this gardener in the community plot. Middle of the night, planting seeds that glow. Actually glow. He hums to them—old lullabies in languages that might not exist. And nobody talks about him online, which in 2025 is basically impossible. You exist on the internet or you don't exist. This guy has chosen option three. Sometimes neighbors leave him sandwiches by the gate. He never takes them, but the next morning, flowers grow where the plates sat.

---

The reporter's having a night too. She's good at her job—three awards on her desk, one lawsuit settled quietly. Done it all: documented humans at their worst, their most desperate, even covered that ridiculous Kombucha Shortage last year like it was the apocalypse (which for some people, honestly, it kind of was).

But her teleprompter's gone weird. Green static where words should be.

"We're investigating impossible botanical phenomena," she hears herself say, but the words feel dusty. Wrong language for whatever this is.

The gardener—she's got his name somewhere but it seems stupid to use it, like naming the wind—he's making this tree happen. Not growing. Happening. All the seasons at once, which shouldn't work but does. Spring baby leaves next to October fire next to December bones.

Her cameraman, three divorces deep and usually drunk by noon, says: "The lens sees only what believes in endings."

Then he starts crying. Dmitri hasn't cried since his son's funeral. The tears smell faintly of developing fluid and forgotten birthdays.

The gardener pulls an apple out of the dirt. Not from a tree. From. The. Dirt. And it's warm, like it's been sitting in sunshine that hasn't happened yet.

"The sun never sets in the garden inside," he says, and Dmitri just loses it completely.

---

The space around this guide person (because what else do you call someone who walks between here and there like it's nothing?) starts doing things space shouldn't do. The garden isn't changing—it's just showing what it always was. Different neighborhoods of paradise, each with its own rules.

Look: water flowing up. Fruit that regrows as you eat it. A pomegranate that bleeds rubies instead of seeds, and each ruby is something kind someone did when no one was watching. That time you gave your sandwich to the guy outside 7-Eleven. When you forgave your ex even though they didn't ask. When you secretly paid for your coworker's mother's funeral flowers. All of it, crystallized. The rubies taste like tears of relief.

"Love generates particles," the guide says, casual as explaining why the sky's blue.

The botanist wants to fight this with facts, but facts feel suddenly fragile. Like using a tape measure on the ocean.

The reporter drops her camera. Just lets it fall. "I've seen mass graves," she tells the tree. "I've interviewed men who smiled while describing murder. But you. You make me want to write poetry."

She HATES poetry. Said so on a panel once. Called it "escapist bullshit."

---

Then boom—the reporter's somewhere else but also still here. Some kind of protest that hasn't happened yet but also is happening. Signs that grow their own slogans:

"More chlorophyll for the dead!"
"My Grandmother Deserves Eternal Photosynthesis!"

Something breaks in her. "NO!" she screams. "You idiots! The gardens are already here! In your chest! Right now! Every breath is photosynthesis! Every kindness is soil!"

She throws her microphone at the sky. Becomes a bird made of light halfway up. Of course it does. Why wouldn't it?

---

The gardener plants like he's got forever. Each seed gets its own story:

"Morning glory. For the uncle who loved fishing. Died Tuesday. Never saw Wednesday. He used to give his catch to the food bank."

More seeds, more stories.

"Roses that heal. For hands that turned into claws but used to play piano. Chopin nocturnes for the nursing home residents, even when it hurt."

He holds out glowing seeds to the botanist. "Your mother's delphinium."

"She's dead." Flat. Factual. Her only defense against this impossible night.

"Death's just filing. Two folders: gone and staying. Both true."

She plants because what else is there? The seeds are warm. Her mother's last word whispers through them: "Garden."

Was always going to be "garden."

---

Time goes visible. The delphinium grows inward, into her blood. She feels her mother in her cells—not poetic feeling, actual cellular presence.

"Bodies are documents," the guide says. "Different people read them different ways."

The reporter's seeing her whole career differently. Every tragedy she covered is composting into forests, into peace treaties written in plant language. "Wrong hashtags," she mutters. "Been using the wrong damn hashtags."

Her mother's voice then, from nowhere and everywhere: "Photosynthesis doesn't stop, baby. Just changes channels. I'm in the colors you can't see yet."

---

The protest updates itself. New signs:
"Photosynthesis Is How Light Prays"
"The Earth Remembers Forward"  
Just "Yes" in letters that leaf out while you watch

They make a garden together that exists everywhen. Plant sadness, grow joy. Plant rage, grow understanding. Plant fear, get courage. Simple as that. Stupid simple.

"My microphone became a bird," the reporter says, still processing. "My anger turned into seeds."

"Every tradition ends up here," the guide says. "Any heaven worth the name—"

"Must be green," they finish together.

Dawn shows up late to its own party. They hear photosynthesis happening—actually hear it, like whispered legislation or the earth trying to remember its own name. The garden's inside them now. That 4 AM question? Answered by planting instead of thinking.

Nothing planted with love ever really dies. Just changes servers. New platform. Becomes what it was supposed to be all along, algorithm be damned.

Any heaven worth the name must be green. 

File that under "duh." Cross-reference with "should've known." Back it up to the cloud that actually rains.
