## Part One: Winnipeg

Marjorie's loops started the day she found David's reading glasses in the freezer. Not the finding—that was just Thursday—but the loops began right after, when she sat at the kitchen table and her hand started moving. Round and round. She used the blue pen from the pharmacy, the free one.

Helen drove in from Vancouver when the neighbors called. Mrs. Dueck from two doors down, specifically, who'd seen Marjorie outside at midnight in her nightgown, drawing circles in the snow with a stick.

"Mom, you're scaring people."

"I was just checking if it worked outside too."

"If what worked?"

But Marjorie was already drawing again. The kitchen table—scorched on one corner from that Christmas when David tried to flambé something—became her territory. She drew while Helen talked about grief counselors, about normal responses, about that widow in her book club who took up salsa dancing.

The loops weren't pretty. Some angry and dark where the pen dug in. Others barely there, ghost circles. Each one different, though you had to look close to tell. Like February days in Winnipeg. Like arguing about the thermostat for thirty-seven years.

David never closed a cabinet door in his life. Not once. Now Marjorie left them all open—the kitchen looked like it was having an argument with itself. But it wasn't the same. Nothing was the same, especially the things she tried to keep the same.

Dr. Chen, who was younger than Helen and wore sneakers with her dress pants, said trauma manifests differently in everyone. She said manifests like it was groceries appearing on the counter.

"My trauma isn't manifesting," Marjorie told her. "I'm making a record."

"Of what?"

"Of continuing."

The doctor wrote something down. Probably that word: continuing.

Helen made Earl Grey. They used to drink coffee, she and David, at 6:15 every morning. Now coffee made her feel like she'd swallowed a handful of pennies.

Three weeks in, Marjorie noticed her middle finger had developed a bump, shiny and hard. Her body keeping its own record.

## Part Two: Oaxaca

The thing about Oaxaca was the bells. They rang for everything—mass, death, celebration, Tuesday. Marjorie rented a place with one window, a hot plate, and a toilet that needed encouragement.

She'd told Helen two weeks. That was in March.

Now it was July and she drew at Café Nuevo, where Luis brought her fizzy water without asking. He'd stopped trying to make conversation after she'd drawn him a picture: circles turning into spirals turning into something that might have been a bird or might have been a scream.

"You're that Canadian woman who draws," an American said one morning. Retired teacher, probably. They all had that same sun damage and turquoise jewelry.

"I make loops."

"Is it therapy?"

Marjorie wanted to say: It's what happens instead of disappearing. But she said: "It's just something my hand does."

The patterns had color now. She'd found a night market vendor selling knockoff markers that bled into each other. Purple into green into a yellow that reminded her of something she couldn't name. A bruise, maybe. Or the sky before tornado weather in Brandon, where she'd grown up.

Helen called on Sundays, Winnipeg time.

"The Duecks ask about you."

"Tell them I'm dead."

"Mom."

"Tell them I've gone feral."

Through the bad connection, Helen's laugh turned into crying, which turned into that hiccup thing she'd done as a baby.

"Dad would hate this," Helen said. "You in Mexico, alone."

"Oaxaca isn't Mexico. It's Oaxaca."

"He'd say you're being dramatic."

"He'd be wrong." Marjorie held up her latest drawing to the screen—thousands of loops now, some overlapping, some strangling others, some so faint they were just the memory of movement. "I'm being accurate."

Helen went quiet. Then: "It looks like that thing you used to doodle. During phone calls."

She was right. Marjorie had been making these marks her whole life, just never noticed. On grocery lists. During staff meetings. While David explained why they couldn't afford the thing they couldn't afford.

"Mom, are you okay?"

Such a strange question. Okay. Like a circle that doesn't quite close.

"I'm here," Marjorie said. Which wasn't an answer but was.

That night, there was a parade for some saint nobody could name. Brass band playing something that sounded like a dish soap commercial slowed down to a dirge, then sped up again. From her window, Marjorie watched couples dancing, the same six steps over and over, but each repetition slightly off, slightly new. 

She drew in the dark, not looking at the paper. Her hand knew the motion now. Had always known it, maybe. The way bodies know how to scar, how to continue past the point where continuing seems unreasonable.

When morning came, she saw what she'd made: hundreds of white loops on white paper, invisible except where the pressure had embossed the surface. A secret pattern. Evidence of a hand moving through darkness.

She laughed. Actually laughed. Not because it was funny but because it was so exactly what survival looked like—invisible work, leaving its impression only if you held it to the light just right.

---

*Two years later, going through her mother's things, Helen would find a suitcase full of these drawings. Thousands of pages. Some just blue pharmacy-pen loops. Others violent with color. A few completely blank except for the indentations of a hand pressing down, around and around. She'd frame one—not the prettiest, but the one from the day her mother had called to say she was staying another month, then another. The one where the loops finally looked less like worry and more like breathing. She'd hang it in her kitchen in Vancouver, where morning light would catch the indentations. Sometimes, making coffee, she'd touch the raised marks with her finger, following the circles. Round and round. Her hand learning the motion.*
