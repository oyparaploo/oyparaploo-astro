## Reading the Room's Language

The code-switching question torments speakers everywhere—thinking they need to pick a lane when really it's about reading the room and matching the moment. Too formal in casual settings alienates. Too casual in formal settings undermines credibility. The gallery opening disaster: artist arrives talking like they're at a dive bar, or worse, defending a dissertation. Both miss the mark.

What does this moment need? Not what makes you comfortable, but what serves the situation. Boardroom presentations about funding need professional voice—not to impress but because that language signals you understand their world. Speaking their native tongue shows you can operate in their ecosystem. It's practical, not pretentious.

## Passport and Protection

Professional language serves as passport into certain rooms. Legal situations, medical consultations, grant applications, academic defenses—some spaces have professional language as entry fee. You literally won't be heard without it. The credibility threshold demands you prove fluency before they'll consider your ideas.

But that same professional voice in someone's living room artist talk creates walls. Intimate spaces want intimacy. Professional language there feels like armor—protecting yourself from real connection. Once your passport gets you in, you might need to put it away.

## The Bridge Voice

Mixed audiences—collectors ranging from corporate CEOs to young artists—demand multilingual conversation. Start professional to establish credibility, then warm up, add personal touches, create bridges. Professional enough to be trusted, human enough to be liked.

Signal your shifts: "Let me put this in plain terms..." or "The technical way to say this is..." Making code-switching visible feels intentional, not manipulative. "Here's the industry term, but what it really means is..." Now you're teaching, not excluding.

## Power to Not Switch

The most powerful people break these rules—casual in boardrooms, human in formal settings. They've proven themselves, accumulated enough capital that casualness becomes power. The CEO saying "This shit doesn't work" in board meetings. The famous artist wearing sneakers to galas. They've earned the right to be themselves everywhere.

But emerging, unknown, trying to establish yourself? You might need professional voice to get in the door, then slowly reveal real voice as you gain standing. Sometimes it's survival—you need resources, opportunities, platforms. Speaking their language gets you those.

## When Common Language Wins

Teaching demands accessible language. The best teachers translate professional concepts into regular speech because the goal is understanding, not impressing. If professional language creates learning barriers, you're failing as a teacher.

Creative spaces often die when someone goes formal—like dumping ice water on fire. In creative collaboration, common language becomes more professional because it serves the work better. An artist who can't talk regularly about their work might actually be bad at their job—part of which is connection, accessibility, humanity.

## The Jacket Metaphor

Professional voice is like a jacket—sometimes needed, sometimes not, sometimes you start with it and remove it. Watch speakers do this beautifully: formal introduction gradually becoming more themselves, like watching someone remove outerwear as they warm up.

Strategic shifts create power. Sudden formality makes people lean in. Sudden casualness in formal moments—"Let me just be real with you for a second"—creates actual connection. Pattern breaks signal "this matters."

## Multilingual in Your Own Language

The answer is fluidity—moving between professional and common as needed. Not choosing one identity but having range. Both voices can be genuine. Professional you and regular you are both really you, just dressed for different occasions, speaking for different purposes, serving different needs.

But always, hopefully, serving.
