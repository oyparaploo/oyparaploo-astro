## Part I: The Authority of Ash

I'm cataloging disappeared persons in post-collapse Manila when Esther shows me the last mango tree growing through her childhood home's ruins, and I realize I've been documenting the wrong extinction—not species or cities, but the bureaucratic erasure of human memory itself.

My credentials: Seven years working for the UN's Identity Recovery Project, restoring citizenship to those whose records vanished in climate disasters. A mother whose immigration papers burned with California, making her legally non-existent. Three thousand interviews with people searching for proof they ever existed.

The paradox: We created systems to preserve identity, but those systems became the machinery of forgetting.

## Part II: The Vendor's Prophecy

*Memory: Manila, 2019, before the floods.*

The mango vendor shouted every morning: "Mangoes! Defiant mangoes! The fruit of revolutions!"

My translator laughed. "He's saying they grow despite the pollution, despite the heat. Defiant against extinction."

I bought three, filmed him for my documentary on climate adaptation. He looked directly at camera: "When bureaucrats decide you don't exist, become like mangoes—grow anyway."

Four years later, his stall location is underwater. His name appears in no records. But Esther remembers him.

## Part III: Esther's Excavation

*Present day, Manila ruins.*

"I CAN'T LOSE ANYTHING ELSE!"

Esther tears through ash and debris, searching for her mother's photograph—the only proof before bureaucrats declared her mother "statistically irrelevant" after losing her papers in Typhoon Bhola.

The old man appears—robes dusty, smell of sandalwood cutting through smoke. He's been following my documentation team, appearing wherever identity dissolves.

"The world turned you into ruins," he tells Esther, "but in ruins, seeds grow."

She laughs bitterly. Points to where mangoes still grow through collapsed concrete. "My father called the vendor an anarchist of agriculture. Now we're all anarchists of existence."

## Part IV: The Bureaucratic Machine

*Government Records Office, temporary structure.*

I interview the clerk processing "non-existence claims"—people trying to prove they exist after losing documentation.

"Thirty thousand applications this month," she says, stamping PENDING on another form. "Without papers, they're ghosts."

The system works backwards: you must prove you existed to get papers proving you exist. Kafka would weep at the elegance.

Behind her, servers hum, containing millions of digitized records. One power surge, one flood, and identities vanish forever.

## Part V: When Streets Grew Limbs

*Documentary footage, six months ago.*

The collapse began absurdly. Streets literally growing limbs, lampposts shaking hands with dogs, traffic signals whispering philosophy. My camera captured it all—reality breaking down not dramatically but surreally.

The mayor declared it "temporary administrative glitch" while rivers ran backward and citizens woke with memories erased.

I thought it was mass hallucination until I checked my own records: my birthdate had changed three times in government databases. My mother's name was now listed as "N/A."

## Part VI: The Mango Resistance

*Esther's rebuilt shelter, present.*

She's planted mangoes in the ash—not for fruit but for defiance. Each tree a middle finger to systems that erased her mother.

"The vendor was right," she says. "When they delete you from records, you grow anyway. Become undeniable."

She shows me her project: collecting photographs from ruins, creating analog archives. No servers, no systems, just physical proof of existence.

"They control digital memory," she explains. "But they can't delete what's buried in dirt."

## Part VII: The Prophet's Archive

*The old man's hideout, abandoned mall.*

He's been collecting too—not photos but objects. Thousands of items labeled with names of vanished people: Maria's comb, Jose's wallet, Chen's sandal.

"Objects remember," he insists. "Even when systems forget."

He shows me the pattern: every climate disaster followed by record loss, followed by identity erasure, followed by land seizure. The forgetting is intentional.

"First they take your papers, then your proof, then your place," he says. "But mangoes keep growing."

## Part VIII: The Ice Shelf Incident

*News footage, last month.*

Ice shelves appeared in impossible places—on gas stations, dangling from bridges, sliding down highways. The government called it "atmospheric anomaly," but I recognized the pattern: reality protesting its own collapse.

Esther was there, photographing. "Nature's showing us what we look like to it—absurd, out of place, insisting on existing where we shouldn't."

The ice melted in hours, but photos remain. Proof of impossible things. Like people existing without papers.

## Part IX: Digital Archaeology

*Tech volunteer, data recovery tent.*

Marcus, a hacker turned humanitarian, tries recovering erased identities from damaged servers.

"The data's there," he explains, "just marked for deletion. Like the system wants to forget but can't quite manage it."

He's recovered twelve thousand profiles—people the system declared non-existent who are very much alive. Each recovery is small revolution.

"We're not saving data," he says. "We're proving ghosts are real."

## Part X: The Coral Registry

*Underwater Manila, diving expedition.*

Where neighborhoods drowned, coral grows through living rooms. I'm filming for the Climate Memory Project when I find it: a filing cabinet, preserved in an air pocket, containing birth certificates.

The papers are intact, impossible but true. Proof of three hundred people the system says never existed.

The coral grows through the files, incorporating them into its structure. Nature creating its own archive.

## Part XI: Mother's Non-Existence

*Personal thread surfaces.*

I finally tell Esther about my mother—how she exists in my memory but not in any system. How I carry her photo knowing it proves nothing legally.

"That's why you document," Esther says. It's not accusation but recognition.

"I'm collecting ghosts," I admit. "Hoping if I gather enough, they become undeniable."

She hands me a mango from her tree. "My mother loved these. Whether the system remembers her or not, the mangoes do."

## Part XII: The Accumulation

*Final scene: Mass gathering, Manila Bay.*

Thousands gather, each carrying objects of erased people. Esther brings her mother's photo. The prophet brings his archive. Marcus brings recovered data printed on paper.

We're not protesting. We're existing. Publicly. Undeniably.

The mayor arrives, says we need permits to gather. Someone shouts: "We don't exist, remember? Ghosts don't need permits."

The crowd laughs. Plants mangoes in the reclaimed land. Each tree a person the system forgot.

## Epilogue: Defiant Growth

*One year later.*

The mango grove thrives where the gathering was. The government calls it "unauthorized agriculture." We call it the Archive of the Erased.

Esther leads tours now, telling each tree's story: "This one is Mrs. Santos, disappeared from records after Hurricane Paolo. This is the Chen family, five generations erased when servers flooded."

The old prophet tends the trees, still insisting objects remember what systems forget.

I'm still filming, but differently now. Not documenting loss but defiance. Not recording extinction but insistence on growing anyway.

A child asks why we plant mangoes for dead people.

"They're not dead," Esther explains. "They're just bureaucratically invisible. The mangoes make them real again."

The vendor was right: when declared non-existent, grow anyway. Be defiant fruit in impossible soil. Refuse the machinery of forgetting.

The systems still delete. The climate still collapses. But the mangoes keep growing through ruins, proving what bureaucracy denies—we existed, we exist, we insist on existing.
