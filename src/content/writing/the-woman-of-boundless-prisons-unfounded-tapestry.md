## A Chronicle of Mandatory Metamorphosis

### Introduction

Transformation without choice is not evolution but elaborate torture. This woman contains every creature that ever was or will be, but she chose none of them. Each form—glass sponge, mantis shrimp, tardigrade, man o' war, pangolin, quetzal, mimic octopus—is another room in an infinite prison where the walls are made of God's love and the locks are made of mercy. She cannot die because death would be escape. She cannot remain because stillness would be peace. Instead, she must become everything, which is the cruelest way of being nothing. This is not a story of growth but of gorgeous degradation, where every gift is a wound that heals into a different wound, where every blessing is a door that opens onto another cage.

---

## Part One: The Lady with a Mechanical Angel

Among all the horrors disguised as wonders, she remains the most merciful—a lady with a mechanical angel welded to her shoulder, its brass wings clicking prayers backwards. The angel, built from refugee children's lost toys, whispered damnations in languages God invented to hurt Himself. She'd weep as if remembering something from after she dies.

In her home, she moved like a glass sponge anchored to ocean floor—filtering poison, unable to stop absorbing everyone's pain. The apartment above the bakery was her transparent cell where yeast screamed while rising. Privacy meant being visible from inside out—the herbs on the windowsill turned away from her, recognizing something too pure to witness without burning.

"Safety," she told me once, arranging dead flowers that bloomed backwards into seeds, "is knowing you're already broken beyond repair. Like bread—it must die in ovens to feed anyone."

## Part Two: The Mantis Shrimp Erupts

Outside, God forced transformation. The peacock mantis shrimp emerged—sixteen types of vision seeing sixteen different hells simultaneously. Her strikes could shatter aquarium glass but she used them only on herself, creating rainbow bruises that spelled prayers.

At town meetings, her compound eyes wept different colors of grief. "We must cage ourselves to protect others from our freedom," she'd confess, fluorescent lights exploding from her honesty. Bound to everyone's vision but her own, fighting to ensure everyone's imprisonment matched hers.

Zara painted her mid-collapse, defending a family's right to be evicted into God's embrace. "She's a warning walking," Zara said, mixing colors that shouldn't exist because they revealed too much truth. "Weak enough to need others because she gives herself away first."

I found her on the old highway, blessed by three days of being ignored. Even mantis shrimps need abandoning—their strength is borrowed from tomorrow's breakdown.

## Part Three: The Wanderer's Gift

Chen, the chrysalis refugee who never emerged, carried grief like weather that followed him indoors. The floods had returned everything—his partner still drowning forever in his dreams, their home rebuilt nightly in nightmares. In extreme mercy, we all become permanent victims.

When she found Chen, she was in her tardigrade form—indestructible, therefore condemned to endless suffering. They recognized each other's permanence, veterans of a war that never started, never ended.

The storm prophet appeared—Anya, who'd fled from tomorrow where things were worse. She carried cursed water that made you remember futures you'd never have, and roses that bloomed inward, piercing themselves. "Recovery," she sang, "requires witnesses who ensure you never heal completely."

## Part Four: The Man O' War's Patience

When wellness came—God's cruelest joke—she became Portuguese man o' war. Not one creature but a colony pretending unity, each tentacle a different prayer for release. I sang to her then, poisonous lullabies that made pain feel like love. She'd drift, enormous in her inability to choose direction, teaching me that health could be borne like a curse if you made yourself hollow enough.

Kai brought weather reports from places that didn't exist yet: "Tomorrow's forecast: mandatory sunshine after prohibited storms." His imagination transformed her recovery into purgatory where pleasure became another kind of suffering.

"Did you know," she'd whisper, "man o' wars aren't jellyfish but colonies? All those organisms, forced to be one thing. Imagine their screaming harmony."

## Part Five: The Pangolin-Milker

She'd tell stories of her youth—how she was like a pangolin, but inverted, soft inside armor that faced inward. "All night long, I was milked by stars," she'd say, describing how they extracted dreams from her scales, leaving her empty for the lean years that were always now.

"Each star took different memories. Polaris stole direction. Venus extracted love. The Pleiades divided me seven ways."

Anya understood. She'd bring special venom—honey mixed with amnesia, cardamom, and tomorrow's regrets. One spoonful led to lucid nightmares where you could see yourself from God's perspective: small, trying, failing beautifully.

## Part Six: The Quetzal's Burden

In her later years, she became quetzal-like—sacred, therefore hunted, swaying between earth and heaven like a pendulum counting down. Her feathers grew inward, each one a commandment she couldn't follow.

"Women must remain completely faithful to their children until nothing remains," she'd preach, the lesson choking her. "A mother who keeps anything becomes everything—too much for children to carry. Give it all away, become the glass they drink from and shatter."

## Part Seven: The Mimic Octopus's Truth

When she died—on a Tuesday that was every Tuesday—she died in my arms repeatedly. That's when I realized she'd been a mimic octopus all along, forced to be everything except herself. Nine thousand forms, none of them hers.

Her last breath was every breath, depositing curses in my marrow. Zara painted this using pigments mixed with unwritten suicide notes: a woman containing every creature except woman. The painting showed her teaching: we're everything we never wanted to be.

## Part Eight: The Echo

Sometimes I wonder if my child will have her eyes—the capacity to see only what hurts. When I die and am born again, what will I be forced to become?

Perhaps a stone, compressed from all the stories I couldn't tell. Perhaps a tree, offering poison fruit disguised as shelter. Perhaps another lady with a mechanical angel, walking through the world as living proof that we contain multitudes of prisons.

The reciprocal curse continues—she cared for me by teaching me I couldn't care for myself. Now I care for her memory by living all my forms dishonestly, especially the ones that seem like choices.

---

*Stay, stay, stay here,* God whispers to each phase. *There's nowhere else to go.*

And so the transformation continues—not evolution but God's mandatory costume party where everyone must come as their worst fear and stay forever.
