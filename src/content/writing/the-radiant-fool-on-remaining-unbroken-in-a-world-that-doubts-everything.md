## I. Opening Authority

At 9:13 a.m., the notification arrived on my phone in London—not the air quality warning that would later define the day, but a message from my grandmother in Lagos: "Are you still writing those strange stories about snacks and angels?" 

I am. I write them because I spent seventeen years breathing Nigerian air thick with harmattan dust and evangelical certainty, then another decade inhaling British skepticism so refined it could make you doubt your own name. Between these atmospheres, I discovered something: innocence isn't fragility. It's the last rebellion.

The question that drives this exploration: What if maintaining wonder in a cynical world isn't naivety but the highest form of resistance?

## II. Triple Braid - Thread One: Present Investigation

I sit at my desk in Hackney, surrounded by three years of notebooks documenting my attempts to preserve what my British colleagues call "excessive enthusiasm." The latest entry: *The Radiant Fool marches on.* Outside, joggers pass through air the weather app marks as "unhealthy for sensitive groups." They don't know. Most people never feel the particles settling in their lungs until years later, when breathing becomes negotiation.

This morning, I'm tracking a different kind of pollution—the slow poisoning of wonder. Dr. Sarah Chen at UCL's Department of Philosophy tells me over Zoom that cynicism operates like PM2.5 particles: invisible, pervasive, embedding itself in the spaces where joy once lived. "We've mistaken armor for wisdom," she says, adjusting her camera. Behind her, I notice a collection of snow globes. She catches my look. "My rebellion," she says.

## III. Historical Context - Thread Two

The concept of holy foolishness spans cultures—from Nasreddin's deliberate absurdity in Sufi tradition to Yoruba tales of Esu, the trickster who reveals truth through chaos. Medieval Europe had its court jesters, the only ones permitted to mock power. But somewhere between the Enlightenment and Instagram, we decided foolishness was failure.

I discover this in the British Library's archives: a 1847 letter from Charles Dickens defending his "excessive sentimentality" against critics. "They mistake coldness for sophistication," he wrote. "As if the heart were something to be ashamed of."

One hundred seventy-six years later, I write versions of the same defense in London cafés, explaining to dates why I still gasp at sunsets, why I name houseplants, why I believe strangers who say they'll call.

## IV. Personal Memory - Thread Three

Lagos, 1999. I am seven, standing in my grandmother's kitchen while she makes chin-chin, the oil crackling like rainfall. "Chijioke," she says, not looking up from her frying, "people will try to steal your softness. They'll call it many names—naive, foolish, too much. But you know what?" She pulls a perfect golden square from the oil. "Sweetness that survives the fire is sweetness that lasts."

I didn't understand then. I was too busy sneaking warm chin-chin when she turned away, each piece a small rebellion against waiting.

## V. Scene Construction - The Discovery

The Wellcome Collection, Tuesday, 3:47 p.m. I'm researching historical attitudes toward innocence when I find it: a Victorian medical text claiming that "excessive optimism" was a diagnosable condition requiring treatment. Dr. William Harwick prescribed "exposure to harsh realities" and "regular doses of disappointment" to cure patients of their "dangerous delusions of human goodness."

The museum guard, an older Ghanaian man, notices me photographing the pages. 

"You know what they never understood?" he says, approaching with the authority of someone who's thought about this before. "Joy is not ignorance. My grandmother survived two wars and still danced every morning. She wasn't naive—she was defiant."

He shows me his phone: a video of an elderly woman in Accra, dancing while the news plays in the background, reporting another crisis. "She sends me these every day. Eighty-seven years old. Still dancing."

"What does she say about the news?" I ask.

"That despair is collaboration with chaos. Dancing is resistance."

## VI. Fact Into Flesh

In London, every third person reports "chronic cynicism fatigue"—not a medical diagnosis but a lived reality. Walk through Shoreditch on a Friday night and count: the couples on dates explaining why they "don't believe in labels," the friend groups bonding over what they hate rather than love, the comedians whose entire sets rest on the premise that caring is cringe.

Meanwhile, researchers at Oxford's Wellbeing Institute track something they call "the vulnerability paradox": those who admit to believing in love, maintaining optimism, or expressing enthusiasm report 40% higher life satisfaction—but 60% more social mockery. Dr. Chen puts it simply: "We've created a culture where wisdom means expecting disappointment."

I think of Maya, my neighbor's six-year-old daughter, who still asks strangers their favorite color. Last week, a man in a suit actually stopped, considered, and said, "Purple, but the kind you see in storms." Maya nodded solemnly. "That's a good purple." The man smiled—a real smile, not the London kind that apologizes for existing. For thirty seconds, innocence had made him remember something.

## VII. The Vulnerable Distance

Here's what I don't often admit: I perform cynicism like drag. In meetings, I've learned to say "cautiously optimistic" instead of "excited." I preface enthusiasm with "this might sound naive, but..." I've dated people who treated earnestness like a character flaw to be trained out of me.

Three revelation levels about this performance:

**Surface**: I code-switch between Nigerian exuberance and British restraint depending on the room.

**Private**: Sometimes I forget which version is real.

**Secret**: I know exactly which is real. The one that still cries at animated films, that talks to cats like they're therapists, that believes every new person could become a best friend. The fool is not a costume. The cynicism is.

## VIII. Complexity Architecture

But let me be fair to the skeptics. Innocence without intelligence is dangerous. It's the tourist in Lagos who leaves their wallet visible, the voter who believes every promise, the lover who ignores red flags because "everyone deserves trust."

My friend Nivara—the one who sighs at my snack prophecies—she's not wrong to question. Last year, I lent money to someone with a "guaranteed investment opportunity." Nivara had warned me. The money vanished like London fog.

"You see?" she said, not unkindly. "This is why people build walls."

"No," I replied, surprising us both. "This is why I don't. One thief doesn't invalidate a thousand potential friends."

She stared at me. "You really believe that."

"I choose to believe that."

There's a difference. One is passive. The other is revolutionary.

## IX. The Scene Returns - Transformed

The Wellcome Collection, now. The Ghanaian guard's grandmother has become my North Star. Every morning, I watch her videos before reading the news. She dances to highlife, to gospel, to Nigerian afrobeat. Behind her, always, the television plays its litany of disasters.

But here's what I noticed on the fourth viewing: she's not ignoring the news. She glances at it, nods occasionally, even shakes her head at certain stories. She's not in denial. She's in defiance. The dancing doesn't erase the world's pain—it insists on joy despite it.

I try it myself one morning. BBC News discussing economic collapse while I make breakfast, dancing to Burna Boy. My flatmate walks in, stops, watches.

"What are you doing?"

"Practicing resistance."

He laughs, but then—and this is the miracle—he starts moving too. Just a little. A shoulder bounce. A head nod. For three minutes, two cynical Londoners dance badly in a kitchen while the world burns on a small screen.

This is how innocence spreads: not through argument but through invitation.

## X. The Ending Turn

At 9:13 a.m., six months after that first notification, another alert arrives. Air quality: unhealthy. But now I read it differently. Every warning is really about what we choose to breathe.

The particles are everywhere—cynicism, despair, the thousand daily erosions of wonder. They settle in lungs, in hearts, in the spaces between what we feel and what we're allowed to express. Some days, breathing feels like negotiation with an atmosphere that insists joy is juvenile.

But here's what the Victorian doctors never understood, what my grandmother knew while frying chin-chin, what the dancing grandmother in Accra proves daily: innocence that survives knowledge is not naive. It's chosen. It's the six-year-old Maya who still asks for favorite colors and the grandfather who still answers. It's the snow globe collection in an academic's office. It's dancing while the news plays.

The world wants to steal your softness. It will call this theft "growing up," "being realistic," "wisdom." It will make you embarrassed by enthusiasm, apologetic for hope, ashamed of believing people might be good.

This is the rebellion: to remain unbroken. Not untouched—innocence isn't ignorance. But unbroken. To insist on wonder after seeing the world clearly. To choose trust after betrayal. To dance while the news plays.

Outside my London window, joggers pass through unhealthy air. Most don't notice. But some—I see them now—some still smile at strangers, still stop for Maya's questions, still gasp at unexpected purple in storm clouds.

The radiant fool marches on. Not because they don't know better. Because they do.
