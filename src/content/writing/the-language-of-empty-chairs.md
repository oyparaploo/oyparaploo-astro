In the square where pigeons once gossiped over breadcrumbs, empty chairs began appearing. First one, then three, then dozens—each bearing a photograph weighted down with stones. The authorities removed them nightly. By morning, new ones had taken their place.

An old woman, buying tomatoes at the market, explained it to a tourist who'd asked: "Every chair is someone who spoke when speaking became dangerous." She selected her vegetables carefully, as if choosing words had taught her to weigh everything. "My daughter sets them out. Four in the morning, before the patrols. She says furniture can't be arrested, though they try."

The mathematics teacher writes equations on sidewalks now—not formulas for velocity or mass, but simple arithmetic: "2,847 days since the journalist disappeared. 1,623 since the union leader fell down stairs that left no bruises. 967 since elections meant anything." Rain washes the chalk away. She returns with fresh boxes, bought with her pension, her handwriting growing smaller as the numbers grow larger.

In the cafés, they've learned to speak in recipes. "Too much salt," means another arrest. "Needs more time in the oven," means wait, not yet, be patient. A baker posts instructions for bread that will never rise properly—the proportions deliberately wrong, but everyone understands the ingredient that's missing can't be bought in shops. It must be taken back.

A child asks her grandfather why the radio only plays the same three songs now. He shows her how to fold paper birds, hundreds of them, until their apartment rustles with wings that will never fly. "Some things," he says, "we make not because they work, but because our hands refuse to forget the motions of creating." Each crane carries a name on its wing—people who once filled the empty chairs.

There's a woman who waters dead plants in the abandoned opposition headquarters, maintaining something that no longer exists except in her daily ritual of care. A man who calls disconnected phone numbers, leaving messages for friends who'll never retrieve them. The diplomat's widow keeps two sets of china—one for guests who repeat the television's words, another for those who remember different channels once existed. Just a chip here, a faded pattern there. But those who eat from the imperfect plates know they're among friends.

In the suburbs, book clubs still meet to discuss novels that don't exist—elaborate plots invented to disguise which stories are really being told. Children learn history through bedtime tales of "the before times," when people could gather without permits, when singing wasn't suspicious, when disagreement didn't require courage.

Some mornings, walking to buy bread, you might see them: the teacher with her chalk, the daughter with her chairs, the grandfather folding his thousandth crane. They nod, slight as breath, recognition passing between those who've chosen the exhausting work of not forgetting. The grandmother still bakes extra bread, though sharing becomes suspicious. The mechanic fixes cars for those whose licenses were revoked for thinking. Each small act insists: we continue to be human when humanity becomes dangerous.

The chairs multiply. The chalk equations grow longer. The paper birds pile higher. Not because anyone believes furniture, mathematics, or origami will restore what's lost, but because the act of placing, writing, folding is itself the statement: We are still here. We remember.

And in this quiet insistence lives the truth that terrifies: silence isn't consent but breath being gathered. Even the emptiest chair holds the shape of the person who will, one morning soon or distant, return to fill it.

The old woman at the market sets down her tomatoes, studies the empty square. "My daughter," she says to no one in particular, "believes furniture can't be arrested." She smiles slightly, the way people do when holding two truths at once—the weight of what's lost, the lightness of what refuses to surrender. "So far, she's right."
