Dawn scraped pale light across the Grosses Walsertal, weak but unafraid. The valley breathed again: owls calling, brooks resuming their small quarrels, the clap of a farmer’s gate carrying down slope. The night’s engineered silence had been broken, but the residue of it clung like soot on lungs.

Troll Tearsen sat on the low wall outside Helene Klausmun’s house, palms open, letting the marbles rest there. Clear, red, cracked, blue, white-coil, seeded. They looked tired—like objects that had been forced to perform too long.

Libaux Torrini came from the barn, brushing hay from his coat. “The dignitaries left before dawn. No arrests, no apologies. Just cars and the smell of leather seats.”

“They’ll tell themselves it was a technical failure,” Troll said. “Something bureaucratic. They’ll make it clean again.”

Libaux lowered himself beside her. “And Stremler?”

She shook her head. “He vanished when the lights did. His aides scattered. He’ll surface. Men like him always do. Silence is patient.”

Inside, Helene had spread reports across her kitchen table. Officers spoke in low tones, their pens scratching as though to convince paper the night had happened. Aïcha’s messages kept arriving, each one a fragment:
*Media framing it as ‘incident.’ Rivage denying involvement. Uma furious.*

And then, finally, from **Uma Arouje** herself:
*Your interference has destabilized negotiations. You’ve made enemies we cannot afford. You’ve also made allies who will not speak their names. I cannot protect you further. Leave Vienna. Leave Paris. Lie low, or lie buried.*

Troll read it twice. “She’s washing her hands.”

“No,” Libaux said. “She’s telling us to disappear so she doesn’t have to bury us herself.”

Helene’s officer cleared his throat. “What happens now?”

Troll closed her fist around the marbles, feeling their cool insistence. “Now the valley remembers. That’s enough for tonight. Tomorrow we follow him south. If Rivage has more braces, more grooves, they’ll be waiting in Collioure, in Banyuls, maybe even further.”

Helene nodded, eyes softening. “Come back when you can. The chapel will keep its silence for you.”

That night Troll finally opened Miyake Coscia’s last message fully, not just the transcript. The recording filled the small bedroom with Miyake’s calm, stubborn voice:

*“Some rooms are forced quiet. Others choose to remember. You don’t have to fix them all. Just keep one true, and the others will follow.”*

Troll pressed the cracked marble to her ear, as if it could carry that voice longer. Outside, the brook rehearsed its ordinary music, awkward and beautiful.

She whispered her mother’s name into the pillow. This time she didn’t wait for echo. She let it belong to her alone.
