Morning came like a curtain reluctantly pulled—grey light sneaking in through the Viennese fog. The conference quarter stirred with the exact noises bureaucracy allows itself: car doors shutting in practiced rhythm, polished shoes scuffing marble with restraint, translators’ bags rolling soft wheels across carpet.

Troll Tearsen and Libaux Torrini carried no bags, only their pockets heavy with marbles. They moved with the current of delegates until they peeled away toward the service entrance of the plenary hall.

“Uma said between the lines,” Libaux murmured. “We’re about to write new ones.”

Troll’s jaw tightened. “Then we don’t erase. We overwrite.”

The hall had been retuned overnight. Troll heard it before she saw it: the air hummed smoother, like someone had ironed the very oxygen. Stremler was already present on the dais, conferring with aides, his hands folded as if in perpetual rehearsal.

The building purred obedience. Every cough arrived muffled, every scrape of chair absorbed like a sin forgiven before confession.

Troll and Libaux slipped into a side corridor. Behind a door marked *Technik*, a lattice of cables and racks hummed in patient chorus. The smug little repeaters blinked, feeding their curated hush. A console screen traced clean green waveforms, smugly stable.

Libaux crouched. “We cut?”

“No,” Troll said. “We play.”

She set her recorder on the desk, scrolling through its files until she found the cough track. She layered it against a low hum she had captured in Collioure—the tide’s breath pressed into digital memory. The mix sounded absurd, like a crowd exhaling on a shore. Perfect.

She patched it into the auxiliary send labeled *ROOM TONE.* The repeaters accepted the new feed with unthinking faith.

On stage, Stremler leaned into his microphone. “Transparency is not spectacle,” he said, warm, grave. “It is the refusal of spectacle.”

The hall accepted the cadence, leaning with him. Then, softly at first, the coughs began. Not disruptive—polite, human. The tide followed underneath, an undertone no architect had designed for.

Delegates shifted in their chairs. Stremler paused half a second too long, waiting for the hall to resume its obedience. The coughs did not cease. A wave broke somewhere deep in the floor—just enough to be heard, not enough to be denied.

Libaux grinned. “We’ve salted his instrument.”

The liaison appeared in the corridor, smooth as ever, smile too practiced for this early hour. “You shouldn’t be here.”

Troll held up a marble—the cracked one from the archive. “Neither should this,” she said, and rolled it across the console.

**tik—tik—tik.**

The contact mics picked it up, rebroadcast it through the hidden transducers. The hall received the sound like a memory it had tried to forget. Delegates turned their heads, eyes searching for the source.

Stremler’s voice tightened. “…we are not in the business of anecdotes.”

The marble’s knock returned in echo. **tik—tik.**

Troll spoke into the talkback mic, just above a whisper. Her mother’s name. It fed through the floor vents, uninvited, soft as breath. The hall stilled, caught between the weight of silence and the presence of something more human than oversight.

Stremler did not flinch. His calm was sculpted for such moments. But Troll saw the line at his jaw—a fraction, a crack where sound had found purchase.

Uma entered behind them, sharp as a verdict. She took in the scene—console, marbles, liaison’s stiff posture, Troll’s hand on the mic.

“This,” she said, “is beyond sanction.”

“No,” Troll answered. “This is dissonance. And dissonance is the only truth left.”

For a moment, Uma’s mask faltered, grief breaking through her authority. She looked at Troll and said quietly, “Make it count.”

Then she stepped aside.

In the hall, Stremler lifted both hands as if calming a crowd he had not summoned. His voice sought control. “We are here to ensure sound procedure. Noise will not distract us.”

Libaux patched the recorder one last time. Not coughs. Not tide. A file Troll had made in the safe flat the night before: a single marble rolling across wood, then striking another. **tik—tok.** Followed by her mother’s name, caught in the room’s echo and made large.

It played across the floor, across the dais, across the polite teal chairs. The warehouse of Vienna, built to absorb, had no choice but to remember.

Delegates whispered. Translators hesitated, unsure whether to repeat a name they did not know. The sound became fact by virtue of being heard.

Stremler lowered his eyes to his notes. He stacked them, one atop the other, as if filing a life away. When he spoke again, it was not for the room but for the record. “This session is adjourned.”

Outside, Vienna’s air felt sharper, freer, as if the city had been holding its breath and let it go.

Troll pocketed the marbles, all of them, heavy now with what they had carried. Libaux walked beside her without a word until they reached the canal.

“Think he’ll recover?” he asked.

“He will,” Troll said. “But so will the room.”

They watched the water drag fragments of light downstream. A tram chimed in the distance, pure and bright, as if rehearsing dissonance.

Troll whispered her mother’s name one more time. The canal gave it back, soft and stubborn.
