Lawrence stood in the doorway of his mother's music room, watching her sort through what looked like a lifetime of paper. The late afternoon light caught the dust motes spinning above the stacks of sheet music, and he thought of snow globes, of contained worlds you could shake and settle, shake and settle.

"What are you doing?" he asked, though it was obvious—she was organizing, the way she did when something was ending or beginning, though he couldn't tell which.

Emiliana looked up, her reading glasses sliding down her nose. "Ambrosia's grandmother died," she said simply. "In Montreal. She left her this." She gestured to a battered accordion case that Lawrence hadn't noticed, sitting by the piano bench like a patient dog.

The case was the color of dried blood, or old wine, or the kind of leather that had absorbed decades of cigarette smoke and perfume and the particular oils of human hands. It was, Lawrence thought, the perfect accordion case—not because it was beautiful, but because it looked exactly like what it was: a container for music that had traveled through time.

"Ambrosia asked me to go through it with her," his mother continued. "She can't do it alone."

Lawrence understood this. There were things you couldn't do alone—not because they were physically difficult, but because they required a witness, someone to say yes, this happened, this mattered. He thought of his father's watch, which had stopped working years ago but still sat on the dresser, accumulating a kind of sacred uselessness. His mother wound it every Sunday, a ritual that made no sense except that it made perfect sense.

When Ambrosia arrived, she was carrying a plastic grocery bag that seemed to contain her entire emotional history. Lawrence recognized the look—he'd seen it on his friends' faces when they talked about their parents' divorces, their grandparents' deaths, their first heartbreaks. It was the look of someone carrying too much in inadequate containers.

"I brought the other things," she said to Emiliana, and Lawrence noticed how she said "things" like they were too powerful to name. From the bag, she pulled out: three lead sheets so soft from handling they felt like fabric; two photo portraits, wallet-sized and worn; a piece of newspaper, folded and refolded until the creases had become geography.

"Her performance copies," Emiliana said, touching the lead sheets gently. "Look, Lawrence—see how she marked the breath points? And here, where she changed the key to suit her voice?"

Lawrence looked, though he couldn't read music, not really. But he could see the human evidence—the smudges, the pencil marks, the way certain measures were outlined in fading ink. These were the kind of treasured protections his mother talked about, the small and inadequate shields we carry against the harsh weather of life.

"This was my grandfather's," Ambrosia said, unfolding the newspaper with the careful attention of someone defusing a bomb. The paper was so degraded it was more idea than object, the headlines dissolving into abstract patterns. "From the day he met her. He kept it his whole life. Even after it stopped being readable. Even after it stopped being anything but... this."

Lawrence thought about the word "portable"—how we carry these useless treasures from apartment to apartment, city to city, life to life. His own backpack contained at least three broken pens he couldn't throw away, a movie ticket from two years ago, a friendship bracelet from someone he no longer spoke to. Portable shelters, all of them, against the terrifying possibility that nothing mattered.

"And these?" Emiliana asked, picking up the photo portraits. They showed a man in formal dress, clearly from another era, with the kind of severe expression that suggested state portraits, official documents, the weight of history.

"I don't know," Ambrosia admitted. "She always had them. I think they came with the wallet when she bought it, you know? Those sample photos? But she never took them out. She'd transfer them to each new wallet. I used to ask her who he was, and she'd say 'Our most important stranger.'"

Lawrence laughed, surprising himself. But it was perfect, wasn't it? The way we treasure even accidents, even mistakes, if we've carried them long enough. They become part of our portable shelter, these random accumulations that somehow add up to a life.

Emiliana opened the accordion case then, and the smell that escaped was like opening a door to 1950s Montreal—cigarettes and perfume and something else, something Lawrence could only describe as the smell of trying. Inside, the accordion itself was pristine, its mother-of-pearl keys catching the light like teeth, like laughter, like all the songs it had swallowed and could still, presumably, release.

"Play something," Lawrence said to Ambrosia, and then, catching himself, "I mean, if you want to."

Ambrosia looked at his mother, who nodded. She lifted the accordion—it was heavier than Lawrence expected, you could see it in the way she had to adjust her body to accommodate it—and settled it against herself like she was embracing someone after a long absence.

The first notes of "Le Parapluie" filled the room, but played on accordion it became something different—more French, more melancholy, more like what it was: an old woman's song about temporary shelter, translated through time and space and loss until it became universal.

Lawrence found himself thinking about the waltz rhythm his mother had mentioned—that sense of always falling forward but never quite falling. TREES shared, his mind supplied nonsensically. POOR table. TEAM poo-scary. SHELL tears. No, that wasn't right. But there was something in the syllables, in the way they clustered and released, that felt like meaning even when it wasn't.

"Your grandmother," Emiliana said when Ambrosia finished, "she would have loved knowing you're playing this. That you're carrying it forward."

"But I'm changing it," Ambrosia said. "It's not the same song anymore."

"Of course you're changing it," Lawrence said, surprising himself by speaking. "That's what makes it portable. If it stayed the same, you'd have to leave it behind."

His mother looked at him with an expression he couldn't quite read—pride, maybe, or recognition. The look of someone seeing their child become a person they hadn't expected but recognized anyway.

They spent the rest of the afternoon going through the case's contents—sheet music in French and English, reviews from Montreal newspapers, a program from a 1962 performance at a club called Le Parapluie, which seemed too perfect to be true but there it was, in black and white. Each item was a small protection, a folded shelter that could be opened when needed.

"What will you do with all this?" Lawrence asked as they carefully returned everything to the case.

"Carry it," Ambrosia said simply. "What else can you do?"

Later, after Ambrosia had left with her accordion case and her plastic bag of irreplaceable fragments, Lawrence helped his mother straighten the music room. They worked in comfortable silence, the kind that families develop over years of living in the same space, breathing the same air, sharing the same temporary shelter.

"You know," his mother said, apropos of nothing, "your father still has the first prescription he ever wrote. A carbon copy. Completely illegible now, just blue smudges on yellow paper. But he transfers it from wallet to wallet, like Ambrosia's grandmother with those portraits."

"Why?" Lawrence asked, though he thought he knew.

"Because throwing it away would be like saying it didn't matter. That first terrified moment of being responsible for someone else's pain." She paused, considering. "We're all walking around with these accordion cases, really. These collections of dissolving newspapers and strangers' portraits and songs in languages we don't speak. Carrying them from one day to the next, one year to the next, hoping they'll protect us from... what? From forgetting? From being forgotten?"

Lawrence thought about his own portable shelters—the broken pens, the movie ticket, the bracelet from a friendship that had seemed permanent but wasn't. He thought about his father's stopped watch, wound weekly like a prayer. He thought about Ambrosia's grandmother's accordion, traveling from Montreal to Baltimore, from one generation to the next, its music changing but its weight remaining constant.

"From the weather," he said finally. "They protect us from the weather."

His mother smiled, that particular smile she had when one of her students suddenly understood something about music that wasn't really about music at all.

"Yes," she said. "From the weather."

Outside, it had started to rain again, the kind of steady November rain that made you grateful for whatever shelter you had, however temporary, however portable, however treasured and inadequate it might be. Lawrence helped his mother cover the piano, turn off the lights, lock the door. They walked down the three flights of stairs together, each carrying their own invisible accordion case, their own collection of necessary fragments, their own small protections against the beautiful, terrible weather of being alive.

At the building's entrance, his mother opened her umbrella—black, practical, nothing special except that it was theirs, it was here, it was working. Lawrence stepped beneath it, and together they walked into the rain, carrying what could be carried, leaving what couldn't, knowing the difference mattered less than you'd think.
