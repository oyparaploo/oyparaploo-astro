*A woman delivering food to a warehouse starts finding photocopied pages about herself under windshield wipers, in bathroom stalls, taped to dumpsters—not understanding she's already inside the story she thinks she's reading.*

Teresa's shift at DoorDash started normal. November, Cleveland, $3.50 base pay plus tips that never came. She had the warehouse delivery again—some abandoned place where people ordered McDonald's at 2 AM.

Under her windshield: a photocopied page. Bad quality. Her name in it. "Teresa walked past the pile every day without seeing it."

But she'd never walked past any pile. Threw it away.

Next delivery, same warehouse. Another page, taped to the door. "Teresa's daughter Marina had been at the protests before disappearing."

Marina was in Phoenix. Rehab. Not protests. Not disappeared. Just gone in the specific way addicts go—present but unreachable.

She knocked. Someone in paint-covered clothes took the food. Behind them, she saw it: a pile of garbage, floor to ceiling. Arranged somehow. Like it meant something.

"What is that?"

"Just stuff."

Drove away. But the pages kept appearing. Not following her—she was following them. Every delivery led to another piece of paper about her life. Wrong but also right. Marina at protests. Marina disappeared. Marina becoming part of something Teresa didn't understand.

Thursday. Five deliveries to the warehouse. Five pages. The story multiplying, contradicting itself. In one, Teresa was an artist. In another, fired from Target. In another, finding blue electrical cords after her daughter's death.

"Stop leaving these," she told the paint-covered person.

"Leaving what?"

"The stories. About me."

They looked confused. "We don't leave things. We just make things here."

But the pages kept coming. Under her apartment door. In her mailbox. At the gas station. Each one showing her life tilted five degrees from true.

She started keeping them. Not collecting—that was what crazy people did. Just... not throwing them away. Had forty-three pages now. The story of Teresa who wasn't Teresa but was.

Went to the warehouse without an order. Just went. Saturday. Day off but what was a day off when you worked seven apps to make rent?

More people now. All covered in paint or dirt or something. Moving things. Building things. Being there in a way she didn't understand.

"Is Marina here?"

"Who's Marina?"

"My daughter. The stories say she's here."

"What stories?"

She showed them the pages. They read. Passed them around. Nobody recognized them.

"But this is about here," one said. A kid, maybe twelve. "That pile. That's our pile."

"So you wrote them?"

"No. They just appear. Like everything."

Teresa looked at the pile. Really looked. Saw Marina's rehab bracelet in it. The purple one. But Marina still had hers—she'd sent a photo yesterday. From Phoenix. Or said it was Phoenix.

Started coming every day. Not delivering. Just there. Watching people make things from garbage. Watching them paint walls with questions. Watching them fall down and get up but differently.

Found herself doing things. Moving a mannequin. Tying rope to pipe. Small things. Involuntary. Like her hands knew what to do even if she didn't.

"You're in it now," the kid said.

"In what?"

"The thing. The movement. The whatever."

"I'm just here for Marina."

"Marina's not here."

"The stories say—"

"The stories say lots of things."

New pages appeared. About Teresa joining. About Teresa making art. About Teresa understanding. But she didn't understand. Didn't want to understand. Just wanted Marina back.

Called Phoenix. Marina had left. Or had never been there. Stories conflicted.

"Mom?" Marina's voice on the phone. Background noise like hammering.

"Where are you?"

"I'm... I'm doing something."

"What?"

"I don't know. But something."

The line went dead. Teresa drove to the warehouse. Marina wasn't there. Had never been there. Was definitely there. The stories said all three things.

She picked up paint. Started painting. Not painting anything. Just moving paint around. Others nodded like this made sense. Like this was what people did here.

"We're making—" someone started.

"Don't explain it," Teresa said. Surprising herself. "I don't want it explained."

Days became weeks. She kept delivering food but also kept coming here. The pile grew. The stories multiplied. Some about her. Some about Marina. Some about people she'd never met but felt she knew.

Lost the apartment. Slept in her car. Slept in the warehouse sometimes. The distinction blurred. Everything blurred except the clarity that she was doing something even if she didn't know what.

"Is this helping Marina?" she asked the kid.

"Is Marina real?"

"Of course she's—" But was she? The stories had versions. Marina the addict. Marina the protestor. Marina the disappeared. Which was real?

"Does it matter?" the kid asked.

It should matter. Everything should matter. But here, covered in paint, surrounded by garbage made important, reading stories about herself that weren't true but felt true, nothing mattered the way it used to.

The warehouse got demolished. Or was scheduled for demolition. Or existed in the space between. The stories said all three.

Teresa kept delivering food. $3.50 base pay. Tips that never came. But now she delivered to people she knew. The painted ones. The falling ones. The ones making garbage into something.

Found the last story under her windshield. About Teresa finding peace. About understanding. About letting go.

"That's not me," she said aloud.

"Not yet," someone answered. The kid maybe. Or Marina. Or the person Teresa was becoming.

She crumpled it up. Then smoothed it out. Then added it to the pile. The big pile in the warehouse that might not exist. Where her daughter might not be. Where she definitely was. Doing something.

Always doing something.

Despite everything.

Despite not understanding.

Despite.

*We join movements not through understanding but through proximity to our own losses, finding ourselves doing things we can't explain, becoming part of stories we never meant to enter, continuing not because it makes sense but because stopping would mean admitting that nothing makes sense, and we're not ready for that truth, not yet, maybe never, but despite everything, we continue.*
