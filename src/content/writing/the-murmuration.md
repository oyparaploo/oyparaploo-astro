The birds came to Helen Choi the week she stopped sleeping. Not metaphorically - actual birds, clustering at her apartment window in Sacramento, their small bodies creating patterns against the glass that looked like writing she couldn't read.

"It's just the drought," her daughter Emma said over video call from Pittsburgh. "They're looking for water."

But Helen had put out water. Seed too. The birds ignored both, pressing instead against her third-floor window in perfect formations - sometimes a spiral, sometimes a heart, sometimes shapes that made her chest tight with recognition she couldn't name.

She'd been a coal miner's daughter in Pennsylvania before becoming a climate scientist in California. Her father used to keep canaries. "They sing until they can't," he'd told her once. "That's how you know."

Know what? she'd asked.

"That it's time to get out."

Now, at seventy-one, Helen watched her data models predict what the birds already knew. Five degrees. Six. The numbers that meant the end of things, including birds. Especially birds.

The cluster at her window grew denser each day. Sparrows, finches, doves - species that shouldn't flock together, moving as one mind. Their small bodies created shadows in her apartment that shifted like weather.

"You should call someone," Emma said during their next call.

"Who do you call about birds?"

"Animal control?"

"They're not doing anything wrong. They're just... gathered."

Emma's face on the screen showed the concern Helen recognized - the look adult children get when they start counting their parents' remaining years. "Mom, your face looks different. Are you sleeping?"

Helen wasn't. How could she, with the birds creating their urgent patterns all night, their bodies catching streetlight, making messages she was failing to read?

On the fifth day, she recognized someone in the cluster's shape - her father's profile, clear as a photograph, formed by two dozen birds holding perfect stillness. She grabbed her phone to capture it, but they scattered before she could focus, reforming into what looked like a date: 1968.

The year the Farmington Mine exploded. Seventy-eight men, including her uncle. The canaries had gone silent three days before, but management said they were just sick. Not a warning. Just sick birds.

Helen opened her window. The birds didn't enter, but they pressed closer, their small hearts visible through translucent chest feathers, all beating at the same impossible rate. She counted: 420 beats per minute. The exact rate of a canary in distress.

She called Emma back. "I need to tell you something."

"Mom, you're scaring me."

"The birds - I know what they're trying to say."

"Mom-"

"Listen. In 1968, before Farmington, the birds knew. All of them. Not just the canaries in the mine. The wild birds left the valley three days before. My mother told me they darkened the sky leaving, but nobody thought it meant anything."

Emma was quiet. Then: "What are you trying to tell me?"

Helen looked at the birds clustered at her window, their formation now creating shapes in empty space - the absence of birds spelling something urgent she couldn't quite read.

"I finished my final report yesterday," Helen said. "We have less time than anyone's saying."

"How much less?"

The birds shifted, dissolved, reformed into empty spaces that looked like the future.

Helen didn't answer.

"I'm flying out," Emma said.

"No. Stay where you are. Keep your garden. Feed the birds."

"Mom-"

"They're trying to tell us something. The way they tried to tell us in Farmington. The way they've always tried. We just stopped knowing how to listen."

That night, Helen fell asleep in her chair by the window. She dreamed of her father's canaries, their yellow bodies bright as warnings. When she woke, the birds at her window had arranged themselves into a perfect replica of the last photograph she had of him, holding a canary with such tenderness it made her chest ache.

She opened the window fully. One by one, the birds entered - not frantically but with ceremony. They settled on her furniture, her shoulders. A dove nestled against her neck.

"I'm listening," she said.

They began to sing - not their individual songs but something collective, impossible. It sounded like her father's canaries but also like wind through empty mines.

Emma called again. Helen answered without moving, birds covering her like a living blanket.

"Mom? I can hear them through the phone."

"They're everywhere now."

"Are you okay?"

Helen considered. She was seventy-one. She'd published forty years of warnings that read like birdsong to people who'd forgotten the language. Her father's canaries had died doing what they were born to do - becoming the message when the message was the only thing left.

"There are birds here too," Emma said suddenly. "At my window. Hundreds of them."

"What are they doing?"

"Watching me. Waiting."

Helen felt the dove's heartbeat against her throat, rapid as warning, soft as forgiveness. "Plant everything you can," she told her daughter. "Make water places. Build nest boxes."

"Will it help?"

"No. But they'll know we finally understood."

The birds stayed with Helen for seven days. On the eighth, they left all at once, their departure creating a vacuum that pulled papers from her desk - all her careful data scattering like seed. She found one page stuck to the window: her graph of extinction rates. The birds had left tiny prints across it, their feet creating a new pattern over her projections. It looked like music notation. It looked like a message. It looked like an ending.

Emma flew out anyway, found her mother sitting by the open window, peaceful as Helen had ever seen her. 

"They're gone," Helen said.

"I know. They left Pittsburgh too. All at once, like a signal."

They sat together, watching the empty sky. Somewhere, Helen knew, the birds were gathering in places humans couldn't follow. Not abandoning but preparing. The way her father's canaries had known to go silent before the explosion. The way creatures always know when to stop singing and start surviving.

"What do we do now?" Emma asked.

Helen took her daughter's hand. It felt like her father's had - roughened by work, softened by care. "We become the canaries," she said. "We sing until we can't. That's how others will know."

"Know what?"

"That it's time to get out. Or time to finally get in."

Emma squeezed her hand. Outside, the empty sky looked like a held breath.
