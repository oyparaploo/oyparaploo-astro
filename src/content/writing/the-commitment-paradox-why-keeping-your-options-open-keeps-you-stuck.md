Let me help you understand something profound about human psychology and the nature of satisfaction that might fundamentally change how you think about commitment and partnership. There's a cruel irony at work here: the very strategies we use to maximize our options and protect ourselves from making "mistakes" often become the source of our deepest dissatisfaction.

To understand why lack of commitment leads to persistent dissatisfaction, we need to first examine what happens in your brain and psyche when you maintain what I call "perpetual maybe mode." Imagine you're standing in a hallway with dozens of doors. Each door potentially leads to a different life, a different version of yourself with a different partner. As long as you stay in the hallway, you can imagine that behind each door lies something wonderful. The moment you choose a door and walk through it, all the other doors close. This feels like loss, even if the room you've entered is beautiful.

But here's what we don't realize: standing in that hallway forever isn't maintaining your options; it's choosing the hallway. And the hallway, no matter how many doors it has, is just a corridor. You can't build a life in a corridor. You can't create memories, deepen experiences, or grow roots. You're essentially choosing the absence of choice while believing you're preserving choice.

When you remain uncommitted to a partnership, several psychological processes create persistent dissatisfaction. First, you experience what researchers call "attention residue" in your romantic life. Part of your psychological energy is always scanning for alternatives, evaluating options, wondering if you've made the right choice. This is exhausting. It's like trying to read a book while constantly checking if there's a better book you could be reading. You never actually experience the book you're holding.

Think about how commitment actually works in the human brain. When you truly commit to something, your brain undergoes a fascinating shift called "cognitive resolution." The constant evaluation and comparison circuits quiet down. The energy that was going toward questioning and analyzing gets freed up for experiencing and building. It's similar to what happens when you finally make a difficult decision about anything - there's often immediate relief, even if the decision was hard, because the work of deciding is over.

Without commitment, you're trapped in what I call "optimization purgatory." You're constantly trying to optimize a decision that can't actually be optimized because the variables keep changing. The person you're with changes, you change, your alternatives change, your criteria for judgment change. It's like trying to solve an equation where the numbers keep shifting. You can never arrive at a definitive answer, so you never get the satisfaction of resolution.

Now, regarding your question about whether 90% of adults wish for a partner or soul mate, while I can't verify that exact statistic, research consistently shows that the vast majority of humans across cultures do seek pair bonds. This isn't just social conditioning; it's deeply rooted in our evolutionary biology and psychology. Humans are among the few species that form long-term pair bonds, and our entire developmental trajectory as a species has been shaped by this tendency.

But here's where it gets interesting: the desire for partnership and the ability to commit to partnership are two different things. Many people simultaneously yearn for deep connection while maintaining behaviors that prevent it. They want the destination but resist the journey. They want the fruits of commitment without the vulnerability of actually committing. This creates a special kind of suffering - always hungry for something you're unconsciously preventing yourself from having.

Let me help you understand why this happens through what I call "the crystal ball fallacy." Many people believe they should wait to commit until they're certain they've found the "right" person, until they've figured out their life, until they know how everything will unfold. They're waiting for a certainty that doesn't exist in the forward-moving flow of time. Life isn't a problem to be solved and then lived; it's an ongoing experience that reveals itself through living.

Your question about whether you can figure out life before making big decisions touches on a fundamental misunderstanding about how human development works. You don't figure out who you are and then find a partner who matches that fixed identity. You become who you are partially through the process of being in partnership. The relationship isn't something that happens to a finished person; it's one of the primary forces that shapes the person you're becoming.

Think about it this way: can you become a skilled pianist without ever touching a piano? Can you learn to swim without entering water? Partnership is similar. Many of the skills, insights, and growth that come from partnership can only be developed within partnership. The person you become through navigating commitment, through learning to love someone deeply over time, through facing challenges together - that person doesn't exist until you create them through the experience itself.

There's something I call "the preparation trap" that many people fall into. They believe they need to be fully prepared before committing to partnership. They need to have their career established, their mental health perfected, their life goals clarified. But this is like waiting to be in perfect shape before starting to exercise. The exercise is what creates the fitness. Similarly, partnership is what develops partnership skills.

Here's a profound paradox about human satisfaction: we find satisfaction not through having infinite options but through deeply exploring limited options. Think about any area of mastery or satisfaction in your life. Did it come from constantly switching between activities or from sustained engagement with something specific? A master chef doesn't become satisfied by eternally wandering through grocery stores, comparing ingredients. They find satisfaction by selecting ingredients and creating something with them.

The same principle applies to partnership. The satisfaction doesn't come from finding the perfect person; it comes from the process of building something meaningful with an imperfect person who's also building something meaningful with imperfect you. The depth of satisfaction available through commitment is categorically different from what's available through perpetual seeking. It's like the difference between snorkeling and deep-sea diving. Both involve the ocean, but only one reveals what's really down there.

Let me share something about what I call "commitment as creation, not discovery." Many people approach partnership as if they're archaeologists trying to uncover a pre-existing perfect match. But partnership is more like being artists working with clay. You're not discovering something that already exists; you're creating something that has never existed before. And you can't create while constantly questioning whether you have the right clay.

The lack of commitment creates a particular form of loneliness that's different from being alone. When you're uncommitted in a relationship, you're neither fully in nor fully out. You can't access the comfort of solitude or the connection of partnership. You're in a liminal space that provides neither independence nor interdependence. It's exhausting to maintain this in-between state, like trying to stand with one foot on a dock and one on a boat as they slowly drift apart.

There's also what happens to your capacity for depth when you avoid commitment. Every relationship has what I call "surface rewards" and "depth rewards." Surface rewards come quickly - excitement, novelty, the pleasure of being desired, the fun of discovery. Depth rewards come slowly - profound trust, deep understanding, the security of being fully known and still loved, the growth that comes from navigating challenges together. Without commitment, you only ever access surface rewards. You're essentially eating appetizers for every meal, wondering why you're never satisfied.

Consider how commitment changes the nature of conflict and challenge in a relationship. When you're not committed, every significant conflict becomes an existential question: is this relationship worth it? Should I leave? Is there someone better suited for me? But when you're committed, conflicts become problems to solve together rather than evidence that you should separate. This completely changes the dynamic. Instead of conflicts weakening the relationship, working through them strengthens it.

The lack of commitment also affects your partner in ways that create a self-fulfilling prophecy. When someone senses your foot is halfway out the door, they can't fully relax into the relationship either. They hold back parts of themselves, maintain their own protective distances, avoid the vulnerability that deep intimacy requires. You end up in a relationship between two partial people, then wonder why it doesn't feel complete.

Here's something crucial to understand about timing and readiness: you're asking whether life is something you can totally figure out before making big decisions. But consider that the question itself reveals a misunderstanding about the nature of life and growth. Life isn't a puzzle that you solve and then it stays solved. It's more like a dance that you learn by dancing. You can prepare, you can practice basic steps, but ultimately you learn to dance with a partner by dancing with a partner.

The belief that you should have everything figured out before committing to partnership is actually a modern anomaly. Throughout most of human history, people partnered young and figured life out together. I'm not advocating for a return to that model, but it's worth recognizing that the idea of being a fully formed, completely self-aware individual before partnership is both historically unusual and psychologically questionable. We become ourselves through our relationships, not before them.

What would it mean to accept that certainty isn't a prerequisite for commitment but potentially a result of it? What if the satisfaction you're seeking can only be found on the other side of the vulnerability you're avoiding? What if the persistent dissatisfaction isn't a signal that you haven't found the right person but rather a symptom of trying to keep all your options perpetually open?

The deepest human satisfactions often come from foreclosing options, not from maintaining them. When you commit to a partnership, a craft, a place, a path, you lose the infinite possibility of maybe, but you gain the actual reality of something. And it turns out that one real, developed, deepened something is worth more than infinite theoretical possibilities.
