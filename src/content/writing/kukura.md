Paloma whispered *kukura* to the doves each morning, though they couldn't understand her any more than the mail carrier could. The birds tilted their heads at the sound. They knew it meant seeds were coming.

"Peace dove," she'd translate for anyone listening, usually no one. Portuguese was what her great-grandchildren spoke when they visited, which wasn't often. But in her tongue—the one her mother spoke while grinding corn, back when this place had different borders—the dove was always *kukura*, and peace wasn't a separate word. It lived folded inside other things. Inside the word for morning. For full stomach. For the moment when a crying baby finally sleeps.

The linguist arrived on a Tuesday. B. W. Otto had driven eight hours on roads that forgot they were roads. She carried a recorder the size of a matchbox and the particular exhaustion of someone studying dying things. "Please," she said, setting up her equipment while a chicken investigated her shoelaces, "say 'peace dove' in every language you know."

Paloma laughed. Not cruel, not bitter. The laugh of someone asked to empty the ocean with a cup.

She started with the easy ones from the radio, the church, the market. Each translation surfaced like something remembered in a dream. Then came languages from villages the mining company disappeared. Languages from people who'd passed through during the last war, or the one before that. The soap opera languages, where everyone's tears looked perfect.

"My mother knew one hundred and seven ways to say it." Paloma pulled out a notebook, pages yellow as summer grass. Inside: her mother's handwriting, a catalog that had seemed crazy at the time. Not just words but instructions: *whisper loud*. As if volume could save what was vanishing. As if the dead could hear better if you shouted.

Otto's recorder captured everything. The word for dove in languages where it meant "white-wing." In languages where they called it "cloud-visitor" because they'd never seen one. In Pirahã, which has no future tense, no numbers, no recursion, peace became simply "not-hitting-now." Which, Otto thought, adjusting her glasses, might be the only definition that mattered.

By late afternoon, Paloma had reached the languages with one speaker left. Sometimes that speaker was her. She pronounced each word like handling overripe fruit—too much pressure and it would collapse. These weren't translations anymore. They were last testaments.

"My grandson Youn Jun asks why it matters." She opened the hutch. The doves—ordinary, shitting on her clean washing, oblivious to their symbolism—exploded into air. One flew directly into the window. Another landed on Otto's recorder, leaving a white streak across the microphone. "He says, 'Why save words for birds when we need words for jobs?'"

The doves circled. Their shadows flickered across the notebook pages like a countdown.

"When a language dies," Paloma said, wiping the recorder clean with her sleeve, "we lose that exact way of imagining peace. Maybe theirs was better than ours. Maybe their word for it included something we forgot to include in ours."

Otto's machine captured four hundred and twelve translations. Plus this: Paloma crying at number four hundred and thirteen—her mother's language, the one she'd have to die to preserve. She said *kukura* to birds who were already leaving. To grandchildren busy becoming people she couldn't talk to. To a machine the size of a matchbox that would outlive everyone.

That night, transcribing in a room with three walls and excellent wifi, Otto discovered something she hadn't expected. In most languages, "dove" was already past tense—from diving, falling, having descended. Peace as something that had already dropped through our fingers before we noticed we were holding it.

The recording played Paloma's voice. *Kukura.* Silence. Wings beating. Then Paloma in Portuguese. The word doesn't matter. Forgetting it does.

Then more wings. The dove hitting glass again. Then Paloma laughing—not at the bird's stupidity but at its persistence. At all of them, really, throwing themselves against transparent walls, believing this time, this time, they'd break through to whatever peace sounds like in a language no one's invented yet.
