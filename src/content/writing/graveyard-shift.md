The phone rang at 2:17 AM. Marlène was awake, dressed. Had been sleeping in her clothes since the eviction notice.

"There's a job," Theo said. "Amazon warehouse. Jersey. Hundred fifty for four hours."

"Playing what?"

"Violin. For the workers. Corporate wellness bullshit."

Rent was three weeks late. The ceiling leaked brown. Queens. $1,850 a month for a studio.

"Yeah, okay."

The warehouse was massive. Jersey wetlands. Concrete and fluorescents forever. The security guard checking her in said, "I have a master's in engineering," while printing her badge. Like he had to tell someone.

Station 7. Workers grabbed items from bins. Same motion every seven seconds. Ten hours. Thirty-minute break, unpaid. Two fifteens, paid, but the break room was six minutes away walking fast.

"You the music lady?"

Woman, maybe fifty. Dominican. Compression gloves on both hands.

"Yeah."

"What, you gonna play us a lullaby?" She laughed ugly. "I'm Maria. Seventeen fifty an hour. Carpal tunnel in both wrists. Play me something for that."

Marlène lifted the violin—her grandmother's, Czech, 1923—and started Bach because what else did she know? The sound disappeared into conveyor belts, scanners, the robot voice: "Current rate: 287. Target: 320."

"Wrong music," Maria said at break. Workers clustered around vending machines. Three-fifty for water. "Play something else."

"Like what?"

"I don't know. Something that moves. When you're at the bottom but still moving."

So Marlène played something else. Not Bach. Not anything. Just matched the rhythm of bodies grabbing items. Seven seconds. Grab. Seven seconds. Grab. Added notes between. Made it almost music.

The workers started moving with it. Not dancing—that would trigger the algorithm. Just... with it somehow.

"Better," said a kid at next break. Mohamed. Had a faded medical school sticker on his badge. His hands shook. "You get it."

"Get what?"

"I don't know. The thing where you keep going even though." He held up the shaking hands. "Week two this started."

She came back next night. And the next. Started watching specific bodies, playing them back. Maria's shoulder rolled the same way every time. Played that. Dev limped. Played that. Chen stared at nothing, Chinese guy, maybe late forties. Played that too.

"What the fuck is that noise?" Supervisor showed up night four. White kid, twenty-five maybe. "It sounds wrong."

"Sounds like work," Chen said. His English was perfect. "Sounds like what work does to a body."

"It's disturbing."

"So?" Mohamed held up his shaking hands. "My rate's up fifteen percent."

True. The metrics showed it. Productivity up 12% when Marlène played. Fewer bathroom breaks—not wellness, just the music made time move weird.

Corporate sent someone. Tuesday. Senior Vice President of something. Marlène looked her up on her phone—$340,000 salary, Harvard, stock options.

"We want to scale this," the VP said. Watched from a glass box above the floor. Climate-controlled up there. "Thirty facilities. Three thousand a week."

"Pay them twenty-five an hour," Marlène said.

"The musicians?"

"The workers."

"That's not—"

"Then no."

But she kept coming back anyway. One-fifty was one-fifty. Rent was now five weeks late. Other musicians started coming too. Troy, guitarist, used to play jazz clubs before whatever happened to him. Keiko, cellist. They played 11 PM to 5 AM. Not concerts. Just... sound that matched bodies moving.

Thursday, around 3:45, everyone synced up for a few seconds. Just happened. The machines, the workers, the music. All moving together. Then it stopped.

"What was that?" the VP asked.

"Thursday," Marlène said.

"Document it. Study it."

"Can't. It just happens sometimes."

Amazon offered her a contract. National expansion. She asked about worker raises again. They offered her more money instead. She took it—had to—but kept playing the warehouse.

The Philharmonic called six months later. Some program about "Non-Traditional Venues."

"This is the most traditional venue," she told them, though she didn't believe it really. Just sounded good. "Work songs. Prison songs. This is where music came from."

They hung up.

Mohamed got into med school. Acceptance letter in his locker, never used. Warehouse paid better than residency and his dad had had a stroke. Maria's wrists got worse. Workers' comp denied surgery. Chen got deported. October. His daughters were at Cornell. He never saw them graduate.

Marlène kept playing. One-fifty a night. 

"We're writing something," Mohamed said one morning walking to their cars.

"Writing what?"

"I don't know. Evidence."

"Of what?"

He shrugged. "That we're still here."

Maria couldn't close her hands anymore by December. Still worked. Had to. Dev's limp got worse. New guy started, quit after three days. "This is insane," he said. "You're all insane."

Maybe.

Marlène played violin at 3 AM in a warehouse. Hundred fifty a night. The workers moved to it or maybe she moved to them or maybe everyone was just moving because stopping meant starving and moving meant breaking but at least it was breaking to a rhythm.

She still plays there. Tuesdays through Saturdays. The brown leak in her ceiling got worse but she made rent eventually. Theo called with other gigs but she kept the warehouse. Don't know why. 

The workers know her now. New ones ask what the music is for.

"Nothing," Maria tells them. She can't make fists anymore but still grabs packages every seven seconds. "It's for nothing. But she plays it anyway."

That's true enough.

3 AM. Jersey. Warehouse. People breaking to rhythm because rhythm is better than silence.

Or it isn't.

But one-fifty is one-fifty.

And everyone needs something at 3 AM.

Even if it's just sound.
