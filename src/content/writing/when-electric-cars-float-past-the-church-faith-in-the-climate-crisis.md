## Part I: The Authority of Drowning

I'm painting portraits of flood survivors in my Houston church when a Tesla floats past the stained-glass windows, and I realize we're witnessing biblical-scale catastrophe through 21st-century technology.

My credentials: Seminary dropout turned disaster relief artist, documenting climate refugees through portraiture for seven years. Daughter of a Baptist preacher who insisted Jesus would return before the ice caps melted. Eight hundred portraits painted in flooded churches across the Gulf Coast, each one asking the same question.

The paradox: We pray for salvation while creating the conditions for our own apocalypse.

## Part II: The Prophet of Undying Love

*First Baptist Church, Houston. Third floor, above the waterline.*

Pastor Marcus—who calls himself "Prophet of Undying Love"—stands knee-deep in contaminated floodwater, baptizing people in the sanctuary.

"If John baptized in the Jordan," he says, "I'll baptize in Hurricane Harvey's aftermath."

The congregation, mostly climate refugees, watches from the balcony. Half their homes are underwater. Electric cars drift past like steel coffins, their batteries sparking in the murky water.

"June has traded places with January," Marcus preaches. "Seasons don't know themselves anymore. But God remains."

## Part III: The Portrait Project

*My makeshift studio in the church attic.*

Eight canvases dry simultaneously, each marked "ISPI"—my code for "I See Past Invisibility." Every portrait is someone the insurance companies call "acts of God casualties."

Maria, 67, lost her third home to flooding: "God didn't do this. Exxon did."

James, 34, software engineer, watching his Tesla float away: "I bought electric to help. Now it's toxic waste in the water."

Each sitting begins with prayer, ends with tears. The portraits capture what FEMA statistics miss—the weight of repeated loss.

## Part IV: The Music of Catastrophe

*Sound installation in the flooded basement.*

My collaborator, DJ Dawo, has set up hydrophones in the floodwater, capturing the sound of a drowned city. He mixes it with gospel hymns, creating what he calls "climate spirituals."

"Sound is more than notes," he explains. "It's presence. The earth is singing its death song, and we're adding harmony."

The installation plays through the church: gurgling water, sparking electrical systems, voices singing "Wade in the Water" while literally wading in water.

## Part V: Doors and Transitions

*Research into the church's history.*

This building has survived six "hundred-year floods" in twenty years. Each waterline marked on the walls like tree rings. The front doors—massive oak installed in 1892—have swollen and shrunk so many times they no longer close properly.

"Doors mark transitions," writes the church historian. "But what happens when the transition never ends?"

I paint the doors from inside, capturing their warped geometry, their refusal to seal out disaster.

## Part VI: Wild Dogs and Hungry Children

*Food distribution, church parking lot.*

While humans debate climate policy, wild dogs thrive in the chaos, scavenging from abandoned homes. They're healthier than the children in our food line—malnourished kids whose families lost everything.

"Some suffer silently," Marcus says, spooning rice into containers. "Young men grasp rifles instead of hope. But here, we grasp each other."

A boy asks if Jesus will stop the floods.

Marcus pauses. "Jesus gave us the knowledge to stop them ourselves. Question is, will we?"

## Part VII: The Corporate Resistance

*Interview with former oil executive, now church volunteer.*

"I spent thirty years denying climate science," Robert admits, sorting donated clothes. "Profitable denial. Then my own house flooded. Three times."

He shows me internal memos from the 1970s, proving his company knew exactly what would happen.

"We turned creation into commodity," he says. "Now creation is collecting payment."

## Part VIII: Art as Healing

*Community painting session.*

Fifty flood survivors paint their experiences on a massive canvas. No instruction, just expression. The result: swirling browns and grays punctuated by fragments of color—a child's toy, a wedding dress, a floating cross.

"Art is healing," says one painter, adding her drowned garden in green. "When insurance calls you a statistic, painting makes you human again."

## Part IX: The Climate Testimony

*Sunday service, relocated to the roof.*

The sanctuary is still flooded, so we worship under open sky. The congregation testifies not about personal salvation but planetary salvation.

"I believe in Jesus," says one member. "But faith without works is dead. And our work is stopping this climate catastrophe."

Another: "God gave us dominion over Earth. We turned dominion into domination."

Marcus leads prayer: "Forgive us for worshipping oil over creation. For choosing comfort over future generations. For denying science you gave us to understand your world."

## Part X: Electric Prophecy

*The Tesla graveyard.*

Behind the church, seventeen electric vehicles have washed up—their owners' attempt at environmental responsibility now toxic hazards leaking lithium into floodwater.

I paint them as modern arks that failed—technology that promised salvation but couldn't float. Each one cost more than rebuilding a flooded home.

"Even our solutions become problems," Marcus observes. "Because we're solving symptoms, not sins."

## Part XI: Sacred Currents

*Midnight. The water rises again.*

Another hurricane approaches. We move the portraits to the bell tower, the food to the roof, the sound equipment to safety. We've become experts at retreat.

Marcus refuses to leave. "Captains and churches," he says. "We go down with our ships."

But this isn't going down—it's transformation. The church becomes boat, shelter, ark, gallery, witnessing station for the new world being born in flood.

## Part XII: Resurrection Architecture

*Six months later. The church rebuilt, again.*

We've raised the entire building eight feet on stilts. Sunday school happens where the sanctuary used to be. The sanctuary floats above flood level.

My portraits line the walls—eight hundred faces of climate survival. The warped doors have been rehung as art installations. Marcus's sermons stream globally as "climate theology."

A new Tesla floats past during service. We don't stop singing.

## Epilogue: Faith in Rising Waters

*One year later.*

The church has become the "Cathedral of Climate Witness." Christians, Muslims, Jews, atheists gather here to organize resistance and resilience. 

My portraits tour museums with a message: "These are not victims of acts of God. They're witnesses to acts of man."

Marcus still baptizes in floodwater when it comes—which is often now. "If this is our Jordan River," he says, "we'll make it holy through honesty."

Faith hasn't saved us from flooding. But it's taught us to float, together, toward whatever comes next.
