Everything Was Something Else First Or Nothing

Everything in the Placings was something first. Before it was arranged and photographed it was a board, a bag, a bulb, a length of cord, lying around or thrown away by ourselves and others. This is the account of it, as full as we could make it. We went back through every gallery with the program that helped build this site, and wrote down what the camera found. Fifty-eight kinds of things. Most of them we are sure of. A few we are not, and we will say so when we get to them.

Start with what was under everything. Soil, mostly dark, in crumbs, in stripes laid across pale sand, and once wet and black and heaped on a folding table. Stone in every size the yard offered: gravel, pebbles by the hundred, cobbles, a flagstone, boulders, rubble, slabs, a block of granite, rock lumps coated white. Sand as a ground and sand as scattered grains. Snow, and ice when the snow gave up. Ash, which here means the marks that fire left behind, black soot and rust-brown scorch. In one picture a scorch burst sits on a pale sheet, orange, with fine lines running outward from its middle.

Then the building site. Concrete in blocks, some perforated, some coated white, and broken pieces and rough lumps hung inside wire mesh. Brick, red and pink and white-painted, and paving brick splashed with paint. Plaster in white lumps and broken pieces, a carved cream trim, pink plaster set in a ribbed cup. And in one gallery hundreds of small pale blocks with black marks, laid in loops with twisted cord over stones and pebbles, the way you would lay out a sentence if you had no letters.

Wood came next, more of it than anything but cloth and plastic. Logs and slices of log. Sticks: plain, white birch-like, thin orange ones laid into a web, sticks and branches wrapped in strips stiffened with white paint. Wood chips. Weathered pieces that had done four seasons more than once. Boards painted pink, teal, cream, orange, white and red, most of them peeling. Planks, beams, one burnt through, posts, rails, fences, a slatted wall, a parquet floor, frames, a drawer front, a painted cut-out shape. And the furniture that is wood before it is furniture: chairs, stools, tables, clothespins by the row. Reeds in bundles, and bamboo, we count as wood's cousins. A small grey stool lies upside down in several pictures of one gallery, and nobody rights it.

The hardware store and the tool shed gave the Placings most of what held the rest up. Metal first: hammers, saws and saw blades, trowels, clamps, pliers, a power drill, shovels, a pick head, an auger bit. Rebar, pipes, rails, chains, coil springs, perforated strips and sheets, a grille, brackets, a pulley, a tripod, ladders, steel shelving racks, scrap, a generator, and red fire extinguishers standing in four different galleries as if on duty.

Wire: chicken wire, welded mesh, window mesh, fishnet mesh, thin wire tangled and looped and coiled, rusted rods, fence wire, green line strung between posts to hang things from. Rope, which was mostly cord, black and white and every colour between, coiled, looped, braided, twisted, knotted, once a ball of twine, and striped rope. Net, nearly always green, sometimes white and hanging, once red. Tape, striped like a warning, a measuring tape, a whole roll. In one gallery white wire loops and rows of white discs lie on cobbles, and a pair of green boots stands among them, picture after picture.

Paint, as splatter, as drips, as smears, as peeling, as gold flakes, in small pots, in cans, and once as a large red disc on a carpet. Silver paint on a row of small heads set on a dark board, and a thin silver figure hung from a wire beside them. Foam: a pink-purple board with holes cut through it, a block, a strip. Rubber: inner tubes, a mat, work gloves, a boot, a shoe sole, cut black pieces, bands, and one large dark teardrop. A paintbrush and a black brush.

And plastic, in nearly every gallery, which figures, since it is the one thing that never finishes being garbage. Sheets, clear and black and camouflage print, one lit from below and one wet with blue lights under it. Tarps, a tent skin, a backlit white table. Bags, tied. Hoses, tubing, white pipes wrapped in pink tape. Buckets, basins, a crate, zip ties, lids, honeycomb pieces, cut shapes, and broken pieces too small to name, laid out by the handful on a sheet of glass. One large white tent was strung with black cord and lit from inside, and a red cross hung on its wall.

From the house: glass, as a marble, a blue glass ball on a grey carpet, jars, window panes, lamp shades, lenses. Ceramic vases and jars. Mirrors: a hand mirror, an oval mirror rimmed with beads that showed trees and sky instead of us, and one whole gallery of mirror broken into hundreds of pieces. Light bulbs doing their work inside the pictures: floodlights, string lights, coloured bulbs, lamps with tin shades, and lamps with no shades at all, about twenty of those, standing on a parquet floor in front of white drapes and later tipped over in a pile. Candles: one in glass with a saint printed on it, one in a jar, one tall and red. Two clocks.

Woven baskets. A pink box. Grey carpet. Paper crumpled and printed, torn strips, tissue, pink crepe, cut dark shapes. Cardboard boxes, two of them wrapped in black and white tape, a tube, a striped circle. Foil crumpled silver, gold, and an aluminum tray. Brooms, which is where the straw comes in.

Then the household metal: brass candlesticks, a silver tray, bowls, lamp bases, cans, scissors, a small sewing table with its machine still on it, a fan, two cowboy hats, one painted gold, a bracelet, a gold ring, a holographic fishing lure, a folding table, an electronic board with switches, a bicycle wheel. The household plastic: a water bottle, a cup, a lamp globe, combs, a toothbrush, sunglasses, goggles, hangers, a CD, a round white sign with a red cross, a piano keyboard, a green chair. One heap of household things gathered around a white-painted box holds a keyboard and a telephone. In another, seen from above, white and printed cloth, silver trays and brass candlesticks surround a dark jar wrapped in gold, standing in the middle as if it had been elected. And small tags with four-digit numbers lie on almost everything in one gallery, as if the whole heap were going to auction.

From the closet and the sewing basket came cloth, in every gallery but six. Satin, velvet, lace, sequins, tulle, chiffon, sheer white and sheer blue, knit, denim, brocade, canvas, floral, printed, striped, leopard. Ribbons, purple by the handful. Yarn fringe. Drapes, a worn cushion, a scarf, a white knit shawl, garments worn past wearing, caps and hats, an orange safety vest, a crocheted bird hung against white plastic. Scraps cut, torn, scorched and stained. Beside the cloth, beads, a necklace of them, a black row of them, one blue bead that was really a lens, and once a ring of white beads laid on a clear sheet lit from below. Shoes: high-heeled sandals under chicken wire, a red shoe, a leather one. A scrap of brown leather on its own. A belt.

From the toy box and the party: tinsel, and the plastic toys, which deserve their own sentence. White masks most of all, in nearly a dozen galleries, some painted, one with a black line drawing on it. Doll heads, nesting dolls, toy guns, a toy rocket, a toy drill, a toy spade, a dinosaur head, a boomerang, a rattle, a painted toy bird, a red heart, a rubber ball. Child's garbage, handled again.

From the body and from living things: long dark hair, a blond wig on white-painted boards, a long piece of orange and yellow fur with an open middle, a grey-brown fur. Leaves dry and green and wet, and one gallery that asks you to ignore them. Flowers, nearly all artificial, and one bunch dried. A potted succulent and a potted spiky plant, because a plant place is often part of a piece.

Now the things we were not sure of, which we would rather name than drop. White things in a heap that were probably bone. A horn, we think, and a pale branched piece that may have been antler. Shells, one large. White crystals on a black sheet that were salt or frost, and we no longer know which. A green apple, a yellow fruit, a round red-yellow one, and a lemon, real. Scattered seeds. A balloon or two, pink and yellow and red. One striped black and white feather. Moss. A sticker. A stuffed toad or frog. Hardened drips that look like wax. And worms, on black soil, in yellow light. The camera caught them. The eye that went back through the pictures could not swear to them. That is the honest state of the record.

Some galleries used nearly everything on this list and some used almost nothing. The widest heaps are Numbers Game Auction, Precious Wet Black Soil, Temporary Frozen Thrill, and a small set of tall narrow pictures called Unlabeled Package, where two taped cardboard cubes, a red candle, a doll head, long hair, dried flowers and glass jars stand together on a speckled slab. Woa Mun is the most varied inside itself: orange sticks in a web beside a dark table, a lacquered board with black patches, gravel over a purple sheet, the sewing table. The narrowest are the quietest. Above Stones Feugo is crumpled printed paper on dark soil and gravel, and little else. Cool Warming Pieces B is chicken wire laid over shoes and a scarf. Day Stage Textile Space is almost nothing but cloth, eight kinds of it, some of it hung against the sky. Eight things run through nearly every gallery and could be called the ground of the whole: cloth, plastic, wood, metal, wire, rope, stone, soil. Cord, in particular, holds more of these pieces together than anything else we own.

So that is the stuff. None of it is rare. Everything on this list is sitting right now in somebody's garage, ditch, closet or bin, not far from wherever you are reading this. We did not find it so much as stop throwing it away. We set it down with the other things and left it alone long enough to be looked at, and then the camera looked. Now you have the list. Go back to the pictures and name what you see.
