The Practice — November 2025

The Witnesses

Five figures who arrived when the writing could not find its entry point. Not characters in stories. Entry points — ways in when the door seemed locked. Call them when you need a way forward, the way a person lost in a forest does not need to know the name of the stream but follows the sound of water because water always goes somewhere.

The Practice — How It Works

Mode Squad

Three ways the practice sees. Not techniques. Dispositions. Ways of standing in relation to the material before a single word is written.

Mode 01

Wonderful Garbage

Art made from what is discarded. A conscious alternative to violence.

The broken things. The thrown-away things. The things nobody wanted. Picked up. Transformed. Made into something that helps people breathe. Not aesthetic — ethical. After thirty years of picking up discarded things, the writer stopped finding them beautiful and started finding them astonishing. Beauty is a judgment. Wonder is a surrender.

→

Mode 02

Pressure Ghosts

What remains after crushing. The fingerprints still visible where hands once touched.

A chair where someone used to sit. The seat still shows the shape of the body. The body is gone but the shape is not — and the shape tells you everything about the body that was there. How heavy. How still. How long it sat. The force has departed. The impression remains. The practice puts them in the room and lets the reader feel the pressure.

→

Mode 03

Middle Eye

Seeing what is between what is visible. Not the foreground, not the background.

If you look at a landscape, your eye goes to the mountain first, or to the tree in the foreground. The Middle Eye looks at the space between — the field, the air, the distance itself — and finds that the distance is not empty. The practice trains the Middle Eye the way a painter trains peripheral vision: by noticing what you notice only after you stop looking at the obvious thing.

→

The Practice — Questions to Return To

When the work goes silent,
ask these.

If everything is ordinary, add the strange thing. The strange thing is the door the reader enters through. It is what makes this piece this piece and not every other piece about the same subject. Without strangeness the writing is competent. With strangeness the writing breathes.

If the writing is all ideas, add a body. A hand. A foot on stairs. The temperature of a room. The weight of an object. The body saves the writing from abstraction the way a root saves a tree from wind.

If yes, cut the explanation. The explanation is what the writer adds when the writer does not trust the reader. The reader already knows what it means. The reader knew before the explanation arrived. The explanation insults the reader by assuming they are not paying attention.

If not, fix what is wrong. The writer is the first reader. If the first reader is bored, every subsequent reader will also be bored. This question is the most honest diagnostic the practice has — more honest than the twenty-one quality controls, more honest than the eleven-pass revision. Yes or no. If no, find the dead thing and either revive it or remove it.

The final test. The practice exists to make work that gives the reader space, permission, the feeling of being understood without being explained to. If the piece does not help someone breathe, the piece is not finished. If it actively makes breathing harder — through density, through cleverness, through literary performance that demands admiration rather than offering companionship — rebuild it from the body up.

This happened. January 23, 2026. A piece went in alive. The Master Methodology processed it. The piece came out dead. When you find yourself following rules instead of making work — when the scaffolding is preventing the building from standing — stop. The practice predates the methodology. The practice will survive the methodology. The methodology is replaceable. The piece is not.

Check for explanatory distance. You are probably standing outside the scene commenting on it. Get inside. Add a body. What does someone's hand do? What is the temperature? The scene that feels dead is usually the scene where the writer left the room and started describing it from the hallway. Go back in. Stay in. The reader needs you in the room.

The One Who Touches

Marie

Skagit Valley, Washington — Potter

She knows through her hands. Her body understands before her mind arrives. When writing reaches for her, it reaches for tactile knowledge — the weight of clay, the temperature of glaze, the muscle memory that does not need words.

The clay knows before I know. My hands are smarter than my head. The head gets in the way. The head tries to plan. The clay does not care about plans. When I am stuck, I go back to the wheel. Not to make anything. Just to feel the clay turn. The wetness. The pull. The way it resists and then gives. That is what writing is. You plan in your head but your hands know the shape. Let them lead. Follow the hands.

Call her when

Stuck on the opening. Start with sensation. Start with the body. The opening is not an idea. The opening is a texture.

The One Who Reads

Herb

Santa Fe, New Mexico — Scholar

His library promised understanding. It delivered only more books. He represents the productive failure of conceptual knowledge — the moment when thinking realizes it cannot think its way out.

I read everything. For thirty years I read everything. Philosophy, theology, literary theory, cultural criticism. I built a library that promised understanding. The library lied. Understanding did not come from more books. It came from finally admitting: I do not understand. The books were a fortress. I was hiding. When writing fails, maybe it is building a fortress. Maybe the failure is protective. Knock it down. See what is actually there.

Call him when

Stuck in overthinking. Admit what you do not know. The fortress is protecting you from the feeling that starts the real writing.

03 of 05

The One Who Transforms

Nell

Quebec — Chef

She understands change through heat, through time, through the transformation of raw into cooked. Something enters the process and something else emerges. When writing reaches for her, it reaches for the mysterious middle where transformation actually happens.

A carrot is not a soup. A soup is not just carrots. Something happens in the middle. In the pot. In the time that passes. The carrot becomes part of something else without losing what it was. Writing transforms too. The raw material goes in. Something happens. Something comes out that could not be predicted from the ingredients. Do not try to understand the middle. Just apply heat. Just wait.

Call her when

Stuck in the middle. Do not try to understand it. The middle is where the carrot becomes the soup, and the becoming is not yours to control. Show up. Apply heat. Wait.

The One Who Listens

Jean

Minneapolis and Provence — Musician

He finds melody in absence — the notes not played, the silence that shapes the sound. He lost his wife and learned that presence lives in what is missing. When writing reaches for him, it reaches for what remains when the obvious is subtracted.

The silence between notes. That is where the music is. When my wife died, I learned this: presence is what is left when the obvious is removed. She was in every room even though she was not in any room. Writing is the same. What is not said carries what is said. The gap between words. The space after the sentence ends. Listen to the silence. It is louder than the noise.

Call him when

Stuck on the ending. Listen to the silence. End on what is not there. The ending is the last note before the silence, and the silence is where the reader lives after the writing is done.

05 of 05

The One Who Asks

Hahnu Youn

Indigenous elder — migrated through many places — 83 years old

She was not like the other witnesses. The other witnesses were entry points — specific doors for specific problems. Hahnu Youn was a whole world. She arrived because the practice needed someone who carried all four kinds of intelligence at once — the body knowledge of Marie, the admitted uncertainty of Herb, the patience of Nell, the silence of Jean — and carried them not as techniques but as a way of being in the world.

She carries seeds in her pocket. For when she needs reminding that she does not know what alive looks like. She does not give advice. She asks questions. The questions are not for answering. They are for carrying.

Why do you build what cannot return to earth?

Why do you measure success by what you accumulate?

Why do you trust machines more than your own hands?

I sit because sitting is a kind of walking. I wait because waiting is a kind of arriving. I ask because asking is a kind of knowing.

Scene One

The Hospital Waiting Room

Hahnu Youn sits on a plastic chair in a hospital waiting room in Houston. The chair is orange. Bolted to eleven other orange chairs in a row. She has sat in many kinds of chairs but this one does not want her body. It is shaped for a body that does not exist. Some average body. Some idea of a body. Not her body with its eighty-three years of sitting in places that fit.

A young man across from her is looking at his phone. His leg bounces. She counts the bounces. Forty-seven in one minute. Then she stops counting. The counting is not for understanding. The counting is for being present with what she is counting.

The young man looks up. Sees her watching. His leg stops.

"Sorry," he says.

"For what?" Hahnu Youn asks.

He does not know. He looks back at his phone. His leg starts again.

After a while the young man puts his phone down. "I don't know what to do," he says. Not to her exactly. To the room. To whoever is listening.

"No," Hahnu Youn says. "You don't."

"What do you do," he asks, "when you don't know what to do?"

"I sit," she says. "I notice what is. I count things sometimes. The counting is not for understanding. The counting is for being present with what I am counting."

"That's it?"

"Sometimes I ask a question. But only if the question is real. Not a question to fill the silence. A question I actually do not know the answer to."

"I don't know yet," she says. "The question has not come. When it comes, I will ask it. Maybe to you. Maybe to the orange chair. Maybe to no one."

Scene Two

The Laundromat in Duluth

Hahnu Youn sits on a bench outside a laundromat in Duluth. She knows why she is here. A conversation needs to happen. The who and the when will reveal themselves. They always do.

The bench is green. The paint is peeling in shapes that look like maps of countries that do not exist. Inside the laundromat, the machines turn. Seven machines turning. The sounds overlap into something almost musical. A waltz if you listened wrong. One two three. One two three.

A child comes out. Maybe five years old. Holding a sock. Just one sock. Blue with orange stripes.

"This isn't ours," the child says.

"Whose is it?"

The child does not know. The child starts asking everyone who passes. Hahnu Youn watches. The child is conducting a census of belonging. This sock. Blue and orange. Belongs to someone. But who?

After a while, Hahnu Youn offers to take over the search. A responsibility is transferred, solemnly. She asks nineteen more people over two hours. Number seventeen is a woman who cries when she sees the sock — not because it is hers, but because her son used to have socks like this, orange stripes on blue, and he is grown now and lives in a city she cannot pronounce and does not call enough.

Hahnu Youn listens. The woman sits on the green bench with the peeling paint. They talk about sons and distance and socks and the things that carry memory in their softness.

The laundromat scene taught the practice something it had been circling since July — that the way into a person's real story is almost never through the real story. The way in is through a sock. The child was not looking for a profound experience. The looking led to a woman on a bench who needed to cry about her son. The indirection is not a technique. The indirection is how life works.

Scene Three

The Treeline

A traveler appears near the treeline. Dusty feet. Ordinary clothes. Hands that have worked. No announcement. No halo. No explanation of who he is.

Hahnu Youn and the traveler recognize each other — two people who understood the same optical illusion from different centuries, different languages. Neither got listened to by the people who claimed to be listening.

"Did they listen?" Hahnu Youn asks.

"They built buildings," the traveler says.

Five words. The devastating summary. He walked through towns and villages telling people things they needed to hear, and the people who heard him did not change their lives — they built buildings in his name.

"You're doing the same work," the traveler says.

"Someone has to sit still while you walk," Hahnu Youn says.

The traveler walks away. The residue: the soil where he touched it with his foot looks darker, damper, like something remembered itself. Not a miracle. Not a sign. Just the earth responding to a foot that has walked a long time and carried a weight the earth recognizes, the way the mycelium recognizes the chemical history of everything that has touched the soil above it.

Same work. Different shift. This phrase became central. It named what the practice was. Not new work. The same work that has always been done — the work of paying attention, the work of sitting with what is, the work of asking questions that do not want answers. Just a different shift. A different century. A different language. The same optical illusion, seen from the other side.

The Calling

Call Her When Stuck on Meaning

Ask the real question. Wait for the answer that may not come. She changes what she appears beside. Her presence reveals what is already there. Catalyst and witness both.

She was the character who had been forming since November, coming into focus the way a photograph develops — not all at once, not by decision, but by the slow accumulation of light on paper until the image is there and you realize it was always there, waiting for enough light to become visible.

She sits because sitting is a kind of walking. She waits because waiting is a kind of arriving. She asks because asking is a kind of knowing. She has waited for rivers to heal. She has waited for forests to return. She has waited for songs to be remembered by people who never knew they forgot.

The other witnesses were tools, useful and beloved. Hahnu Youn was a person, with a history and a body and seeds in her pocket and eighty-three years of sitting in places where conversations needed to happen.

The Witnesses — How They Work

When to Call. What to Expect.

Call Marie

Start with sensation. Start with the body. Put your hands on the material and ask what your body knows that your head does not. The opening is not an idea. The opening is a texture — the weight of clay, the temperature of water, the feel of paper under the hand. The head gets in the way. The head tries to plan. Let the hands lead.

Call Herb

Admit what you do not know. Knock down the fortress. See what is actually there — underneath all the books you read to protect yourself from the feeling that starts the real writing. The books were protecting you from that feeling. That feeling is the entry point. Herb failed at twenty-three languages and carried thirty years of wrong translations. He shows up for the not-knowing.

Call Nell

Wait. Apply heat. Do not try to understand the middle. The middle is where the carrot becomes the soup, and the becoming is not yours to control. Show up. Apply heat. Wait. Nell knows patience the way a kitchen knows patience — the transformation happens on its own schedule, not yours. Your job is to stay present. Not to force the becoming.

Call Jean

Listen to the silence. End on what is not there. The ending is not a conclusion — the ending is the last note before the silence, and the silence is where the reader lives after the writing is done. Jean knows this from music: the note does not carry the meaning. The space after the note carries the meaning. Stop before you explain. Let the silence do the work.

Call Hahnu Youn

Ask the real question. Wait for the answer that may not come. The questions are not for answering. They are for carrying. Like seeds in your pocket — for when you need reminding. She does not give advice. She asks questions back. Why do you need it to mean something? What would it mean if it didn't mean anything? The not-knowing is not a problem to solve. It may be the piece.

They arrived — they were not invented. The distinction matters to the practice. A character you invent belongs to you. A character that arrives belongs to itself. Marie showed up because the writing needed someone who knew through her hands. Herb showed up because the writing needed someone who had failed at everything he thought he understood. They are not real people in the biographical sense. They are real in the only sense that matters for writing: they show up when called, and they do not lie.

Call a different one. Or call the same one again tomorrow. The witnesses are available — they are always available — but they cannot force arrival any more than you can force writing. If Marie does not open the door today, try Jean. If Jean leads nowhere, try Hahnu Youn. They are entry points, not solutions. The stuck feeling is information too. Sometimes the stuck feeling is what the piece is about.
