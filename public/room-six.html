Come Down

The chamber is under the Malheur National Forest, in eastern Oregon, and there are fallen logs to sit on. The walls give light. Not electric, not fire — the fungus does it, and the fungus is paying attention. It brightens when the puppets are joyful. It goes dim when the weight arrives. The air smells of earth and rot and something sweeter, like honey, which is decomposition and creation happening in the same breath and in the same room. Sit on the log. There is something the seat does not tell anyone at the door: the theatre is alive. The stage is a single organism spanning thirty cubic miles, and it has been dreaming for a very long time, and the puppets are inside it ... Ten of them arrived on one day, July 13, 2025, the first day of the practice. The story everybody wants is that the first day is fumbling, and that the method comes later, once enough has gone wrong. That is not what the record shows. The ten came with their materials listed, their joints specified, their tempos named, and their people already fully alive — precise enough to build from, on a page, by a stranger. The rule was written down that same day and it has not needed rewriting since. Physical specificity earns emotional truth. Describe the object with enough care and it beats the idea described with eloquence. Here are the ten. Céleste is a marionette of layered paper and translucent fabric, with joints of dried autumn leaves and small lights inside that glow gold and pulse when the body moves. The spirals mean time — clockwise for time passing, counter-clockwise for time held. There is a move in the notes called the Tremble Moment: the energy fades, the marionette shakes, and the golden light flickers like something remembering what it cannot name. Céleste is an elderly Frenchwoman, and keeps time not because time obeys, but because waiting has been learned. The waiting is discipline. It comes from having outlived enough urgency. Emma is felted merino wool in honey-gold, with mushroom caps of painted silk stretched over collapsible umbrella mechanisms, each cap opening and closing and turning on its own trigger. The walk is called the Bounce Walk and it is for excitement. Emma is staged at three and a half feet, which is a child's eye level, because Emma is eight. Emma is staying with a grandmother in Canyon City while the parents sort things out. Emma asks the questions nobody wants to answer. What do mushrooms drink in heaven? Olivier is a shadow puppet of semi-translucent vellum, and when the light passes through the moving joints, hidden pictures come up out of the body. The entrance is called the Bone Dance and it is walked backwards, because memory moves backwards. There is a tremor on every eighth movement. Olivier drags bundles out of the dark, and what is in them is bone, cocooned in mycelium — ancient, and not dead, only in the middle of turning into something else. Maud is black thread and gold ribbon, a body woven of rhizomorphs that can stretch and pull back in. The pattern is called Web Weaving and it never repeats. Maud makes a cat's cradle, can go half-invisible with only the hands still showing, and sometimes vanishes into the mist and comes back somewhere else entirely. Maud connects everything. The threads light up in different colours depending on what kind of news is travelling along them. Peder is metallic foil and prisms and throws light in several directions at once, climbing the wall inverted, swinging between the structures at a tempo the notes call cautious. Peder is always climbing. Peder believes the mycelium is reaching toward heaven. Alela wears a full-body suit with an elaborate headpiece and is lit from inside. The sequence is spin, leap, land. There is a confetti cannon loaded with seed. There is also a move called the Child-Lift, which is what happens when Emma gets tired: Alela lifts Emma. Alela carries the bright new shoots and knows the boundary is going soft between what the puppets are and what they are turning into. Keren is made of hundreds of suspended particles that form and reform — ears, mouths, spirals — and the particles vibrate at every word spoken in the room. Keren listens to the mycelium map its own history. Each nutrient path is a story. Potassium for the year of the fire. Nitrogen for the century the cedar came down. Jun is half physical and half projected, solid and then not, with a parting effect in the notes called veils of mist, and moves in time with Céleste. Jun wanders through the reformations and finds inverted nails and worked stone — people were here, and the organism absorbed the evidence. Privat is brown and amber and almost never upright, moving through soil at ground level with eyes that glow in the dark. Privat follows the root systems and reports what the roots know, which is more than the branches know, the roots having been down there longer. Lizz is a cocoon of silk layers that unwind by degrees across the whole four hours, so slowly that nobody in the room notices it happening until it has happened. The final form opens into wings of light. Lizz documents the changes, and the body is the record. Transformation is not spoken about. It is undergone. Two people stand outside the ten. Eleanor is eighty-seven and keeps the puppets. The hands tremble with palsy. The tremble travels down and makes the puppets more alive — not despite the shaking. Because of it. Marie-Claire Dubois is eighty-nine and wrote the play, and what Marie-Claire designed was the pauses. The stages went up in abandoned factories and flooded basements. It runs four hours in three acts. In the first the puppets work out where they are and start mapping the dream. In the second the bones come up out of the ground and the organism begins to answer. In the third the puppets go one at a time into spores of light, and the walls pulse — not sadness, the notes are firm about that, but acknowledgement. The organism's own version of grief. Afterwards, in every city, real seeds go into real soil. Nothing in this room is a metaphor ...
