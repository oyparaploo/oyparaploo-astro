Chapter Thirteen ~ The Marble Corridor

Words / 2025 Variety

Vienna took sound seriously. It wrapped it in carpet, tucked it behind heavy doors, taught it to behave in rooms with names longer than the meetings inside them. Even the trams that chimed their delicate warnings along the Ringstraße seemed to do so with good posture.

Troll Tearsen and Libaux Torrini arrived at a building on the canal whose entrance smelled faintly of lemon polish and old decisions. Marble caught their footsteps and returned them in tidy replicas—tok… tok—before a runner absorbed the rest and pretended they had never been there. The security gate beeped in a key that suggested guilt had been tuned out of the scale.

Inside, the delegation floor wore three languages on its walls and fifty on the breath behind closed doors. A sound-masking system exhaled a fine, lawful shhh, engineered to make conversations private and applause theoretical. A gavel somewhere came down without drama and became a rumor.

Uma Arouje waited in a small conference room where the glass didn’t reflect, it judged. She stood beside a table that had already been set with water as if for apology. When Troll and Libaux entered, she did not offer chairs; the chairs offered themselves and then were ignored.

“You are late,” Uma said, voice even, consonants crisp enough to make paper. “And you are not in possession of what I requested.”

“When we have it,” Libaux said, “it will no longer be a request.”

Uma considered him the way a museum guard considers a tourist who believes the cordon is suggestion. “You tampered with a philanthropic broadcast,” she said to Troll, “and turned it into a public-relations incident.”

“We returned the room to itself,” Troll said. “It remembered how to breathe.”

Uma folded her arms, sleeves whispering. “Your metaphor does not travel well in policy. Neither does grief.”

There was no cruelty in it; it was worse—a caution given as if it could be taken back if it went unused. Troll placed both palms on the table, spread fingers to steady the tremor that didn’t show. “You will get your deliverable,” she said. “But not if we continue pretending that oversight and outcomes live in different buildings.”

A pause. The sound masking hummed like a small, loyal instrument. Uma’s face softened one degree deeper than zero. “I’m sorry about your mother,” she said, soft enough that the system failed to believe it was part of the conversation and didn’t try to hide it. Then the degree vanished. “There is a plenary in forty minutes. You will attend with your credentials. You will not improvise.”

“We brought the right shoes,” Libaux said. It was almost a joke, so it almost helped.

Uma left them their badges. When the door closed, the shhh sighed as if pleased to be alone with itself again.

---

The plenary hall separated speakers from consequences with architecture. Teal upholstery absorbed politics. Translators’ booths perched at the back with their own private acoustics, glass-fronted fishbowls in which syntax could be cleaned and fed to delegates through earpieces. The ceiling suspended disks that held light the way good hands hold good glass.

Troll sat with her badge turned inward and the interpreter channel muted. She wanted the room naked. Libaux sat two seats over, enough distance to make them look like colleagues who did not yet trust each other. On the dais, nameplates smiled the practiced smile of metal. The agenda moved forward on invisible rails.

Stremler appeared as a harmless noun: special adviser, process monitor, a person who could be invited anywhere because his function sounded like it had no door. He wore the neutrality of a good window. When he leaned toward the microphone, the room did not object.

“We are here,” he said, voice warmer now than in Paris, “to improve what we are already doing well.”

Translation arrived a half-beat later in French, then German, then another English with a different country under it. In Troll’s bare air, the original rode unaccompanied. She could hear the sound system’s veil breathing along the floor, a low mmmm that made edges less truthful.

Libaux tapped an earpiece, listened to the German channel for a sentence, then set it down like a careful toy. He leaned over just enough for his voice to belong to no one. “Same under-floor. Different trick.”

“Different orchestra pit,” Troll said.

She reached into her pocket and let her fingers close around the seeded stone she’d taken from the warehouse tray. It cooled her palm like an object that wished to be innocent.

The first presentation was about numbers that tried to be merciful. The second was about transparency that never quite reached nouns. The third was Stremler again, who praised the first two without praising anything and thanked unnamed colleagues for work that made harm harder to count. He used the plural like a charm.

“Outcomes are unpredictable,” he said, “but processes can be perfected.” The hall, trained to accept an aphorism if it arrived wearing a suit, accepted it.

Troll slid the marble onto the floor beside her shoe. Even in a room designed to eat small sounds, the tik it made when it kissed tile was a heresy. Heads turned one degree and pretended not to have. A delegate from somewhere Nordic smiled into his sleeve as if a child had done something clever in church and shouldn’t be encouraged.

Uma, on the aisle, didn’t turn. Her posture made a wall that had been planned years ago and installed carefully. Troll considered not dropping the stone again. Then she did it anyway. tik.

Stremler did not pause. The sound system swallowed the treason efficiently. Troll lifted the marble back into her hand. She felt foolish and alive.

Libaux pointed slightly with his chin to the booths. “Interpreters,” he murmured, “live in the only rooms here where echo is allowed. We should live there.”

They stood as if for coffee and climbed the stairs as if hires. The booth for English sat open; a technician was away collecting a bottle of water that would soon be confused with someone else’s. Troll slid into the chair. The headset smelled of plastic and caution. Through the glass, the plenary hall looked like an aquarium where applause had been trained to remain in its cave.

She pressed the talk key and didn’t speak. The hall didn’t notice her non-voice. When she did speak, it was not into the hall at all but into the recording channel that fed archive. She said her mother’s name. In the little room it sounded bold; in the headphones it sounded like a term of art without a category. The recorder’s red light held its patient dot.

Libaux tapped a fader labeled FLOOR and lowered it a tittle; the interpreter channel breathed a fraction more on its own. The gulf between what was said and what was meant became audible if you were the kind of person who had come to learn that difference.

Below, Stremler told a story about a front-line worker in a town with a sea. He did not say Collioure, but his vowels held wind. He said liaise as if the word did not bruise when touched. He said oversight the way a city says statue.

Troll looked at his hands. They were clean, unmarked by labor that would have taught them to behave differently when the truth was heavy. She slid the seeded stone across the console surface. The tik was too small for the room and large enough for her. She wanted to throw it; she wanted to bite it; she wanted to walk down the center aisle and set it on the dais beside his notes like a punctuation mark that would decide the sentence by refusing to move. She did none of those.

Uma appeared in the glass like a reflection uneager to be acknowledged. She entered the booth without knocking, closed the door carefully, and took the headset away from Troll’s ears with a gentleness that came too late to be permission.

“You will not,” Uma said, “turn this building into a tool for your private war.”

“My war is public,” Troll said. “He made it that way.”

Uma held her eyes a long moment. Then: “He is in my meeting room at fourteen hundred. If you stay between the lines until then, I will allow you to be present.”

Libaux said, without looking up from the console, “That’s not how allowing works.”

Uma ignored him. “Between the lines,” she repeated, and left a silence behind her that had already been moved onto the agenda.

---

At 14:00, the meeting room had round corners that discouraged corners of conversations. Stremler sat at the far end without claiming it; the end claimed him. Three aides wore their invisible work like natural illumination. Uma introduced Troll and Libaux with names and no adjectives. Water waited in thin glasses that clicked against coasters with polite ambitions.

“Detective Tearsen,” Stremler said, as if greeting a physician who would eventually agree with him. “Vienna looks well on you.”

“It looks like you,” Troll said. “Which is not a compliment.”

He folded his hands. If the gesture noticed the marbles in her pocket, it did so historically, not presently. “You’ve become adept at sound. It’s a common detour for people who cannot yet tell the difference between noise and narrative.”

“Your narrative is made of noise,” Libaux said. “You just pay better speakers.”

Uma’s glance was a placard: stop. Libaux smiled without teeth and put his hands flat on the table so they could be counted.

Stremler turned toward Troll. Not a full turn—just enough to pretend to be granting her his time. “You have a loss,” he said softly, and the words did not apologize for having learned about it. “I am sorry.”

She had prepared. She had prepared and still his use of the sentence reached into the wrong room. She let the air hold the reply she did not give.

“We are not adjudicators,” he said, louder, into the record. “We are facilitators of paths. You know this. You work for the same ideas I do, when you are not rewriting the acoustics.”

“Then show me a path that ends in a child who was found,” Troll said. “Not a memo. Not a ribbon-cutting. A door with a person behind it.”

His jaw moved and did nothing. He tapped the table once, like a musician checking the tempo before joining a piece already in progress. “Outcomes are messy,” he said, almost regretfully. “I care for clean.”

“Clean is where rot hides,” Libaux said.

Uma’s voice found an evenness that had rebuilt itself five times in three minutes. “We all care for clean,” she said. “We also care for outcomes. We will align as the mandate demands. In the meantime, there will be no further theatrics in our venues.”

“Between the lines,” Stremler said, and the phrase sounded like something he had said to rooms all his life.

“Between the rails,” Troll said. “Until we learn to jump them.”

“Learning is good,” he said, and stood, and ended the meeting with the tone of a conductor closing the first half, certain the audience would return after wine.

He left them a smell of pleasant soap and very little else.

Uma stayed until the door closed. Then: “If you are going to break something,” she said, “break it so well that it cannot be mended quietly.”

Libaux’s mouth twitched. “Permission?”

“An observation,” she said, and walked toward her next crisis as if the floor had told her where it would be.

---

They stood in a corridor where the marble returned everything in measured copies. A delegation passed, their shoes a fluent tok-tok; a pen fell and the sound rang longer than it had reason to. The sound-masking system thinned here, as if the building had forgotten to be careful in its own ribs.

Troll took the seeded stone from her pocket. She rolled it onto the baseboard. tik. It sounded louder than it was—a heresy in a carpeted church. The corridor accepted it and sent a sliver further than she had asked. It was not Paris. It was not Collioure. It was a room that believed in polite echoes and had not wholly succeeded in abolishing them.

Her phone buzzed. Helene Klausmun:

> We found another at the Fontanella shelter this morning. No body. The marble is clear with a fine bubble seam. Scratches read like initials, but I’m not trusting them yet. The valley is listening. So am I.

Another buzz. Aïcha:

> Fondation Rivage filings show a subsidiary registered here under a different spelling. RIVAGE → RIVAJE. Your keypad map holds. He is still writing in glass.

A third. Mage Meuller:

> Sent you a spectrum. The seeded stone you muted last night sings a B nearly sharp when rolled on oak, an A flat on steel. On marble: a clean E. Tell me if the corridor says E. If it does, the “marble corridor” isn’t just poetry. It’s calibration.

Troll rolled the stone again, listening past her own blood. tik. The return found the rib of the wall and rang small, pure.

“E,” she said.

“E,” Libaux repeated, not doubting that she was right. “So he tunes buildings.”

“He expects us to play them.”

“Then we play wrong.”

Troll put the marble away. The corridor went back to pretending it was a place where decisions sound like nothing at all.

“You said earlier,” Libaux began.

“I know,” she said. “We will go back.” She meant the valley. The hospice without the patient. The bench where a marble might be left now the way flowers are. “After we finish with this room.”

He nodded. Neither of them said what it meant to finish with a room that replenished itself by design.

The meeting behind them let out. The hallway filled with polite voices, five kinds of translation, and a laugh that traveled well between languages. Stremler passed at a distance that could be measured in titles. He did not look at them, which was how he looked at them. The corridor held its echo for him with professional respect.

Troll said her mother’s name under her breath, and the corridor returned it thin but audible, a copy with its edges worn.

Libaux checked his watch and, finding it told nothing urgent, let his hand fall. “Between lines,” he said.

“Between rails,” she answered.

The marble corridor answered too, in its small way, and then remembered itself.
