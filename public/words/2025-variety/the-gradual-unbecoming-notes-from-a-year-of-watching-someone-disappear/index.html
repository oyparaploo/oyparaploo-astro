The Gradual Unbecoming

Words / 2025 Variety

Notes from a Year of Watching Someone Disappear

---

Maya stopped answering her phone in March. By July, she'd stopped answering to her name.

I found her in the kitchen of her mother's house, sorting pills by color instead of purpose. The windows were covered with newspaper from 2019. She'd drawn faces in the condensation on every glass surface—different versions of herself, she said, from timelines where she'd made better choices.

"I'm conducting an experiment," she told me, not looking up from her rainbow pharmacy. "If I take enough different medications at once, maybe they'll cancel each other out and I'll feel nothing."

That's not how chemistry works, but I didn't correct her. By then, I'd learned that Maya's logic operated on frequencies the rest of us couldn't hear.

---

The First Disappearance

It started with small things. She'd forget words mid-sentence, replacing them with gestures that almost made sense. She'd show up to work in clothes from different decades—her grandmother's 1960s shift dress, her teenage brother's skateboard shoes, a coat she insisted belonged to someone she hadn't met yet.

"I'm not myself today," became her standard greeting. After a while, we stopped asking which self she was instead.

Kai from IT started tracking her changes in a spreadsheet. Not maliciously—he had a brother with schizophrenia and recognized something in Maya's drift. "She's not psychotic," he told me over terrible office coffee. "She's just... becoming permeable."

---

The House as Archive

Maya's childhood home sat on a corner lot in the part of town where developers hadn't arrived yet. Her mother had died two years earlier, leaving Maya the house and a collection of unfinished craft projects that filled every room like archaeological layers.

Half-knitted scarves. Canvases with single brushstrokes. A quilt with one perfect corner and yards of loose fabric. Maya moved through these fragments like a curator, occasionally adding her own incomplete contributions—a poem that ended mid-word, a meal prepared but never eaten, letters addressed to people whose names she'd invented.

"My mother was always starting things," Maya explained, holding a ceramic bowl that was really just hardened clay with good intentions. "I'm finishing them by leaving them exactly as they are."

---

The Painter

Zara lived next door, an art student who paid rent by house-sitting for people who never traveled. She started painting Maya without permission, quick watercolors from her bedroom window. Maya in the garden, pulling up flowers instead of weeds. Maya on the roof at 3 AM, having conversations with satellites. Maya carrying groceries she'd never cook into a house she'd stopped cleaning.

"You're documenting my dissolution," Maya said when she discovered the paintings.

"I'm documenting your reconstruction," Zara countered.

They were both right, which was the problem.

---

The Religion of Unraveling

Maya's grandmother had been devout, the kind of Catholic who saw God's hand in burnt toast and parking spaces. Maya inherited the impulse but not the faith. She created her own theology:

- Every dropped call was a message from the universe
- Expired milk was a reminder about impermanence
- Broken shoelaces meant it was time to stop running
- Finding pennies was the universe's way of saying "you're almost worthless, but not quite"

She built altars from discarded things: bottle caps, dead batteries, shopping lists strangers had dropped. "Someone believed these mattered once," she'd say, arranging them like relics.

---

The Support Group

Dr. Chen ran a Wednesday night group for "individuals experiencing reality differently." The fluorescent lights in the community center made everyone look like they were already ghosts.

There was Asha, who believed she was slowly becoming electromagnetic radiation and wore rubber gloves to contain herself. Marcus, who spoke only in questions since his divorce. Beth, who'd started aging backwards emotionally and now threw tantrums in grocery stores despite being forty-three.

Maya attended twice. The first time, she sat silently and took notes in a language she'd invented. The second time, she brought cookies that tasted like disappointment and gave a presentation about how everyone in the room was actually the same person at different stages of breakdown.

"We're all just decay pretending to be growth," she concluded, then left and never came back.

Dr. Chen called it progress.

---

The Storm Pattern

Kai discovered it by accident, overlaying Maya's behavioral changes with weather data. She disappeared during high pressure systems. Became manic during thunderstorms. Spoke in complete sentences only when it was exactly 70 degrees.

"I'm barometric," Maya explained when he showed her the correlation. "Atmospheric pressure presses me into different shapes."

She started watching weather forecasts obsessively, not to plan but to understand who she'd be tomorrow. Low pressure meant she'd speak in metaphors. Cold fronts brought clarity that hurt. Humidity made her permeable to other people's thoughts.

"Tuesday will be partly cloudy with a chance of becoming my mother," she'd announce, then prepare by wearing her mother's perfume and cooking foods she hated.

---

The Love That Wasn't

There had been someone, before the unraveling. Jordan, who worked at the library and brought Maya books she didn't ask for but always needed. They'd dated for three years, lived together for two, split up over something so mundane neither could remember what it was.

"We eroded," Maya explained to the empty chair she still set at dinner. "Not dramatically. Just... grain by grain until there was nothing left but the shape of what we'd been."

Jordan had moved to Portland. Maya kept their shared Netflix account and watched their viewing history backwards, trying to pinpoint when they'd stopped choosing movies together.

---

The Mother Archive

I helped Maya sort through her mother's things in August, when the heat made the house smell like old grief and lavender potpourri. We found:

- Seventeen journals, each with only the first page written
- A wedding dress for a wedding that never happened
- Baby shoes for a child who didn't survive
- Love letters from three different men, none of them Maya's father
- A suicide note dated 1987, obviously never used

"She was rehearsing endings her whole life," Maya said, holding the yellowed note. "I'm rehearsing beginnings that never begin."

We made a museum of it all, displaying her mother's almosts in the living room like art. Visitors (there were no visitors) could walk through and see a life built from interrupted intentions.

---

The Pregnancy That Wasn't

In September, Maya convinced herself she was pregnant with an idea that would save her. She took prenatal vitamins, avoided alcohol, played classical music to her stomach.

"It's not a baby," she explained patiently. "It's a new self gestating. When it's ready, I'll give birth to who I'm supposed to be."

Zara painted her during this period—Madonna with child that didn't exist, glowing with purpose that wasn't real. The paintings were beautiful and terrible, like watching someone drown in shallow water while insisting they're learning to breathe.

The idea was never born. Maya mourned it properly, with a ceremony in the backyard involving burning sage and burying a doll she'd made from her own hair.

---

The Final Form

By November, Maya had developed a routine that was really just organized chaos:

- Morning: Wake up and decide which version of herself to be
- Noon: Realize she'd chosen wrong
- Afternoon: Attempt to switch selves mid-day
- Evening: Give up and become no one
- Night: Make plans to be someone better tomorrow

She'd stand in front of mirrors practicing different faces, trying to find one that felt true. She'd introduce herself with different names to delivery drivers. She'd speak in accents she'd learned from YouTube videos about places she'd never visit.

"I'm not losing my mind," she insisted. "I'm just discovering I never had one. Just a collection of habits pretending to be a personality."

---

The Witness

I kept visiting because someone had to see it happen. Someone had to document that Maya existed, even as she insisted she didn't.

She taught me things:
- How to make tea that tastes like regret (oversteep it while thinking about mistakes)
- How to walk through rooms like you're not sure they're real (heel to toe, testing each step)
- How to have conversations with people who aren't there (address the space they should occupy)
- How to disappear while remaining visible (stop responding to your own thoughts)

"You're the only one who doesn't try to fix me," she said one day, arranging dead flowers in a vase with no water.

"I'm just taking notes," I replied.

"Same thing," she said, and maybe it was.

---

December

The house was foreclosed. Maya had stopped paying the mortgage months ago, not from lack of money but because she'd forgotten she existed in a system that required participation.

She moved in with Zara, who'd turned her second bedroom into a studio for painting Maya's transformations. The canvases covered every wall—Maya as smoke, as water, as a collection of loose parts that might have been a person once.

"I'm becoming abstract," Maya announced, looking at herself multiplied across canvases.

She was right. Each day she became less solid, more suggestion. She'd start sentences that trailed into gesture. She'd begin tasks that dissolved into staring. She'd make plans that evaporated before they could disappoint her.

---

The Email

Jordan wrote from Portland in January. A simple message: "I dreamed about you. You were yourself again."

Maya stared at it for three days before responding: "Which self? I've been all of them. None of them fit."

Jordan didn't write back. Maya didn't expect them to.

---

What Remains

I have:
- Kai's spreadsheets tracking her patterns
- Zara's paintings showing her transformations
- Dr. Chen's notes marked "treatment resistant"
- Her mother's incomplete projects, now doubly abandoned
- Voice messages where she forgot what she was saying mid-word
- Photos where she looks like different people depending on the angle

Maya, though—Maya I don't have. She's become exactly what she always said she was: a collection of moments that don't add up to a person, a story that refuses to conclude, a question that isn't looking for an answer.

She still lives with Zara. Still takes pills sorted by color. Still practices being different people in mirrors that show the same face making the same confused expression.

"I'm not crazy," she told me the last time we spoke. "I'm just honest about not existing."

I wanted to argue, but she was already walking away, becoming smaller with each step, until she was just another stranger in a city full of people pretending they know who they are.

---

The Moral

There isn't one. That's the point. Maya taught me that not every dissolution is a tragedy. Sometimes people just come apart, like old books losing pages, like houses settling into their foundations, like names you forget were ever important.

She's still out there, being everyone and no one, proving that identity is just a story we tell ourselves until we can't remember the beginning anymore.

I still take notes. Still document. Still witness.

It's the kindest thing I can do for someone who's chosen to disappear in plain sight.
