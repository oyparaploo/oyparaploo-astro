Other Makers

The Best Wild Cat There Ever Was (long version)

A lynx is agile. Has keen senses, excellent vision. They meow and growl. This wild cat is the best there ever was.

Was. The tense shifts while nobody's watching.

The franchise began in 1999, which in women's professional sports might as well be the Mesozoic. Most leagues fold after three and a half years—a life cycle shorter than a presidential term, than high school, than the time it takes to pay off a car. This one survived twenty-five years. Not through magic or smart marketing but through the specific stubbornness of women who were told no so many times that yes became irrelevant. They just kept showing up.

Tuesday, 3:47 PM. Practice should have ended at three-thirty but nobody notices. The coach watches them run play forty-three—a pick and roll that dissolves into something gorgeous and unnamed. They could run it unconscious. Have run it unconscious, actually, during that game in Seattle when the point guard played through a concussion nobody knew about until she vomited in the locker room after.

Light breaks through gymnasium windows and does something to their bodies. Makes them into stained glass. Here's amber where morning practice sweat dried into salt crystals. Here's silver where fresh perspiration rises. Here's the purple-green-yellow progression of bruises—impact, pooling blood, healing. The shooting guard has one shaped like Australia on her thigh. She's named it. Calls it her seventh continent.

The coach opens her mouth to say something about their dominance, about maintaining it, about not letting it slip. But her throat does something unexpected. Makes a sound like a door that doesn't want to close. What comes out instead: "I don't want this to—" She stops. They know she doesn't mean the dominance. She means them. These specific women in this specific configuration that will never exist again after this season.

Numbers, because numbers feel clean: They've won 70.1% of their games across twelve seasons. For context, the Lakers at their Showtime peak managed 68.2%. Jordan's Bulls, the team every other team gets compared to, hit 64.7% and entered mythology. But here's the thing about women's excellence—it conjugates differently. They don't dominate, they achieve. They don't destroy, they defeat. Language does this thing where it softens female victory, makes it polite.

Fifth title. That's what they're hunting before the expansion draft, before contracts expire, before their bodies finally say the thing they've been whispering for three years: enough. Two rookies from other teams will get selected. The league will scatter these women like dandelion seeds—Portland, Atlanta, Chicago, somewhere in Texas that hasn't been decided yet. The general manager has a spreadsheet that calculates who stays, who goes. It factors in age, salary, potential. Doesn't factor in the way number twelve knows exactly when number four needs help without looking. Can't quantify that.

Last year should have been theirs. Five bad calls in the final game. Not subjective calls—the kind where the replay shows the referee looking directly at the foul and swallowing the whistle. The point guard's body hit hardwood and the sound carried. Through the arena, into the tunnel, out to the parking lot where later she'd sit in her car screaming at the steering wheel until her voice turned to whispers. The center stood in the shower until the hot water ran out, then kept standing there. The forward texted her wife "coming home" then sat in the arena garage for two hours with the engine off, memorizing the concrete wall in front of her.

That whistle lives in their bodies now. Phantom pain during practice. Sharp reminders during film study when they see similar plays. The body remembers what the mind pretends to forget. Ankles remember every inversion. Knees catalog each betrayal. The forward's surgically repaired shoulder predicts weather changes better than radar.

But watch how they play. Not with overwhelming talent—though they have it—but with something harder to name. Their movement isn't basketball, it's origami, each fold revealing a shape that was always there, waiting. The point guard passes to the shooting guard without looking because she knows—knows in her bones, in her muscle memory—exactly where she'll be. Five women becoming one organism, not through planning but through repetition so constant it's become autonomic, like breathing during a full-court press.

Number thirty-two tears her ACL in February. They all hear it—that wet branch sound that means everything changes. She comes back in October different. Not worse, not better. Different. There's metal in her knee now. She sets off airport security. Jokes about it. Doesn't joke about the dreams where she's running and her leg dissolves beneath her.

They know what's coming. The expansion draft will take at least three of them. Maybe four. The math is simple and ruthless. They think about it during practice, during breakfast, during sex, during the national anthem when they stand with linked arms and the forward's daughter sings from the stands, her voice high and perfect and unaware that this is ending.

The rookies on the bench watch everything. Memorize the veterans' routines—who needs coffee before speaking, who ices her knees prophylactically, who throws up before big games, who prays, who doesn't, who pretends not to pray but does. They're trying to learn something that can't be taught. Chemistry isn't science here, it's alchemy. It's the center knowing the forward needs three seconds of silence before free throws. It's the shooting guard's wife bringing extra sandwiches because the point guard forgets to eat when stressed. It's sitting on the bus through Indiana and understanding that nobody needs to talk right now, that silence can be its own kind of love.

Someone—a reporter, probably—calls them sisters. The forward laughs until she cries. Or cries until she laughs. Sisters don't choose each other. These women built what they have from nothing. From 2 AM ice baths where they'd sit with their knees touching because the tub was too small. From defending each other against coaches, media, their own front office. From that time in Detroit when the power went out and they played cards by phone light until 4 AM, nobody wanting to break the spell by suggesting sleep.

The coach makes them feel not good enough. This is her job. Also makes them feel special. This is also her job. She breaks them to rebuild them, breaks them again, until the breaking becomes its own kind of making.

"Special" is a word that holds too many futures. Special like temporary. Special like marked. Special like blessed and cursed with the same breath.

The highway to a championship looks like construction zones. Lanes ending without warning. The GPS insisting on roads that no longer exist. Orange cones everywhere that seem to lead nowhere but somehow mark the path. They've been here before. Four times, actually. But this time feels different because everybody knows it's the last time with this exact group, in this exact configuration of bodies and hearts and histories.

They're hungry. The kind of hungry that lives in bones. February lynx in chest-deep snow hungry, where every calorie burned hunting might be the one that tips you into starvation. They were girls who were told they wanted too much—too much attention, too much money, too much recognition. Then they were told they weren't enough—not fast enough, not strong enough, not marketable enough. The criticism became white noise, the soundtrack to their becoming.

Now they're women who work basketball camps in July heat, teaching ten-year-olds how to box out. Who explain that professional means getting paid, but doesn't mean getting paid enough. The rookie makes $64,000 a year. The veteran max is $230,000. The men's rookie minimum is $1.1 million. These numbers live in their throats. They swallow them daily like pills without water.

During practice, somebody's body crashes into somebody else's. They stay tangled for a second longer than necessary. Not sexual, not romantic. Something else. Recognition, maybe. Or gratitude. Or the physical acknowledgment that they're here, together, still.

Twelve games remain in the regular season. Playoffs could add twenty more. Or could add two. Thirty-six more practices, give or take. Four more plane rides where they'll sit in the same configurations they've maintained for three years—the point guard and center playing cards, the forwards watching film on an iPad, the shooting guard asleep against the window making small sounds that might be words or might be dreams.

The ball spins midair during the Tuesday scrimmage. For a second—less than a second—it hangs there, rotating. Each turn catches light differently. Nobody notices except the coach, who sees everything, who will remember this moment in twenty years when someone asks about the best team she ever coached and she won't be able to explain it, will just say "they were special" and know that word holds everything and nothing.

After practice officially ends, they don't leave. Sit on the court like high schoolers, backs against the padding under the basket. Someone's knee makes a sound like bubble wrap. Someone laughs—not at the knee but at something the rookie says about her date last night, how the woman didn't know what the WNBA was, asked if it was "like basketball but easier?"

The janitor turns off half the lights. They don't move. The shooting guard's daughter runs onto the court, still in her school uniform, and they pass her the ball, watch her shoot with her whole body, the way kids do before anyone teaches them proper form. She makes it. They cheer like it's game seven.

This isn't about basketball. Hasn't been for a while.

The janitor turns on all the lights again. Says he found something that might be theirs, leads them to the lost and found box in his closet. It's full of mouth guards, hair ties, a single sock, someone's grandmother's funeral program from two months ago, three phones with cracked screens. Nothing's theirs but they look through it all anyway. The forward finds a practice jersey from six years ago, number eighteen. That player's in Los Angeles now. Has two kids.

"Remember her jumper?" someone says.
"Remember her defense?" someone else says.
They remember.

The shooting guard's daughter falls asleep on the bleachers, still clutching the ball. Her mother carries her to the car, and they all walk out together because that's what they do. In twelve games this won't be true anymore. In twelve games plus playoffs plus summer, they'll be scattered across the country, learning new plays, new teammates' names, new cities' streets.

The parking lot is empty except for them. The rookie realizes she left her keys inside, has to bang on the door until the janitor hears. They wait with her. Nobody says much. The center's stomach growls and the forward offers her a protein bar that's been in her bag since October. It tastes like cardboard and peanut butter. She eats it anyway.

When the rookie finally gets her keys, they separate to their cars but don't leave. Sit there with engines running. The point guard rolls down her window and yells something about Thursday's flight time. They all know the flight time. She's really saying something else.

One by one they drive away. Tomorrow they'll be back. Same time. Same place. The lynx will remember how to hunt in a pack for a little while longer.

That's enough.

That has to be enough.
