Grief, Resistance, and Renewal

The heat in Valledupar clings to the skin, heavy and unrelenting, saturating the streets with an almost tangible weight. Vendors under sagging tarps wipe their brows with damp cloths, their hands coarse and cracked from years of toil. Fruit piled high on wooden carts grows sticky in the sunlight, and the rhythmic stomp of security boots cuts through the air like an iron pulse. La Tramacúa Penitentiary looms in the distance, its concrete walls streaked with blackened rain and time’s relentless decay. Rust flakes off the razor wire that coils atop its perimeter, the corroded metal cutting faintly into the pale sky.

In a corner shaded by the skeletal remains of an old jacaranda tree, a child kneels on cracked pavement, her small fingers smudged with chalk dust as she carefully draws petals around a bright yellow flower. The chalk crumbles in her grip, but she doesn’t stop. She works quietly, as if the vibrancy of her drawing might push back against the shadows that seem to stretch endlessly across her world. Nearby, her mother’s hands tremble as she wraps bruised mangos in newspaper. She sets them gently into a basket, its wicker frayed and uneven. Her gaze drifts toward the distant silhouette of La Tramacúa, her heart tightening in her chest.

“Why did they take Papa so far away?” the little girl asks, her voice barely above a whisper. The mother’s grip on the basket tightens until her knuckles whiten, the question twisting painfully inside her. “Because they said it would be better,” she answers softly, though the lie feels sharp against her tongue. Her shoulders sag as she pulls her daughter into an embrace, the woven fibers of her blouse pressing roughly against the child’s cheek. The warmth of their closeness is the only solace she can offer.

A Prophet in the Shadows

Do not let them convince you otherwise.”

Her fingers press against the basket handle, its rough texture grounding her as the prophet’s words stir something deep within her.

The Prophet’s Words

The prophet steps forward, his staff pressing lightly into the cracked earth as he approaches the vigil. His presence carries a quiet gravity, drawing the eyes of those gathered. “Do you know why this grief feels so heavy?” he asks, his voice steady, unyielding.

The grandmother looks up, her weathered face marked with tears. “Because we love them,” she says, her vulnerability breaking through her words.

The prophet nods. “Yes. But it also feels heavy because they know you love them. That is what they fear most—love cannot be confined. Love erodes walls. It is stronger than chains.”

He turns toward the prison, its jagged silhouette framed by the dying light of evening. “Empires build cells, fortresses, chains. They call them protections, but they are monuments to fear. And fear crumbles, just as these walls will.”
