The Heat in Valledupar

The heat clings with a vengeance, wrapping the town in its suffocating grip as though daring anyone to fight back. Vendors wipe sweat from their brows with cloths too worn to absorb anything anymore. Their hands—scarred, hardened, permanent artifacts of survival—arrange fruit that softens in the relentless sun, tempting flies that hover like scavengers. The cracked pavement groans under the rhythm of boots that announce authority with every step. La Tramacúa Penitentiary rises nearby, looming like a shadow of human failure, its concrete walls streaked with the residue of blackened rain. Razor wire coils above it, rusting thorns stretching toward a sky they’ll never reach.

In the shade of a skeletal jacaranda tree, its branches brittle and reluctant, a little girl crouches on the fractured ground. Her chalk falls apart in her fingers, but she keeps drawing—a bright yellow flower surrounded by petals trembling under the weight of hope. “Mama, does it look happy?” she asks softly, her voice reaching for something she can’t name.

Her mother’s hands tremble as she wraps bruised mangos in newspaper barely holding together. The fruit isn’t the only thing bruised here; her gaze wanders to the prison, her chest tightening as she exhales the weight of their lives.

“Why did they send Papa there?” the girl whispers, pressing her chalk-streaked fingers against her mother’s arm. The basket shifts, the wicker biting faint lines into the woman’s palm.

“They said it was better,” she whispers, swallowing the bitterness that scrapes her throat raw. She pulls the child close, her blouse pressing roughly against a cheek too young to understand what imprisonment truly means.

The prophet turned slowly, his eyes steady and piercing. “Grief is direction,” he said simply. “Follow it—it leads to what endures.”
