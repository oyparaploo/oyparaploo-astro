The Sound of Olive Wood Burning

Words / 2025 Variety

Part One: The Decibel Meter

I learned to measure silence at 4 a.m. on Tuesday, March 12th, when the sound engineer handed me his decibel meter outside what remained of the Al-Shifa hospital. The device, a Lutron SL-4035SD, registered 47 decibels—the exact volume of my grandmother's last breath in 1948, though of course I couldn't have known that yet.

"Hold it steady," Khalil said, adjusting his headphones. "We're documenting absence."

The meter's needle trembled between 47 and 52 decibels. Behind us, three olive trees older than the Balfour Declaration stood in someone's former garden, their leaves rustling at exactly 38 decibels—I measured. One tree bore scorch marks up its eastern side. When I placed my palm against the blackened bark, carbon came away on my skin like historical evidence.

This is how I came to Gaza: not as a journalist or aid worker, but following a question I couldn't quite articulate about how sound transforms into light, how violence becomes visible, how silence itself carries decibels. My great-aunt Layla had been an artist here, drawing what she called "graphic scores"—visual representations of sounds that couldn't be heard. Her notebooks, which I inherited after her death in Amman, showed explosions rendered as spirals of gold ink, checkpoint arguments transformed into geometric patterns, the call to prayer depicted as ascending ladders of blue.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Or behind. Time works differently when you're measuring the volume of rubble.

[Braid shift to historical context - 1000 words]

The British Mandate's 1945 Survey of Palestine recorded 1,368,000 olive trees in what would become the Gaza Strip. Each tree, according to Ottoman tax records I found in the Istanbul Archives, could produce 15-20 kilograms of olives annually, enough oil to light a family's lamps for three months. My grandmother's family owned forty-three trees in Huj, a village that no longer exists except in the basement archives of Yad Vashem, where they keep the aerial photographs taken by the German air force in 1917.

I know this because I spent six months comparing those photographs with current satellite imagery, overlaying them in Photoshop until I could see the ghost roads, the phantom orchards, the spectral outlines of houses now buried under Highway 4. The olive trees appear in the 1917 images as small dark circles, arranged in neat rows like periods at the end of sentences no one finishes anymore.

In her notebook from 1952, four years after the Nakba, Layla drew her first graphic score. The page, yellowed and water-stained, shows a series of concentric circles labeled in Arabic: "The sound of mother calling for Hamid (95 dB)," "Dogs barking at abandoned houses (73 dB)," "Breathing through tear gas (41 dB)." She had no decibel meter then, of course. These were estimates, guesses, the arithmetic of trauma.

Dr. Salma Khayyat, who runs the oral history project at Birzeit University, told me that survivors of the 1948 expulsions often describe sounds before sights. "They remember the pitch of bullets over their heads," she said, pulling file after file from her metal cabinet. "The specific crack of their front door being kicked in. The way their grandmother's breathing changed during the forced march."

We sat in her office, which overlooks a valley where you can still see the terraces from Ottoman-era agriculture, while she played me recordings. Voice after voice describing not what they saw but what they heard: the particular silence that falls after a village empties, the way wind sounds different through an abandoned house, the unexpected loudness of fires consuming hundred-year-old wood.

"Your great-aunt understood something," Dr. Khayyat said, showing me a photocopy of one of Layla's later works—a massive score showing the sound levels of the 1967 war, hour by hour, location by location. "Sound is evidence. Decibels don't lie."

[Braid shift to present investigation - 1000 words]

Khalil's workshop occupies a converted garage in Gaza City's Rimal district, though "converted" suggests more intentionality than the reality of tarps, salvaged equipment, and extension cords snaking through holes knocked in cinder blocks. When I arrived at dawn, he was already calibrating his equipment, preparing for what he called "the morning chorus"—that hour when the city wakes and every sound matters.

"First the generators," he said, pointing to his laptop screen where sound waves appeared as mountains and valleys. "Always between 5:47 and 5:52. Sixty-eight decibels from the hospital, seventy-three from the bakery."

He handed me headphones. Through them, I heard Gaza deconstructed: generators indeed, but also water pumps gasping, a child practicing violin, someone hammering metal, pigeons, a radio playing Fairuz, car engines, a woman calling someone named Mohammed, gas canisters being delivered, the Mediterranean hitting the breakwater at intervals of eleven seconds.

"Now watch," Khalil said, switching programs. The sound waves transformed into ribbons of color—generator noise appearing as thick red bands, the violin as sharp yellow spikes, the sea as undulating blue. "This is what your aunt was trying to draw. Sound made visible."

A younger woman entered, signing rapidly. Khalil signed back, then told me: "This is Noor. She wants to show you something."

Noor led us three blocks to a demolished house where she'd installed what she called a "vibration garden." Speakers buried in the rubble transmitted subsonic frequencies—sounds below human hearing but feelable through your bones. She handed me what looked like a stethoscope modified with copper wiring.

"Put it against the concrete," Khalil translated as she signed.

I pressed the metal disk against a chunk of fallen ceiling. Through the earpieces came a rhythmic pulsing, like a heartbeat but mechanical. Noor signed something rapidly.

"She says that's the sound of rebar oxidizing inside the concrete," Khalil explained. "Four hertz. The frequency of fear, some scientists say. But also the same frequency as ocean waves during storms."

Noor pulled out a notebook filled with drawings that looked remarkably like Layla's—circles and spirals, waves and jagged lines, each annotated with frequencies and timestamps. She'd been mapping the sounds of destruction, but also what she called "sounds of becoming"—the specific frequencies of rebuilding, of new growth through cracks, of children playing in ruins.

[Scene construction - 500+ words]

The explosion happened at 11:43 a.m.

I know the precise time because Khalil's equipment recorded everything—the initial crack at 157 decibels, the subsequent rumble at 134, the car alarms at 95, the silence that followed hovering at an impossible 32 decibels, quieter than the desert at night.

We were in the basement of the community center, watching Fadi perform his visual poetry—a form where he simultaneously speaks, signs, and projects written words on the wall behind him. Thirty-seven people in plastic chairs. The poem was about his daughter's first word, which wasn't a word but a gesture, a reaching toward light.

The blast wave hit the building's foundation first. I felt it through my feet, a vibration that climbed my skeleton. The projector swayed. Dust filtered down from the ceiling joints like snow in the beam of light.

Nobody screamed.

This detail matters—in my grandmother's stories of 1948, people screamed. In the recordings from 1967, 1973, 1987, 2000, 2008, 2014, 2021, people screamed. But here, now, silence. We'd evolved past screaming into something else.

Fadi didn't stop performing. His hands kept moving, shaping words in the air while his voice dropped to match the new bass note that filled the room—the building itself groaning, deciding whether to stand or fall. The projected words behind him flickered but held: "She reached for light the way plants reach for sun, unknowing but certain."

Khalil had his decibel meter out, recording. Noor pressed her palms against the wall, reading vibrations like braille. Someone's phone rang—an absurd normal sound playing an Egyptian pop song.

"Is anyone hurt?" This from Samah, the activist who'd organized the reading. She moved through the rows, touching shoulders, making eye contact. Professional at catastrophe.

The building held. After seventeen seconds—Khalil showed me the timestamp later—people began to breathe normally again. The reading continued. This is Gaza: you finish the poem.

Afterward, we climbed the stairs to find the top floor's windows blown out, glass scattered like sharp rain across the tiles. Through the empty frames, we could see smoke rising two blocks north. Black smoke, which meant fuel. Gray smoke, which meant concrete. White smoke, which meant phosphorus, though nobody said that word.

Noor walked to the window frame and held up her palm, feeling the air currents. She signed something to Khalil.

"She says the explosion changed the building's resonance. It vibrates at a different frequency now. Half a hertz lower." He paused, calculated. "That's the difference between middle C and B."

The building had been retuned by violence, dropped a half-step like a guitar with loosened strings.

[Personal memory woven with research - 1000 words]

My grandmother only spoke about the olive trees once, the summer before she died. We were sitting in her kitchen in Detroit, where she'd ended up after Cairo, after Amman, after Beirut. She was peeling apples with a knife that had belonged to her mother, the blade worn thin as paper from ninety years of use.

"Forty-three trees," she said suddenly, as if continuing a conversation we'd been having my whole life. "Do you know what that means?"

I was twenty-two. I didn't know what anything meant.

"Six hundred kilograms of olives per season. Fifty liters of oil. Enough to trade for wheat, cloth, coffee. A dowry for daughters. University for sons." She quartered the apple, removed the seeds with surgical precision. "We named them."

"The trees?"

"Like children. The grandfather tree—350 years old, trunk so thick three people couldn't wrap their arms around it. The wedding tree—planted when my parents married. The revolution tree—1936, the year of the Arab Revolt."

She arranged the apple slices on a plate, a spiral from center to edge.

"What happened to them?"

She looked at me with an expression I'd later recognize in every Palestinian over seventy—a mixture of exhaustion and incredulity that this question still needs asking.

"What do you think happened?"

Israeli military documents declassified in 2019 show that between 1948 and 1955, approximately 2.5 million olive trees were uprooted in what became Israel. The official reason: security buffer zones. The unofficial reason found in soldiers' diaries donated to the Israel State Archives: the trees were sold to landscaping companies in Tel Aviv. Ancient olive trees, some dating to the Roman period, now decorate shopping centers in Herzliya.

I found the sale records in a cardboard box in the Haifa municipality building. Tree #447: Olive, est. age 200-300 years, trunk circumference 3.2 meters, purchased May 1949 for 50 Israeli pounds, currently located at the entrance to the Dan Caesarea Hotel.

That could be the grandfather tree. Or not. The records don't include origin villages.

When I showed Khalil the documents, he pulled out his computer and opened a sound file. "Listen," he said. The recording was just wind through leaves, but he zoomed in on the waveform, enhanced certain frequencies. "Every tree has a signature based on its leaf shape, branch pattern, age. I've been recording olive trees all over Palestine."

He played me dozens—trees in Jenin, Hebron, Jaffa. Each sounded different, though I couldn't articulate how. Like accents, or fingertips, or the way each person's breathing is distinct.

"If we had a recording of your grandmother's trees," he said, "we could identify them. Sound as DNA."

But nobody was recording olive trees in 1948. Layla's notebooks begin after the expulsion, in the refugee camps where the only trees were memory.

[Return to present scene with complexity - 1000 words]

Three days after the explosion, Sami the actor organized what he called "a theatrical investigation." We would return to the damaged community center and perform the exact moment of the blast—not to recreate trauma, he insisted, but to understand its architecture.

"Violence has dramaturgy," he explained, pacing the room where we'd gathered. "Rising action, climax, denouement. If we can understand its structure, we can write a different ending."

Fifteen people volunteered. Khalil brought his equipment. Noor set up vibration sensors. Samah, despite calling the idea "trauma tourism," agreed to participate.

We took our exact positions from three days earlier. Fadi stood where he'd been performing. I sat in the third row, fourth chair. The projector was positioned to cast the same angle of light.

"Begin," Sami called.

Fadi started the same poem, his daughter reaching for light. But this time, we noticed details that terror had erased. How he shifted his weight left foot to right at the word "reaching." How his signing hand trembled slightly—not fear, but a palsy from a childhood injury. How the projected words appeared first in Arabic, then English, then Hebrew, a trinity of languages refusing segregation.

At 11:43 exactly—Sami had synchronized his watch with the explosion's timestamp—he clapped once, sharp and loud.

The sound was nothing like an explosion, yet everyone flinched. Muscle memory. Cellular anticipation.

"What did you notice?" Sami asked.

"The birds," someone said. "Three seconds before, all the birds went quiet."

"The temperature dropped," said another. "Or felt like it did."

Samah raised her hand. "Fadi's daughter. In the poem. What was her first word?"

Fadi signed something. Khalil translated: "Light. Her first word was 'light.' But in Arabic, it's the same word as 'revolution'—thawra and thor. She confused them."

This is when I understood what Layla had been drawing all those years. Not the sounds of violence but the moment just before—when birds go quiet, when temperature shifts, when language itself trembles between meanings. The graphic scores were early warning systems, teaching us to read danger in frequencies, to see sound before it arrives.

Sami had us perform the moment seven times. Each repetition revealed new layers—how dust motes had danced in the projector beam, how someone's phone had vibrated silently against a chair leg, how the building's AC unit had shuddered then resumed, mechanical dedication to normalcy.

On the seventh run, Sami didn't clap. We waited at the explosion moment, suspended. The poem continued. Fadi's daughter reached for light/revolution. Nobody flinched.

"This," Sami announced, "is rehearsal for a different future."

But Samah challenged him: "You think we can rehearse away trauma? Practice until bombs don't matter?"

"No," Sami replied. "But we can rehearse response. Instead of screaming, we document. Instead of running, we witness. Instead of forgetting, we transform."

They argued for an hour—Samah insisting on the danger of aestheticizing violence, Sami defending art as resistance, others taking sides or finding middle ground. The debate itself became performance, their voices rising and falling in waves Khalil recorded, later showing me how argument follows musical patterns—statement, variation, crescendo, resolution or its absence.

[Weaving in philosophical questions and complexity - 1000 words]

The question that haunts every Palestinian artist: Does rendering occupation beautiful make it bearable, therefore extending it? Or does beauty become resistance by insisting on humanity despite dehumanization?

Layla's notebooks obsess over this. Pages of crossed-out attempts to draw suffering without glorifying it, to make visible without making spectacular. In 1968, she wrote: "The sunset over rubble was gorgeous. I hate myself for noticing."

Dr. Rashid Masharawi, who teaches philosophy at Al-Azhar University, invited me to his seminar on "Aesthetics Under Siege." His students—mostly born after 2000, raised entirely under blockade—debated whether Gaza's artists had a responsibility to show only ugliness, to refuse the Western hunger for "resilience porn."

"We're expected to be beautiful victims," said Yasmin, a photography student. "Photogenic suffering. Children flying kites in ruins."

"But ugliness is also a trap," countered Omar. "Show only destruction, and we become only destruction."

Dr. Masharawi let them argue, occasionally inserting questions: "Who decides what is beautiful? Can aesthetic judgment exist under occupation? Is the mere act of creating art in Gaza already political, regardless of content?"

He showed them Layla's graphic scores on a laptop. The students studied them silently.

"These are medical charts," Yasmin finally said. "Diagnosis of a sick society."

"These are musical compositions," Omar disagreed. "Songs for a new instrument."

Both were right. Layla had invented a form that documented trauma while transforming it, that served as evidence while becoming art. But the central question remained: For whom? Who was the audience for these visual symphonies of suffering?

Later, privately, Dr. Masharawi told me his theory: "Your great-aunt wasn't drawing for us or them. She was drawing for a future that doesn't exist yet, when someone will need to understand not just what happened but how it felt in frequencies."

This future-tense audience appears throughout Palestinian art—creations addressed to people not yet born, or perhaps to a version of ourselves after liberation, strangers to our current selves.

But there's another interpretation, one Samah suggested during a particularly heated discussion: "Maybe she was drawing for the trees. The olive trees that can't hear but might feel vibrations through their roots. Sending messages to the dispersed orchards: We remember you."

It sounds mystical, but Khalil showed me research on plant bioacoustics—how trees communicate through ultrasonic clicks, how root systems respond to specific frequencies, how forests maintain underground networks of sound and chemistry. If trauma can be inherited epigenetically, why not ecologically? Why shouldn't the land itself remember in frequencies?

[The vulnerable distance - most emotional moment through physical description only - 500 words]

In Layla's final notebook, dated three months before her death, a single page:

The drawing shows a woman's hand pressed against paper. Around the handprint, concentric circles like topographical maps, each ring labeled with a year: 1948, 1967, 1973, 1987, 2000, 2008, 2014, 2021. The lines grow thicker with each ring, the space between them smaller, until they merge into solid black at the edges.

But in the center, where the palm would be, nothing. Blank paper. An absence the exact size of a heartline.

I found the notebook in a plastic bag inside another plastic bag inside a suitcase that hadn't been opened since my grandmother's funeral. The pages smelled like Amman—diesel and jasmine and dust. Some drawings had bled together from a water leak. Others were pristine, as if drawn yesterday.

On the page before the hand, she'd written a single line: "The sound of forgetting: 0 decibels. The sound of being forgotten: ∞."

I brought the notebook to Gaza, showed it to everyone I met. Most looked briefly, politely, the way you examine someone's family photos. But Noor studied each page for hours, her fingers hovering just above the paper, tracing the air above Layla's lines.

Through Khalil's translation, she explained: "The hand is a speaker. These rings are sound waves. But reversed—instead of emanating out, they're pressing in. The years are compression, getting denser until they create silence at the center."

She pulled out her own notebook, drew quickly: the same hand, but with lines radiating outward from the palm, each one labeled with a frequency rather than a year. Where Layla had created absence, Noor created presence.

"This is the answer," she signed. "Not silence but new frequency. Not forgetting but transformation."

She was twenty-three, the age Layla was during the Nakba. The age my grandmother was when she lost the olive trees. The age I was when I inherited notebooks I didn't understand.

[Facts transformed to images - 1000 words]

The Gaza Strip: 365 square kilometers. But measure it in footsteps—the average person takes 5,000 steps per kilometer—and it becomes 1,825,000 footsteps long, 600,000 footsteps wide. The distance my grandmother walked from Huj to Gaza in 1948: 97,000 footsteps. She counted.

Two million people live here. Stack them vertically, and they'd reach 3,400 kilometers into space, higher than satellites. Lay them horizontally, head to toe, and they'd stretch from Gaza to London, each person a link in a chain of breath and pulse.

The blockade permits 80 trucks of supplies daily. Each truck carries 20 tons. Divided equally: 800 grams per person—the weight of a loaf of bread, a bottle of water, three apples. The weight of Layla's final notebook.

But numbers lie through omission. They don't say how 800 grams feels in your hand when it's all you have. They don't capture the specific density of rationed bread, how it sits in your stomach like geological sediment, layer upon layer of waiting.

Khalil showed me how to read statistics sonically. "Unemployment at 47%," he said, "means every other footstep on the street belongs to someone without work. Listen." We stood on Omar al-Mukhtar Street during rush hour. He was right—you could hear it in the rhythm, a syncopation of purpose and aimlessness.

The fishing limit—three nautical miles from shore—translates to 5,556 meters, or the distance sound travels in 16.2 seconds. Fishermen time their nets by echolocation, shouting toward shore and counting until they hear the echo. More than sixteen seconds means they've crossed into forbidden water where Israeli gunboats circle.

Power cuts: 18 hours daily. But experience it as temperature—how indoor air thickens without circulation, how food spoils at predictable rates, how darkness has its own weight measured in sleepless children. Noor showed me her drawings of electricity schedules, not as timeline but as heartbeat—four hours on (systole), eighteen hours off (diastole), the whole city pulsing like a dying organ.

Water delivery: once weekly for most neighborhoods. The sound of empty pipes: 73 decibels of air rushing through copper. The sound of water finally arriving: 89 decibels of celebration, children shrieking, mothers calling, fathers dragging hoses. Khalil has recordings spanning five years—a sonic calendar of thirst and relief.

The wall is eight meters high. In acoustic terms, it creates what's called a "shadow zone"—frequencies above 500 Hz can't cross, while bass notes below 100 Hz pass through. Families separated by concrete learn to speak in low tones, their voices dropping octaves to reach each other.

[Return to opening image transformed - final scene - 1500 words]

On my last morning in Gaza, I returned to the three olive trees outside Al-Shifa hospital. The same trees from my first morning, but now I carried Layla's notebooks, Khalil's recordings, Noor's vibration maps, my grandmother's apple-paring knife wrapped in cloth.

The trees stood exactly as before—ancient, scarred, persisting. But I saw them differently. The scorch marks up the eastern trunk weren't just burns but a calendar—each ring of char marking another war, the tree incorporating violence into its growth rings like a timeline written in carbon.

Khalil met me there with his full equipment. "I want to try something," he said. He attached contact microphones to each tree's trunk, connected them to his laptop, began recording. "Trees conduct sound through their wood. If we can isolate their resonant frequency..."

The screen filled with waveforms—low, almost subsonic rumbles. He adjusted filters, enhanced certain ranges. Slowly, patterns emerged. Each tree hummed at a slightly different pitch, their frequencies separated by precise mathematical intervals.

"It's a chord," he said, amazed. "They're harmonizing."

Noor arrived with Sami, Fadi, Samah, and a dozen others from the workshop. Word had spread about this final recording. They formed a circle around the trees, witnesses to whatever we were discovering.

Dr. Masharawi came with his students. "These trees," he said, "were here before the hospital, before the neighborhood, before the Nakba, before the British, before the Ottomans. They're older than nation-states."

Yasmin, the photography student, touched the burned bark. "My grandfather told me they used to press olives here. Right here, where the parking lot is now. Stone presses from the Roman period."

Omar added, "There's a well underneath. Sealed in 1967. But you can still hear water if you put your ear to the ground."

One by one, people lay down on the broken asphalt, pressing their ears to the earth. I joined them. At first, nothing—just the vibration of generators, the distant traffic. But then, barely audible, a rhythmic pulsing. Not water. Something else.

"It's the roots," Noor signed, her hands dancing. "The trees talking underground."

Khalil moved his equipment closer, inserted a probe into a crack in the pavement. The laptop screen exploded with activity—frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 300 Hz, the trees' root systems electrochemically communicating in patterns that looked exactly like Layla's drawings.

"She knew," I said out loud. "Somehow she knew what they sounded like."

Samah shook her head. "She didn't know. She felt. There's a difference."

Sami stood up, brushed off his pants. "We should perform something. For the trees."

And so we did. Fadi recited a poem about light and revolution. Noor signed a story that made shadows dance. Sami enacted a wordless scene of planting and harvesting. The students sang a song their grandparents taught them, about a woman who turned into an olive tree to escape soldiers.

Khalil recorded everything, the human voices mixing with the trees' frequencies, creating what he later called "a sonic garden"—layers of sound that shouldn't exist together but did, here, in this moment.

As the sun climbed higher, the morning sounds of Gaza joined us—cars honking, vendors calling, children heading to school, the Mediterranean breaking against the seawall. The decibel meter read 87, then 92, then 95. The city waking up, adding its voice.

I opened Layla's final notebook to the blank palm at the center of concentric rings. Using the charcoal from the burned bark, I began to draw—not lines but dots, thousands of them, each one a frequency we'd recorded, a sound we'd witnessed, a story we'd shared. The dots became constellations, the constellations became a map, the map became a score for an instrument that didn't exist yet.

"What are you drawing?" Dr. Masharawi asked.

"The sound of return," I said, though I wasn't sure what that meant.

Noor looked at the drawing, then at the trees, then at me. She signed something complex, her hands moving like birds.

Khalil translated: "She says you're drawing the wrong direction. Sound doesn't return. It continues. Even when we can't hear it, it continues. In the wood, in the roots, in the stone, in us."

She was right. I turned the notebook sideways. Now the palm became not a destination but a source, the rings not years collapsing inward but waves expanding outward, each one carrying fragments of what was toward what might be.

The scorch marks on the olive tree caught the morning light, and for a moment, they looked like Arabic calligraphy, spelling words I couldn't read but somehow understood. The burned parts weren't wounds but language, the tree writing its own survival in carbon and time.

I thought of my grandmother peeling apples in Detroit, each spiral of skin a year of exile. Of Layla in Amman, drawing sounds she'd never hear again. Of these trees, standing here before any of us, witnessing every arrival and departure, every explosion and celebration, converting it all into wood, into leaves, into olives that would become oil that would become light.

The decibel meter read 32—that impossible silence from after the explosion. But now I understood: it wasn't silence. It was the frequency of persistence, too low for human ears but readable by roots, by bones, by the parts of us that remember without words.

As we packed up the equipment, I asked Khalil to play back the recording of the trees' chord. He did, and everyone stopped. It sounded exactly like the call to prayer if you played it at one-quarter speed, or like the Mediterranean if you sped it up, or like children laughing if you listened with your whole body instead of just your ears.

"What will you do with this?" Samah asked, meaning the recordings, the notebooks, the story.

I looked at the olive trees—grandfather tree, wedding tree, revolution tree, though these weren't those trees, could never be those trees, but carried their frequency anyway, the way all olive trees carry the memory of the first olive tree, the way all exiles carry the memory of home in their bones.

"I'll draw it," I said. "The way Layla did. As evidence and art. As diagnosis and song. For the future that doesn't exist yet."

The sun was fully up now. Gaza roared to life around us—beautiful and terrible, persistent and fragile, a city that should have died a thousand times but didn't, couldn't, wouldn't. The olive trees stood as they had for centuries, converting light to sugar, sound to growth, violence to witness.

In my pocket, the decibel meter continued recording, its memory full of numbers that meant nothing and everything: 47 decibels, the exact volume of my grandmother's last breath and first. The mathematics of survival. The frequency of return that is not return but continuation.

As I walked away, I heard Khalil call out: "The trees! Listen!"

I turned back. A wind had picked up, moving through the olive leaves in waves. It sounded like applause, or rain, or the sea, or pages turning, or time itself made audible—all of it, none of it, everything at once.

The meter read exactly 38 decibels. The same as that first morning. The same as always.

But now I knew how to listen.

Coda: The Forty-Fourth Tree

Six months later, in Detroit, I received a package from Khalil. Inside: a USB drive and a single olive leaf, dried and pressed. The drive contained ten hours of recordings—not just our trees but olive trees from across Palestine, each file meticulously labeled with GPS coordinates, age estimates, trunk circumference.

The note read: "I found it. Recording from 1946. British ornithologist studying birdsong near Huj. Background: olive trees. Your grandmother's trees. Frequency analysis matches Tree #447 at Dan Caesarea Hotel. The grandfather tree survived."

I listened to the recording on repeat—wind through leaves, birds calling, a dog barking in the distance, someone singing in Arabic, too faint to make out the words. It sounded exactly like every olive tree and like no olive tree, specific and universal, past and present collapsed into ninety seconds of tape hiss and life.

I began drawing, the way Layla taught me without teaching me, the way sound becomes visible when you have no other choice. Circles within circles, each ring a year, a frequency, a story, a loss, a continuation. The drawing grew until it covered my studio wall—a map of sounds that connected Gaza to Detroit to Huj to places that no longer exist except in the resonance of wood and memory.

The olive leaf crumbled in my fingers, releasing an scent that was every grandmother's kitchen, every village morning, every tree that ever was or will be.

I added its dust to my charcoal, and kept drawing.

The sound continues.

Even now.

Even here.
