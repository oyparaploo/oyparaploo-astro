When She Was: The Complete Chronicle

Words / 2025 Variety

Fragment 1: The Moth at Home (The Narrator's Memory)

When she was home, she was a moth—a creature of darkness and light transformed into flesh through the radical experiment. In the underground settlement of Tomorrow's Edge, with its intricate tunnels carved from bedrock, phosphorescent moss lining crystalline water pipes that sang, she embodied the quiet navigation between shadows.

Her name was Beit-47 to the tribe that reduced identity to cold digits, but I knew her as Marina. I was the one who found her once, who gave her water and a rose when she was lost. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

In moth form, her consciousness flowed differently. The transformation wasn't metaphor anymore—the experiment had made it real. Soft gray wings emerged from her shoulder blades, antennae from her temples sensing the electromagnetic fields of the settlement. Through the plant-like neural formations that grew when she concentrated, she connected to the settlement's living walls, drawn to the bioluminescent guides like ancient instinct, sharing memories across blurring, delirious decades.

Fragment 2: The Scorpion in the Wild (Observer 47's Log)

From orbital platform Azure-Nine, we overseers watch mother earth and her strange children. Today I observed subject Beit-47 leave the settlement. The transformation was immediate—moth to scorpion in seconds. Chitinous armor wrote itself across skin that had been soft wings.

When she was out, she was a scorpion in the deadlands. The old lyrics echo true: a scorpion in the wasteland survives alone. She embodies the deadly survivor, adapted to the harshest conditions, her venomous tail a warning to other changed ones. In this form, she is armored, patient, lethal—surviving where nothing should survive.

Above her, a murder of crows circles—not true crows, for those are nearly extinct, but consciousness given dark wings. So intuitive, how they spiral together. One carries a scavenger's awareness, a human who chose to inhabit corvid form permanently. Wings have become timeless consciousness over ash-gray skies.

The scorpion-Beit hunts not for food but for meaning, for genetic material, for answers to the question: How much can humans really change, both physically and culturally, and remain human?

Fragment 3: The Salamander on the Road (The Narrator's Account)

When she was lost, she was a salamander. I found her that day on the old road, the one that leads nowhere now. The transformation had failed—cellular exhaustion, they call it. She was neither moth nor scorpion but something primitive and desperate. A creature caught between states, skin weeping toxins, unable to maintain form.

This is my role—I remain unchanged, stubbornly human, walking between settlements with messages and medicines. I refuse the experiment, keep my original form though it means I age while others transform.

I gave her water infused with stabilizing minerals. I placed a rose—one of the few that still blooms, engineered to survive radiation—beside her trembling form. As she stretched back into something resembling herself, the sun rose over the wasteland. That sunrise symbolized recovery, hope returning. It was a tender moment—I had cared for her as she had once cared for me in her moth form, when I was sick with the loneliness of being unchanged.

"Thank you," she whispered, her voice neither human nor animal but something between.

Fragment 4: The Octopus and the Stars (Beit's Memory)

When I was young—before the cultivation, when I was still partly my grandmother's stories—she said I was an octopus. "All day long, you reached for stars with your eight arms," she would tell me, her hands mimicking tentacles grasping at light.

This wasn't nonsense but prophecy. In the settlement now, I do reach for stars—I extract possibility from the void with multiple consciousness streams, each tentacle of thought exploring different realities. The image is literal now: through my bio-architectural gift, I create nurseries where neural tendrils extend like cephalopod arms, each one drawing different wavelengths of cosmic radiation to feed the cultivated young.

My grandmother, one of the wise elderly women who were the unsung forces of survival, the archetypes of nature nurturers, taught me the hardest truth: "Women who survive must be unfaithful to their child." Not cruel abandonment, but the necessity of maintaining identity beyond motherhood. In this new world where traditional reproduction has failed, where we cultivate rather than birth, her words take new meaning. We must be unfaithful to what we were to become what we need to be.

Fragment 5: The Settlement's Architecture (Multiple Voices)

The child Echo speaks: I discovered how to weave human biological tissue into living stone. Watch—I sing the frequency that makes cells remember their purpose. This is my mythic significance, my epic contribution.

Beit shapes the walls: With Echo's discovery, I build memory cathedrals. Each room holds decades of experience. Here, consciousness shared as fluid, every being an US of sorts. The walls themselves ask: Does god exist? Or are we becoming god through our transformations?

The unchanged wander speaks: I see their creation and mourn. These axolotl-born children, DNA spliced with regenerating amphibians for their immortal cells, build monuments to forgetting. Yet somehow, in their strange architecture, human impulses persist—organizing emotions within an enduring grieving community.

The settlement breathes: Intricate underground passages spiral downward. Meditation chambers where individual identity rubs against collective consciousness. Hydroponic gardens growing organs like fruit. Water recycling systems that sing in harmonics. Bioluminescent guide paths. Living furniture that responds to need. Air filtered through modified lungs embedded in walls. Each detail a testament to adaptation.

Fragment 6: The Eyes of Providence (Historical Chronicle)

The transformation began as all transformations do—with good intentions that became self-sabotage. Even benevolence, we learned, can be a noble cause that destroys.

A murder of crows—the last corvids enhanced with human consciousness—serve as our witnesses. Now they fly eternally, carrying the awareness of scavengers who chose to abandon human form. Their eyes have become providence itself, watching the world transform below, feeding on the death that brings new life.

In settlements like Tomorrow's Edge, humanity split into isolated cells. Each developed unique survival strategies while wildlife dwindled at rates that made counting irrelevant. By the time we thought to preserve, there was little left to save except ourselves, and we were already changing beyond recognition.

Fragment 7: Sparks of Life (Collective Consciousness)

—when she was home, she was our moth, drawn to light—
—when she was out, she was our scorpion, surviving poison—
—when she was lost, we found her as salamander—
—when she was young, she reached eight ways for stars—

We share these memories like water, consciousness flowing between us. The axolotl-born Beit has become our living question. Through her transformations, we experience the tension between evolution and identity.

She teaches us what her grandmother taught in different words: survival requires unfaithfulness to our child-selves. Not betrayal but growth. Not abandonment but transformation.

In her moth form, she navigates darkness. In her scorpion form, she endures. In her salamander form, she regenerates. In her memory of being an octopus, she shows us how to grasp multiple realities simultaneously.

Fragment 8: The Failing of Natural Process (Scientific Record)

Traditional reproduction ceased seven years ago. The last natural birth was stillborn, the infant's DNA too corrupted to sustain life. Now all new beings are cultivated, incorporating genetic material from multiple species.

Subject Beit-47 has developed a solution—bio-architectural wombs that gestate hybrid beings. But each generation moves further from baseline human. The children emerging have:
- Consciousness partially external to their bodies (octopus-inspired distributed neurons)
- Telepathic abilities manifesting as plant-fungal growths
- Ability to photosynthesize (moth wing adaptations)
- Memory storage in crystalline exoskeleton structures (scorpion-derived)
- Regenerative capabilities (axolotl cellular functions)

This is evolution or extinction, depending on perspective. The narrative transcends concepts of individual ego, even at great cost.

Fragment 9: Community in Isolation (The Wanderer's Observation)

They create strange normalcy here. I watch them—these transformed beings—maintain friendships that transcend species boundaries. Adult-child bonds persist though "child" and "adult" have lost traditional meaning. Female and male categories blur and reshape.

Spiritual rituals emerge: The Remembrance, where they temporarily merge consciousness. The Shaping, where Beit and Echo create new architectures. The Witnessing, where they acknowledge what they've lost.

Old world institutions have faded, yet humans recreate society in familiar but not identical ways. There are still leaders and followers, artists and scientists, lovers and rivals. They love one another, strongly dislike each other—a lament for stagnation and weird praise for the constancy of human nature.

Fragment 10: Questions Without Answers (Beit's Creation)

I build a room that asks questions:
- How much can we change and remain ourselves?
- Does consciousness require a single body?
- Is memory individual or collective?
- Can we survive by becoming unrecognizable?

The walls pulse with collective uncertainty regarding older cycles of life. This ambiguity reinforces that survival is just unanswered questions, human flaws doomed to repeat even in new forms.

A child asks, "Does god exist?"

I shape the answer into the walls: Perhaps god is what we're becoming. Perhaps god was always just consciousness trying to understand itself through endless forms.

Fragment 11: The Crow Watcher's Perspective (Consciousness in Flight)

I chose wings. My human body rests in stasis below while my awareness soars with the murder—corvid consciousness circling over dead lands.

From here, I see everything: Beit transforming from moth to scorpion to salamander, always returning to her hybrid human form. The settlements spreading underground like fungal networks. The overseers in their orbital platforms, documenting our strange evolution.

We who chose flight are the eyes of providence now. We watch humanity's arrogance and resilience, self-destruction and ongoing hope. We are the fable being written in ash-gray skies, neither wholly tragedy nor triumph.

Fragment 12: Hard-Earned Wisdom (Elder's Testament)

The sparks of life persist, combined with hard-earned wisdom. We learned:
- Survival means constant change
- Identity is not fixed but fluid
- Love transcends form
- Grief is the price of memory
- Hope must be fragile to be real

We're careful not to offer naive optimism. Any rebirth occurs under mother earth's watchful gaze—she who has seen countless species rise and fall, who watches our experiment with neither approval nor condemnation.

Beit embodies these lessons. When she shapes our living walls, she includes spaces for doubt, rooms for grief, halls for joy. Her architecture acknowledges that we are not the crown of creation but just a different link in the chain.

Fragment 13: The Rose and Water (Love Story)

I loved her—this shape-shifting prophet. Not romantic love, but something deeper. Recognition, perhaps. She was everything I refused to become, and I was the constant she could return to.

When I found her as a salamander, giving her water and a rose, I was returning a debt. Months before, when the loneliness of being unchanged threatened to break me, she had sat with me in moth form, her presence a quiet comfort in the darkness.

We were opposite answers to the same question. She chose transformation; I chose constancy. Together, we proved that humanity persists not in single forms but in the connections between them.

"Why don't you change?" she asked me once.

"Someone must remember," I said. "Someone must witness with unchanged eyes."

"Then I'll be the change you witness," she replied, and transformed before me—moth to scorpion to salamander to something unnamed, each form a verse in humanity's evolving song.

Fragment 14: Artistic Creation (Echo and Beit's Collaboration)

Echo discovered it first—how to sing dead tissue back to pseudo-life. Not resurrection but reimagining. She could take extinct DNA and create new myths from old bones.

Together with Beit, they celebrated artistic creation with powerful unexpected viewpoints. Beit shaped structures from living tissue while Echo sang them into consciousness. Their work became the settlement's highest art:
- Halls that dreamed
- Walls that remembered
- Floors that responded to emotion
- Ceilings that opened to impossible skies

One day, Echo asked, "Are we artists or gods?"

Beit replied, "We're the question between."

Fragment 15: The Humbling Truth (Final Chronicle)

This is the humbling idea: humankind is not special, just different. We were supposed to be so much more than what we were. Perhaps we're becoming it through transformation, perhaps losing it through change.

The settlements persist. Tomorrow's Edge with its axolotl-born prophets. The Northern Merge where consciousness flows freely. The Unchanged Territories where stubborn humans maintain original form, dying but refusing transformation.

Mother Earth watches. The overseers document. The crow-consciousness provides providence from above.

Beit has become the settlement itself now—her consciousness distributed through every wall, every carefully shaped corner. When asked if humanity survives, she responds through the architecture: "We are still loving, still hating, still creating. We are recognizably human in our unrecognizable forms."

Epilogue: The Fable We Became

The unchanged wanderer writes:

This is how the story that could have become extinct chose to transform instead. We are fragments now—perspectives and voices, first person and third, personal confessions and historical chronicles. The structure itself conveys our theme: transience of individual lives against the backdrop of eons.

Beit taught us that to survive, women—all humans—must be unfaithful to their child-selves. Not betrayal but necessary growth. She showed us transformation in all its forms:
- Moth (navigating darkness in safety)
- Scorpion (armored survival in wilderness)
- Salamander (regeneration through vulnerability)
- Octopus (grasping multiple realities simultaneously)

The prose remains calm, understated, conveying extraordinary events in matter-of-fact language. We treat the surreal as mundane because it has become our normal. There's haunting serenity in accepting that we are no longer what we were.

Yet human nature persists in this: we continue to love one another, hate one another, create beauty, destroy meaning, hope against reason. Our capacity for contradiction endures.

The story ends as it began—with a woman who transforms, who was given water and a rose when lost, who reached for stars with eight arms when young, who learned that survival requires letting go of who we were.

She is the radical experiment incarnate. Through her, we ask: How much can humans really change?

The answer is the change itself. The question is the only home we have left.

Beneath it all, mother earth breathes slowly, watching her strangest children become something she never imagined, something that was never meant for earth yet could exist nowhere else.

The transformation continues.
