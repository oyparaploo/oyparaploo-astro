## Chapter One: The Server Room

August 15, 5:23 AM

Santijn Chen felt the wrongness in her teeth before she badged into Harrington Industries. The parking lot should have been empty at this hour, but three cars huddled near the loading dock like mourners at a wake—engines off, windows fogged from inside. Security's golf cart sat abandoned, driver door open, keys still in the ignition. The morning felt suspended—like that moment between when a glass tips and when it shatters.

The badge reader blinked red. Invalid. She tried again. Red. On the third try, green, but the lock took seven seconds to disengage. She tracked the seconds—a habit from her daughter's early years when Melody would hold her breath during hide-and-seek, and Santijn would count until the girl gasped back into existence. Seven seconds then. Seven years since she'd seen her daughter breathe.

Inside, the building's air conditioning had failed. The pressure differential made her eardrums flex—not just heat but wrong density, like the building was exhaling something it had been holding too long. The elevator panel showed every floor illuminated, impossible. She took the stairs.

Server Room B-7 was on the fifth floor, northeast corner. The stairwell door stood propped open with a yellow pencil, worn down to three inches, eraser completely gone. Santijn photographed it before touching anything—Document the periphery before the center—her method, always. The pencil was warm against her latex glove, warmth that had no source. She bagged it, labeled it, tried not to think about the bite marks in the wood that looked familiar as her own hunger.

The hallway stretched longer than architecture should allow. Fluorescents flickered in sequence, a wave of failing light that led to B-7. The door was closed but not latched. Through the gap, she could see light—not electric but dawn, amber and thick as honey.

She pushed the door open with one finger.

The server room breathed.

That was the only word for it—the metal racks expanding and contracting with heat, ticking like cooling engines, though the temperature display read 97°F and climbing. Dawn leaked through the single window, catching particles that shouldn't exist in a clean room. They drifted with too much purpose, forming shapes almost recognizable—numbers, letters, faces—before dispersing.

The crystals were what stopped her mid-step.

They grew upward from the server bases, delicate structures reaching toward the ceiling like frozen exhales. In the shifting light, they pulsed between states—solid when she looked directly at them, translucent in her peripheral vision. She'd seen crystal formations before, plenty of them in financial crime scenes where someone had tried to burn evidence with the wrong accelerant. But these grew toward heat rather than away from it, as if fed by temperature rather than destroyed.

She approached slowly, documenting each angle. Through her camera lens, the crystals showed differently—darker, more substantial, arranged in patterns that reminded her of transaction trees, each branch splitting into smaller denominations until they became too small to track.

The security badge reader on the inner door blinked steadily. Red. Every thirty seconds. She checked the log: last valid entry 11:47 PM. CFO Harrison Wells, though she'd interviewed him yesterday and his badge had been in his wallet the entire time. Either someone had cloned his access or—

A drop of condensation traveled down the window, slow enough to follow, collecting dust and crystal particles in its path. It left a clear trail through the grime, and in that trail, she saw it: the outline of a hand, five fingers splayed against glass. Someone had stood here recently, forehead pressed to the window, breathing fog onto the pane. The mark was still warm—she could see where their breath had nucleated, forming new crystals that grew even as she watched.

On the center server, stuck to the monitor with condensation alone, a Post-it note curled from humidity. The ink had bled, but the message remained readable:

"The data isn't missing. It's everywhere now."

Santijn's hand went to her jacket pocket, where she kept her own Post-it pad. Same color. Same size. She pulled it out, compared the edge tears. Perfect match. The note had come from her pad.

But the handwriting—

She photographed it, uploaded it to her tablet, ran it through her handwriting analysis app. The one she'd trained on her own writing, on Melody's early attempts at letters, on suicide notes and ransom demands and forged checks. The app took longer than usual, percentage climbing slowly: 67% match... 78%... 89%...

97% match to Santijn Chen.

She looked at her hands. No ink stains. She'd been home last night, hadn't she? Sitting at her kitchen table, growing salt crystals in shallow dishes, watching them form their predictable lattices while she tried not to think about how Melody was growing too, changing in ways she'd never see.

The percentage climbed: 98%... 99%...

She pulled up her phone's location history. Home from 6 PM to 4:47 AM. Gap from 4:47 to 5:15. Then here. Thirty minutes missing. Long enough to drive here, write a note, leave. Not long enough to remember.

100% match.

A sound made her turn—something between a sigh and a settling. The crystals were sublimating, she realized. Changing directly from solid to gas, no liquid phase between. The server room was filling with whatever they'd been made of, and she was breathing it in. The air tasted sweet and chemical, like the candy-scented markers Melody used to draw with. Used to. Still did. Would be using right now, at foster home number three, drawing families where everyone's face was carefully scribbled out.

On the keyboard beneath the Post-it: a yellow pencil, balanced perfectly on the space bar. No fingerprints—she could tell without checking. The eraser worn to nothing, just the metal ferrule remaining like a socket missing its tooth. The wood warm to touch even through her gloves.

She bagged it. Labeled it: "Evidence #2. Location: Keyboard. Position: Balanced on space bar. Temperature: Approximately 98.6°F (body temperature). Eraser: Absent."

Her phone buzzed. Text from her mentor, James Chanson: "Saw the news about Harrington. You there yet?"

She hadn't told him. The call had come directly to her, bypassing the department. She typed back: "How did you know?"

"Same way you knew to go. We need to talk about the Pattern."

"What pattern?"

Three dots appeared, disappeared, appeared again. Finally: "Check the transaction logs. Nothing over $9,999. It's starting again."

Santijn pulled up Harrington's financial records on her tablet. The CFO had given her access yesterday, sweating through his shirt, insisting nothing was wrong even as he provided passwords to systems she hadn't asked for. $9,997.23

She ran a quick sum: $47 million, dispersed across 5,000 employee accounts over five years. Ten dollars here. Twenty there. Amounts small enough to look like accounting errors.

The crystals were growing faster now, or maybe she was seeing them more clearly. They formed on her tablet screen, spreading from the heat of her fingers. She brushed them off and they dispersed into the air, adding to the sweet chemical haze.

Behind her, footsteps in the hallway. Slow, measured, counting each step. She recognized the rhythm—Chanson's walk, favoring his left knee from an old investigation injury. He appeared in the doorway, silhouette backlit by failing fluorescents. In his breast pocket: three yellow pencils, perfectly sharpened, points visible like tiny spears.

"Don't touch anything else," he said. "And whatever you do, don't try to remember last night."

"Why?"

He stepped into the room, and she saw his face clearly. He'd aged years in the weeks since she'd seen him. His eyes kept focusing on things that weren't there, tracking movement she couldn't see.

"Because you were here," he said. "So was I. So were at least seventeen others. All of us investigating. None of us remembering. The protocol doesn't just dissolve evidence, Santijn. It dissolves the investigators too."

The sun climbed higher, turning the crystals into prisms. Each one threw rainbow patterns on the walls—transaction IDs, she realized. Account numbers. Routing codes. The crime scene was documenting itself, writing its confession in light that would fade the moment clouds covered the sun.

Everything photographed, aware the images would corrupt. The crystals would be gone by noon, sublimated into air that everyone in the building would breathe. By evening, 500 employees would go home with trace amounts in their lungs, each carrying away a fragment of evidence too small to matter, too dispersed to prosecute.

"We need to find the source," she said.

Chanson laughed, bitter as eraser dust. "We are the source. Every investigator who's touched this case. We investigate, we document, we dissolve. The pattern doesn't exist in the crime—it exists in how we look for it."

The pencil in evidence bag #2 had started sweating, droplets of something clear forming on the wood. The bag clouded from inside. She held it up to the light, and through the condensation, she could see impressions in the wood—words pressed so hard they'd left permanent indents:

"Daddy was at work late."

Her handwriting. Age seven. The first lie she'd ever written, the night her father didn't come home, the night she'd learned that truth could be revised if you pressed hard enough, erased often enough, rewrote until the paper tore.

"How many others?" she asked.

"Investigating? Dozens. Maybe hundreds. All of us drawn to financial crimes, all of us with childhood connections to the original case."

"What original case?"

But she knew. Could taste it in the air—cedar and chemicals, the specific combination that meant Ravenholl Elementary, 1987. The poisoning that had never been solved, never been properly documented, never been anything but rumors and disappeared witnesses and twenty-three sick children whose parents all worked at the same plant.

"We were there," Chanson said. "All of us. Different times, different roles, but all connected to Ravenholl. And we've been investigating each other ever since, thinking we're solving crimes when really we're just..."

"Dissolving," Santijn finished.

The server room had reached 101°F. The crystals were sublimating faster, creating updrafts that lifted the Post-it note off the monitor. It spiraled upward, caught in thermal currents, writing visible for one moment more:

"The data isn't missing. It's everywhere now."

Then it dissolved too, paper becoming fiber becoming dust becoming nothing. Only the impression remained on the monitor screen, the ghost of adhesive that would fade by afternoon.

Santijn sealed the evidence bags, knowing they would degrade. Photographed the empty spaces where crystals had been, documenting absence. This was her method—archaeological investigation, working outward from what wasn't there.

In her pocket, her phone buzzed. Text from an unknown number: "Check your daughter's drawings. She remembers everything."

When she looked up, Chanson was gone. Only his pencils remained, three yellow soldiers standing at attention on the server rack, their points already beginning to soften in the heat.

Outside, the city woke to another Tuesday, unaware that $47 million had dispersed through its economy in increments too small to notice. In coffee shops, extra cents appeared in tip jars. Parking meters showed paid time no one had purchased. Bank accounts grew by amounts that looked like interest, felt like error, dissolved like morning frost.

Santijn left the server room at 6:47 AM. The security log would show no exit, just as it had shown no entry. She existed in the gap between recorded moments, investigating crimes that erased themselves, leaving evidence that would sublime before trial.

In her car, she found a yellow pencil on the dashboard. Not there when she'd arrived. Warm as fever. Eraser intact but soft, ready to remove whatever truth she was about to write.

She drove toward her storage unit, the one where she kept Melody's drawings, the ones that were dissolving, the ones that might remember what she'd forgotten.

## Chapter Two: The Investigators

August 15, 7:15 AM

Ernest Vance watched the seventeenth alarm on his phone expire without touching it. Not the first, which meant wake up. Not the fifth, which meant leave apartment. Not the eleventh, which meant arrive at office. The seventeenth meant nothing except that seventeen was how many minutes Zeca had been underwater.

His apartment occupied the thirty-first floor of a building that swayed in wind. His possessions occupied two bags by the door—always packed, always ready. The walls were white, undecorated except for water damage that looked like contour maps of places that didn't exist. His refrigerator hummed at 59.7Hz, just under the 60Hz standard, a deviation that made his teeth ache.

He stood at the window, counting. Prime numbers against his thigh with his left hand—2, 3, 5, 7, 11—while his right held an empty coffee cup he'd already forgotten wasn't full. Below, San Francisco dissolved into fog that moved wrong, upstream against wind, carrying something that made the birds fly inland.

His phone buzzed. Text from Supervisor Harrison: "Harrington Industries. Now. Ask for Chen."

Ernest knew three Chens in financial forensics. Santijn Chen was the one who'd been convicted, the one who'd been exonerated, the one who investigated her own crimes without knowing it. He'd read her files. All of them. Even the ones that didn't exist, that appeared in his desk drawer with no explanation, written in handwriting that looked like his but couldn't be because he'd never seen them before.

He tried to drink from the empty cup. Set it down. Picked it up. Set it down. The ritual of almost-consuming, a performance of normalcy for no audience.

The bathroom mirror showed him clearly for exactly three seconds before fogging from shower steam that had no source—he hadn't showered in two days, afraid of dissolution, afraid water would wash away whatever was holding him together. In those three seconds, he saw Zeca's face. Not similar. Identical. The way identical twins are identical, except Zeca had been identical to himself, one person who'd become two, or two who'd been one, or—

The eighteenth alarm went off. This one he'd added yesterday. No significance. Just noise to break the pattern of meaning, to dissolve the ritual into randomness. He let it ring while he dressed: gray suit, white shirt, black tie. The same combination for 1,847 days. Tomorrow would be 1,846.

In his jacket pocket: a yellow pencil. Not his. Never his. But always there, edges worn smooth from unconscious counting. Eight edges on a hexagonal pencil. The geometry was wrong, had always been wrong, would always be wrong.

He left the apartment at 7:47 AM. The security log would show 7:43. The elevator camera would show 7:51. The lobby timestamp would show [ERROR]. He existed in the gaps between surveillance, investigating crimes that happened in the spaces between recorded moments.

The drive to Harrington Industries took him through fog thick enough to have weight. His headlights carved tunnels that sealed behind him. The radio played static at 17Hz—below hearing but not below feeling. His bones resonated at that frequency, skeleton becoming tuning fork for a note that meant nothing good.

The parking lot was full of empty cars. That specific fullness that meant everyone had arrived but no one was there. Security waved him through without checking credentials, eyes focused on something seventeen inches behind his head.

Inside, the building felt exhaled—like something had been holding its breath for hours and finally given up. The elevator panel showed floors -1 through 71, though the building only had 30 floors. He knew her by posture alone—the way investigators stand when they're documenting absence. She turned before he announced himself, as if she'd been expecting him, as if they'd done this before.

"Ernest Vance, SEC," he said, though his badge was in his car, though he wasn't sure SEC existed the way he thought it did.

"Santijn Chen. I know who you are." She gestured at the empty server racks. "The crystals are gone. Sublimated between 6:47 and 7:15. But they left impressions."

"Forty-seven million, dispersed in increments under ten thousand. Classic dissolution pattern, but..." She paused, looking at her tablet. "The patterns are familiar. Like I've seen them before."

Ernest pulled up his own records. He'd been tracking similar patterns for three years, possibly four, possibly forever. His files went back further than his employment, further than his education, further than Zeca's death which may not have been death but transformation.

Twenty-three.

"Who's the center?" Ernest asked.

Chanson's hand shook. The pencil fell, hit the ground, shattered like glass. Inside wasn't graphite but something organic, something that smelled like cedar and chemicals and childhood fear.

"I don't know. Every time I get close to remembering, I dissolve. But they're here. In San Francisco. Have been since 1987."

Ernest's phone showed 8:47 AM. The security logs would show they'd been in the server room for three hours. Their conversation would be recorded as silence. The investigation would continue without progress, as it had for thirty years.

"We need the children," Santijn said. "The other twenty. If we can find them—"

"We've found them," Ernest said, pulling up his files. "We've been finding each other for years. We just keep forgetting."

"How do you know?"

Chanson smiled, sad as old paper. "Because we've done this before. Seventeen times. Maybe eighteen. Each time we get closer. Each time we forget more. Eventually, one of us will remember everything or forget everything, and either way, the protocol completes."

He left. Santijn and Ernest stood in the empty server room, surrounded by absence that had weight, evidence that existed in negative space.

"My daughter," Santijn said suddenly. "Melody. She draws things before they happen. Houses that burn down three days later. People who die the next week. She's been drawing server rooms full of crystals for months."

Ernest thought of Zeca's videos. Frame 4,381, the one that didn't exist yet: "Tell Santijn to check the storage unit. The one she doesn't remember renting."

"We should go," he said.

They left together but in separate cars, taking different routes to the same destination. The city dissolved around them—fog eating architecture, heat making solid things soft, time moving in directions that calendars couldn't track.

In Ernest's pocket, the yellow pencil reformed from shattered pieces, warm as fresh bread, impossible as everything else.

He had 1,846 days left.

Or had already been gone for 1,846 days.

The distinction mattered less with each passing hour, each dissolution, each investigation that led back to a circle of poisoned children trying to write their way out of truth.

## Chapter Three: The Pattern

August 15, 11:47 AM

The storage unit existed in a part of San Francisco that maps forgot. South of abandoned warehouses, north of nothing, in an industrial zone where street signs had dissolved into rust and suggestion. Santijn's GPS died three blocks away, screen flickering between coordinates that couldn't coexist—latitude from here, longitude from elsewhere, altitude from below sea level though she could see the bay.

Unit 1847. She didn't remember renting it, but the key was on her chain, worn smooth between keys she used daily. The lock knew her touch, opened before the key fully turned, as if eager to share what it had been holding.

Inside: her daughter's drawings. Hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, stacked in banker's boxes labeled with dates that progressed backward—2024, 2023, 2022—then jumped to 1987, 1988, 1989. Melody's work and someone else's, a child's hand that might have been Santijn's, might have been anyone's from Ravenholl.

She pulled the nearest box. August 2024. Last week. These should be at Melody's foster home, should be on refrigerators and bedroom walls, should be anywhere but here in a storage unit she didn't remember visiting.

The first drawing paralyzed her mid-step.

Yellow pencils arranged in a circle, points inward, forming a sun that burned black. In the center, barely visible in crayon wax: a server room, crystals growing from floor to ceiling, and a figure that could have been Santijn, could have been anyone, dissolving into particles of color.

Dated August 14. Yesterday. Before the crystals formed. Before the call came.

The next: Pier 47 at night, fog thick as wool, two figures at the water's edge. One falling, one watching, though the perspective made it impossible to tell which was which. The fog formed letters: "1847 DAYS REMAIN."
