Quiet Lives

Thunk Thunk

Part One: Tokyo

Keiko's therapist had suggested knitting, but yarn made her think of things unraveling, so she bought stamps instead. Then thought: why buy them? She carved her own five-year-old face from rubber, working from a school photo where she was missing a front tooth.

"Is this healthy?" her sister Yui asked. They were in Keiko's kitchen, which smelled like the instant ramen neither of them would admit to eating.

"Compared to what?" Keiko said, inking the stamp purple. Thunk. A child face on fabric. Thunk. Another.

It was August. Tokyo was doing that thing where the air felt pre-chewed. The apartment's air conditioner wheezed like it was personally offended by summer.

By September, she'd covered three meters of cotton with tiny versions of herself. The early prints were clear—you could see the missing tooth, the uneven bangs her mother had cut. But she kept using the same stamp without cleaning it properly, and the faces started degrading. Getting fuzzy. Some were just purple smears with the suggestion of eyes.

By October, her right thumb had a permanent purple bruise from pressing. People at the convenience store started avoiding eye contact, probably thinking it was domestic violence. She didn't correct them.

"This is genuinely creepy," Yui said, not unkindly.

"I know."

The thing nobody tells you about anxiety is how boring it is. The same thoughts, over and over. Did I lock the door? Better check. Did I check? Better check if I checked. Keiko figured if her brain was going to repeat everything anyway, she might as well make it visible.

She posted one photo online—just a corner of the fabric, maybe fifty faces. Her caption was just "thunk thunk thunk." Somehow this got three thousand likes overnight. Someone DM'd her thirty photos of their own face, progressively blurrier. Someone else asked if she sold the actual stamp, said they wanted to "continue the degradation." A third person sent a video of themselves hitting their desk with a spoon, same rhythm, for ten minutes straight.

Her second piece: stamping her passport photo onto her old university swimsuit. The striped one she'd worn for 6 AM laps in the pool, back when swimming felt like the only way to make her brain shut up for forty-five minutes.

She pressed too hard on the seventy-third stamp. The fabric tore.

She stared at the rip for so long her eyes went unfocused. Then she remembered her grandmother's sewing box, the one with gold thread that cost more than dinner.

The stitching took four hours. Her repairs looked like tiny gold veins. Or maybe like those diagrams of neural pathways in textbooks. Or maybe like nothing but thread holding fabric together.

Part Two: Lisbon

Miguel's gallery in Bairro Alto had the kind of tiles that made tourists take photos, but he covered them partially with her fabric faces, which felt either perfect or sacrilegious.

"Why Portugal?" he asked while they hung the pieces. His English had that careful quality of someone who'd learned it from subtitled Breaking Bad.

"My therapist said I should travel more. This counts as therapy if there's art involved, right?"

He laughed. Not politely, but actually laughed. "I told my therapist I was quitting smoking. Instead, I switched to a different brand. Still counts as change."

The gallery smelled like old wood and those cleaning products that only exist in Europe. Keiko touched the traditional tiles, thinking how someone had probably stamped the same pattern five hundred years ago. Different from her faces but also exactly the same—repetition as prayer, as proof, as insanity, as art.

Opening night: wine in plastic cups, people saying "fascinating" in four languages, someone's baby crying in the corner.

A woman stood in front of the swimsuit piece for so long that other people started avoiding that wall.

"I count my heartbeats," the woman said finally, quietly. "During meetings. On trains. Started when I was twelve."

"How many?" Keiko asked.

"Today? 47,293. Approximately."

They stood there, two women who counted things that didn't need counting.

"I stamped this 1,847 times," Keiko said.

"Odd number."

"I know. It bothered me too."

The woman smiled. Actually smiled. She bought the piece and later sent a photo of it in her apartment—she'd added her own stitches in silver thread, fixing tears Keiko hadn't even made.

Back in Tokyo, Keiko's mother called at 2 AM, forgetting time zones again.

"I saw the photos online. All those little faces."

"Mm."

"You hated school photos. You used to hide in the bathroom."

Had she? Memory was unreliable. Or maybe it was reliable and the present that kept changing.

"I still have my old swimsuit," her mother said. "The striped one like yours. We bought them at that department store that closed. You insisted we match."

Keiko made a sound that wasn't yes or no.

That night—or morning, her schedule had gone liquid—she carved two stamps. Five-year-old her. Current her. She alternated them on fresh linen. Thunk thunk. Child adult. Thunk thunk. The sound like a very slow heartbeat or a very lazy drum circle.

The child stamp cracked on the fortieth press. She kept using it. Now it only printed half a face, sometimes just an eye and part of a smile. More accurate somehow.

She stitched these with thread from 7-Eleven, the kind that comes with a needle too thick for the fabric but you use it anyway because it's 3 AM and what else is open. White. Visible but not trying to be special about it.

Miguel emailed: "There's a man who comes every day. Just stands there. Lost his daughter last year. He doesn't speak. Is this okay?"

Keiko thought about her thousands of faces, degrading with each copy. About the woman counting heartbeats. Her mother saving a swimsuit for no reason except that's what mothers do.

"Tell him Tuesday afternoons are quiet," she wrote back. "Better for standing."

Her new piece: fabric with no stamps, just gold thread stitching closed tears that didn't exist yet. Or acknowledging tears that were already there, invisible, waiting.

The needle went in. Out. In. Out.

The sound it made wasn't thunk but more like a whisper. Or breathing. Or nothing.

She kept going anyway.

Outside, Tokyo was waking up or going to sleep—she'd lost track. Someone's air conditioner dripped steadily on her window. Thunk. Thunk. Thunk.

She almost laughed. Almost.
