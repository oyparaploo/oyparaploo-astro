Pressure Ghosts (fourth hour)

Third Movement: When Singing Becomes Sight and the Shelterless Teach Us About Home

There's a pointed critique that needs to be made about institutional barriers, and we might as well make it plainly: the systems designed to protect us have instead perfected the art of marginalization. Watch how they treat shelterless citizens—not as people experiencing a temporary condition but as a permanent category, a problem to be managed rather than human beings to be housed. Watch how they treat handicapped citizens—building a world of stairs and assuming everyone can climb them, then acting surprised when some bodies refuse to cooperate with architectural violence.

The elder gardener had been trying to explain this through the seventeen unnamed senses, but let me translate it into language that institutional barriers might understand: When you design a society that only works for certain bodies, certain incomes, certain neurologies, you're not creating stability—you're creating pressure ghosts. You're creating the vibrational imprint of everyone you've excluded, and that sound never stops. The Goon Ox can hear it. Some of us are learning to.

Here's what's interesting about singing as a powerful tool: Desta discovered it after she lost her spoken voice. She would stand at the intersection in her Barney costume and hum—not words, just sustained tones that her damaged vocal cords could still produce. And something remarkable happened. People who would have walked past a woman holding a sign about radical infrastructure would stop for the humming. There was something about wordless sound that bypassed the part of the brain that argues, that critiques, that finds reasons to dismiss. The humming went straight to the part that remembers being comforted, being held, being told without words that existence is worthwhile.

But let's interrogate this to expose the inequities that render music artists disabled in ways we rarely acknowledge. Because here's what happened next: Desta tried to record her humming, to share it more widely, to maybe earn enough to pay for the throat surgery that might restore her speaking voice. And she discovered that every platform, every venue, every opportunity required things she didn't have: a permanent address for contracts, a bank account for payments, identification that matched her current name and gender, the ability to navigate forms designed for people whose lives fit into standard boxes.

The music industry—like most industries—had accidentally-on-purpose made itself inaccessible to artists living in extreme deprivation realities. You could have the voice of an angel, but if you didn't have a fixed address, you were essentially invisible. Your talent didn't matter if you couldn't prove your existence to the satisfaction of bureaucratic systems that measured humanity through documentation.

This is how souls become invisible in mainstream discourses. Not because they lack value or beauty or power, but because the infrastructure of visibility requires resources they don't possess. The discourse talks about "emerging artists" and "raw talent" and "authentic voices" but only if those voices can afford to be amplified through sanctioned channels.

So artists living in extreme deprivation realities put out a call to dismantle cruel and unusual hierarchies. Not politely. Not with the deference that established systems expect from those seeking entry. But with the authority of people who have survived conditions that would have destroyed the comfortable critics judging their work. They said: The hierarchy that puts housed artists above shelterless ones, able-bodied performers above disabled ones, documented citizens above undocumented ones—this isn't natural order. It's constructed violence. And we refuse to accept it.

The story needs to embrace a broader empathetic understanding here, which means we need to meet a new character who perceives reality in a way that helps us see what we've been missing.

Her name was Yuki, and she heard with her eyes. Not metaphorically—she had a rare form of synesthesia where visual input created auditory sensation. When she watched Desta hum at the intersection, Yuki didn't just see a woman in a costume making sound. She saw the sound itself as color and movement, saw how it created ripples in the air that touched people before they consciously registered hearing anything. She saw pressure ghosts the way the Goon Ox heard them, except her medium was light rather than vibration.

Yuki spent her time contemplating dimensions that are free from all traces of oppressive occupation. She would sit in public spaces and visually-auditorily map them, identifying where surveillance cameras created specific pressure patterns, where architectural design funneled people into controllable flows, where public benches had been designed with armrests specifically to prevent shelterless people from lying down. She could see the occupation in the architecture itself, the way control had been literally built into the environment.

But she was also planning for reclaiming agency, and this planning featured a couple of wonderful aspirations. First, she wanted to create what she called "visual soundscapes"—public art installations that people with any sensory configuration could experience. If you could see, you'd experience it one way. If you could hear, another way. If you had the elder gardener's seventeen unnamed senses, yet another way. The point was to prove that beauty didn't require standard-issue perception.

Second, she wanted to document the pressure ghosts—create a visual archive of all the people and possibilities that institutional barriers had erased. She called it "The Museum of Invisible Citizens," and it would feature Desta's humming translated into color, the Goon Ox's wobbling translated into spatial dynamics, Karim's experience of checkpoints translated into something people who'd never lived under occupation might begin to comprehend.

This brings us to a distinct perspective that combines political analysis with cultural critique. Because what we're really examining here is how power operates through making certain people perceptible and others invisible. Political power decides who counts as a citizen, whose suffering counts as newsworthy, whose death counts as tragedy versus statistic. Cultural power decides whose art counts as legitimate, whose voice counts as beautiful, whose story counts as worth telling.

The institutions don't just fail to see Desta, Karim, the Goon Ox, the elder gardener, and Yuki—they actively invest in not seeing them. Because truly seeing them would require acknowledging how the system is designed to produce their suffering. And that acknowledgment would demand change.

Now, let's offer some universal thoughts about the complex toll of extreme conflicts combined with potential climate change natural disasters, and the toll it can take on humanity as a whole. Because here's what was happening while Desta hummed at intersections and Karim watched checkpoints burn and Yuki mapped oppression through visual-auditory synthesis:

The world was learning that violence and disaster don't stay in their assigned categories. The wildfire that consumed Karim's green zone sent smoke across three countries, affecting the lungs of people who'd never heard of his village. The drought that preceded the fire was linked to climate patterns disrupted by industrial emissions from nations that had never experienced checkpoint control. The refugees fleeing both fire and occupation arrived in cities where shelterless populations were already being criminalized, adding to the pressure ghosts that the Goon Ox could hear growing louder every day.

Everything was connected, which meant everyone's suffering was connected, which meant everyone's liberation had to be connected too. You couldn't solve climate disaster without addressing the military conflicts that prevented coordinated response. You couldn't solve extreme deprivation without addressing the systems that made land, water, and shelter into commodities rather than rights. You couldn't solve any of it without first learning to perceive it accurately, which meant developing new senses, questioning language's supremacy, listening to pressure ghosts.

Here's an example of absurd humor involving the concept of physical immediacy, because even in the darkest analysis we need moments that reveal how ridiculous our situation has become:

The Goon Ox, wobbling on four uneven legs, tried to apply for disaster relief after the wildfire. The form asked for a permanent address. The Ox, whose existence was defined by instability and movement, wrote: "The ground, wherever it currently is beneath my feet." The form asked for proof of citizenship. The Ox, who existed across conceptual boundaries, wrote: "I am a citizen of pressure, a resident of vibration, a national of the frequencies that stable creatures cannot perceive." The form asked for a signature. The Ox, who had no hands, pressed one hoof into an inkpad and left a mark that could have meant anything or nothing.

The form was, predictably, rejected. But the Goon Ox kept the rejection letter, because it was perfect evidence of how institutions measured reality: through parameters that worked for some bodies and therefore assumed they should work for all bodies. The physical immediacy of the Ox's suffering—its hunger, its exposure, its need for shelter—meant nothing compared to its failure to properly document that suffering in formats designed by and for entirely different forms of existence.

Finally, let's verbally choreograph a spatially dynamic setting that features lives defined by suffering, because understanding requires us to see the stage where all this takes place:

Picture a urban intersection where five roads meet at awkward angles, creating a small triangular island in the middle where nothing official was supposed to exist. This is where Desta stood in her Barney costume, humming. The intersection was flanked by a condemned apartment building where shelterless families had created unauthorized homes, a check-cashing place that charged 30% fees to people who couldn't access banks, a boarded-up clinic that used to provide free healthcare before funding disappeared, a convenience store with bars on the windows, and a bus stop where people waited for transportation to jobs that didn't pay enough to move them out of this neighborhood.

This spatial dynamic told you everything about how suffering gets concentrated. The people here weren't randomly distributed across a neutral landscape—they'd been pushed here by institutional barriers, by discriminatory housing practices, by gentrification that labeled certain areas "investment opportunities" and others "blight zones," by the simple mathematics of who gets resources and who gets abandoned.

In this space, the Goon Ox wobbled between stopped cars, the elder gardener tended to weeds growing through sidewalk cracks with the attention most people reserved for roses, Yuki documented pressure ghosts through her visual-auditory perception, and Karim—having fled the burned checkpoint—arrived with ash still in his hair, looking for anyone who might understand what it meant to live under conditions no human should have to endure.

And above all this, in this spatially dynamic setting defined by suffering, Desta hummed. Wordlessly creating the sound that said: we are still here, we are still human, our invisibility to your systems doesn't make us any less real, and one day you'll develop the senses needed to perceive us properly, and on that day you'll realize we were the ones who understood reality all along.

The pressure ghosts are getting louder. Can you hear them yet?
