Brrd Roots ~ United States Revolutionary War Story ~ Version 10

Words / 2025 Variety

War smells like the awkward, damp corner of the world—where humanity’s broken plans tend to pile up like soggy laundry. That’s what the farmer-turned-soldier thought as he crouched low in the trench, gripping his musket like it was the only thing tethering him to sanity. Around him, the battlefield clamored like a poorly rehearsed orchestra: cannons bellowing like they’d skipped anger management therapy, soldiers yelling vague directives (“FIRE WHERE?!”), and the peculiar thud of musket balls finding their mark, which always sounded a bit too much like someone trying to dig a hole with their face.

And then, slicing through the cacophony, came the clink-clink-clink of metal. It was quiet enough to be suspicious.

“What in the name of God’s soggy blessings is that?” he muttered, peering through the haze of smoke, mud, and regret.

Ten paces away, there sat a soldier. He looked almost serene, perched in the mud like he’d chosen it deliberately, stirring what was—bafflingly—a very small pot of soup.

“Morale soup,” the soldier announced when he caught the farmer’s confused gaze. He raised the pot, perhaps as a toast. “Mostly water. Sometimes… vibes.”

“Soup?” the farmer repeated, stunned into paralysis. “Vibes?”

The soldier didn’t even blink. “Would you like a spoon?”

But before the farmer could answer, the officer’s whistle ripped through the air, and suddenly everyone was running—or attempting to, because the mud wasn’t cooperating. The farmer stumbled forward, his boots sucked deeper into the muck as though the earth itself was trying to stage an intervention. He fired his musket blindly, mostly in protest against the situation. Across the field, a British soldier crouched behind a wall and raised his own musket. They locked eyes for the briefest moment, and the farmer thought: Does he also hate this? Does he dream of leaving the gun and opening a pub? Does he know my boots are stuck?

The whistle screamed again, shattering his train of thought. The farmer sighed. “Reload!” someone bellowed behind him, which sounded less like an order and more like a cruel joke.

Elsewhere, tucked high in the branches of a particularly judgmental-looking tree, a young sharpshooter held an intense moral debate with God. His Pennsylvania Long Rifle rested steady against his shoulder, but his heart thumped like it was auditioning for an opera.

Below him, a British officer was stomping back and forth, shouting commands about proper posture and accurate aiming. It was unclear whether anyone was listening, but the officer’s buttons shone brilliantly in the sunlight, which felt rude given the circumstances.

The sharpshooter took aim. And then his brain, the traitor, started up again: What if he’s secretly nice? What if he crochets blankets for orphans? What if all this yelling is just his therapy exercise?

“Alright, God,” the boy muttered, clearly exhausted. “If I miss, let’s call that Your way of saying stop. If I hit, uh… I don’t know. Good aim?”

Bang.

The officer fell dramatically, as though auditioning for the role of “Most Flamboyant Collapse.” The sharpshooter slumped back against the tree, letting the rifle drop. Below, squirrels paused their foraging to briefly judge the sharpshooter’s moral dilemmas before returning to acorn duty. Somewhere nearby, a bird sang a suspiciously cheerful tune.

At Valley Forge, things had taken a turn for the theatrical. The snow wasn’t just cold—it was mockingly cold, piling high around the half-built cabins as though daring anyone to hope for survival. Inside, soldiers huddled like damp laundry, their feet wrapped in burlap that had clearly been designed by an enemy.

One soldier coughed until he passed out, slumping dramatically against the wall like he was auditioning for a tragedy. His friend dragged him closer to the sputtering fire, muttering, “If you die, you’re giving us all ideas.”

Another soldier sat in the corner, clutching a crumpled letter. He’d read it so many times that the ink was threatening to leave out of protest. “It’s all falling apart,” he mumbled, although whether he meant the war or his life was unclear.

Across the room, a man pulled his last piece of bread from his pocket, tore it in half, and handed the other piece to the despondent soldier. “Eat it,” he said. “Before you disappear too.”

The soldier stared, speechless. “Why?”

The bread-giver shrugged. “I don’t know. I guess… because screw the snow?”

The march to haul cannons from Fort Ticonderoga had officially become a nightmare. Snowdrifts rose around them like judgmental mountains, and the wind screamed louder than anyone’s internal breakdowns. One soldier stopped abruptly, pointing into the storm with a trembling hand.

“There,” he whispered, his voice full of terrible certainty. “The British general. He’s a ghost now. And he’s judging us.”

“Keep moving!” Knox barked, clearly at war with his own sanity. “Nobody cares about ghost generals!”

A soldier behind them started laughing—a wild, almost musical sound that felt utterly inappropriate given the circumstances. “We’re dragging these giant metal beasts across the wilderness just so we can WHAT? Blow more people up? Someone explain how this counts as progress!”

Knox didn’t respond. But when one man fell, Knox saw another step forward to pull his rope, a silent act of rebellion against the snow’s very existence. Knox paused, then thought: The cannons aren’t the burden. We’re the burden. And somehow, we’re still pulling.

By the time Yorktown arrived, everyone was tired enough to question reality itself. Smoke hung heavy over the trenches, the kind of smoke that smelled like existential exhaustion. Washington paced the line, stopping beside a soldier slumped over the body of his best friend.

“If you fall, we all fall,” Washington said, delivering the line like he’d practiced it in front of a mirror.

The soldier glanced up, unimpressed. “Sir, it feels like we’ve already fallen.”

Washington, clearly not in the mood to argue, pulled the soldier upright. It wasn’t so much leadership as it was just… standing. But sometimes standing was enough.

When Cornwallis surrendered, the white flag fluttered in the breeze, embarrassed by the entire situation. Nobody cheered. They stared at the flag as though waiting for it to yell, “Just kidding!” But silence prevailed.

War, the farmer-turned-soldier decided, smelled like a moldy root cellar. Damp, earthy, and faintly sour, it lingered in the mud-filled trenches and clung to their clothes like shame. He crouched low, gripping his musket tightly, though his hands shook from more than the cold. Around him, the noise rose in waves—cannons roaring like drunken gods, men shouting orders that dissolved into screams, and the gut-wrenching thud of bodies hitting the earth.

And yet, through it all, came the persistent clink-clink-clink of something metallic. He frowned and leaned forward, squinting through the haze of smoke and misery. There, ten paces away, sat a soldier calmly stirring a tin pot balanced precariously on a tiny flame.

“What in God’s name are you doing?” the farmer hissed, disbelief slicing through his fear.

The soldier didn’t look up. “Morale soup,” he answered, his voice flat but oddly calm. “Mostly water. Sometimes a pinch of salt. Once, I found an onion.” He paused, tasting it with the solemnity of a judge delivering a verdict. “Today, it’s just water.”

“Morale soup,” the farmer repeated under his breath, almost laughing at the absurdity of it. But before he could ask another question, the officer’s whistle shrieked, and the world turned itself inside out.

Mud sucked at their boots like a greedy child refusing to let go, and the farmer stumbled forward, barely managing to keep his musket level. He fired once, the recoil slamming into his shoulder with enough force to knock him off balance. In the smoke-filled aftermath, he saw a British soldier crouched behind a broken wall, his face pale and taut with fear. For a fleeting, surreal moment, the farmer thought: Does he hate this as much as I do? Does he pray to go home at night? Does he wish he could just leave the gun and run?

The thought dissolved as quickly as it came. “Reload!” someone yelled, and the farmer’s body moved out of instinct, his hands mechanically working the musket. There was no time to wonder about humanity—not here, not now.

Elsewhere, perched high in the branches of a gnarled tree, a young sharpshooter whispered to himself. His Pennsylvania Long Rifle was steady, though his heart pounded loud enough to drown out the forest’s silence. Below him, a British officer paced back and forth, shouting commands in crisp, clipped tones. The officer’s buttons gleamed in the sunlight like tiny golden stars.

The sharpshooter took aim, but his finger hesitated on the trigger. What if he’s got children? What if he writes poetry by candlelight? What if he’s only shouting to drown out the voice in his head that says, ‘Go home, go home, go home?’

“Alright, God,” the boy murmured under his breath, his voice cracking. “If I miss, that’s your way of stopping me. If I hit—well, I’ll take that as a sign too.”

The rifle cracked. The officer fell. The sharpshooter leaned back against the tree trunk, letting the rifle slip from his hands. Below him, squirrels scattered, unbothered by the moral weight pressing down on his chest. The sun continued shining, oblivious to what it illuminated.

At Valley Forge, survival wasn’t just a battle—it was a lottery. The snow was relentless, burying what little hope remained under freezing drifts. Inside the cabins, men huddled around sputtering fires, their feet wrapped in rags barely thicker than their pride.

One soldier, his breath rattling in his chest, coughed until he passed out. His friend, too exhausted to care, dragged him closer to the fire, muttering, “If you die, you’ll be giving the rest of us ideas.”

Another soldier sat in a corner, staring at a crumpled letter in his hands. He’d read it so many times the words were seared into his mind, yet he couldn’t stop. “It’s all falling apart,” he whispered, though whether he meant the war or himself was unclear.

Across the room, a man with hollow cheeks tore his last piece of bread in half and offered it without a word. “Eat it,” he said simply. “Before you disappear too.”

The other soldier hesitated, his hands trembling. “Why?”

The bread-giver shrugged. “Because we’re still here.”

The march to haul cannons from Fort Ticonderoga defied logic. Snowdrifts towered over the soldiers, the wind howling through the trees like a taunting specter. One man stopped abruptly, pointing into the swirling white.

“There,” he whispered, his eyes wide and haunted. “Do you see him? The British general. He’s floating. Laughing at us.”

“Keep moving,” another soldier muttered through chattering teeth. But his eyes darted nervously toward the horizon, as if he half-believed the vision.

Behind them, a soldier burst into hysterical laughter, loud and sharp enough to cut through the wind. “Look at us!” he cried. “Dragging these stupid things so we can WHAT? Blow each other up better? Someone pinch me. This can’t be real.”

Knox, their leader, didn’t respond. He trudged forward, his jaw tight. But when one man fell into the snow, too exhausted to rise, Knox saw another soldier step forward, wordlessly taking the fallen man’s rope. For a moment, he thought: The cannons aren’t the burden. We’re the burden. And we carry each other, because that’s what keeps us human.

Yorktown should have been the climax, but it felt like a sad, strange joke. Smoke choked the trenches, and soldiers crawled through mud that smelled like iron and decay. Washington walked the line, his face grim and heavy. He stopped beside a man slumped over the body of his closest friend.

“If you fall, we all fall,” Washington said quietly.

The soldier didn’t look up. “Feels like we already did, sir.”

Washington didn’t argue. Instead, he knelt and helped the man to his feet. Sometimes, standing was the only rebellion left.

When Cornwallis surrendered, the white flag fluttered weakly in the breeze. No one cheered. The battlefield was silent, thick with ghosts. The men stared at the flag as though waiting for someone to shout, “Just kidding!” But no one did.

The Revolutionary War wasn’t just about independence—it was about survival, about stubborn hope clinging to the edges of despair. It didn’t answer all the big questions, but it left behind small flickers of light: a neighbor lighting a fire for a grieving widow, a soldier sharing his last crumb of bread, an unspoken connection between enemies too tired to hate.

It was absurd. It was surreal. It was human. And maybe, somehow, that was enough.

War smells like secrets gone bad—like a root cellar where someone tried to hide turnips from God but God found them anyway. That’s what the farmer thought as he crouched low in the trench, gripping his musket tightly. His hands were shaking, though whether from fear, cold, or existential indignation, he couldn’t tell. Around him, the symphony of chaos roared—cannons bellowing like gods who didn’t know where to aim, men shouting orders like lost tourists, and, of course, the ungrateful thud of bodies hitting mud.

But cutting through it all was a quiet and persistent clink-clink-clink sound that seemed, against all odds, very smug.

“What in God’s name is that?” he muttered, craning his neck toward the noise.

There, ten paces away, sat a soldier—calm as a man on vacation, stirring a tin pot that looked like it had been stolen from a dollhouse.

“Morale soup,” the soldier replied without looking up. “Mostly water. Sometimes a pinch of salt. Once, there was a potato. Today, it’s just vibes.”

The farmer blinked, as though staring harder might explain things. “That’s not how morale works.”

“Well,” the soldier said thoughtfully, “it hasn’t killed me yet.”

And, somehow, the farmer felt worse.

Elsewhere, a sharpshooter perched in the branches of a tree was losing a very personal argument with God. His Pennsylvania Long Rifle was steady—years of hunting squirrels had taught him precision—but his heart was refusing to cooperate. Below him, a British officer paced back and forth, his coat so red it looked actively hostile. The officer’s buttons gleamed in the sun, and the sharpshooter could swear they were mocking him.

He aimed, his finger hovering over the trigger. Then, like a traitor, his brain began its usual nonsense: What if he has kids? What if he’s secretly an excellent violin player? What if he never wanted to shout at people about “flanking maneuvers” and only does it because yelling drowns out the sound of his own internal screaming?

“Okay, God,” the sharpshooter whispered. “If I miss, that’s your sign. If I hit, well—let’s call that divine endorsement.”

Bang.

The officer fell. The sharpshooter sat back against the tree trunk, letting the rifle slip from his hands. Below him, squirrels scattered like journalists running to cover the scandal, unimpressed by humanity’s moral dilemmas. The sun continued shining, utterly indifferent to what it illuminated.

Valley Forge was less a place and more an elaborate insult. The snow piled up like it was being paid overtime, burying the men under drifts so deep they began to suspect divine meddling. Inside the cabins, soldiers stared at sputtering fires as though willing the flames to take pity on them.

One soldier coughed so hard he passed out, collapsing into the dirt like a puppet whose strings had been cut. His friend, more annoyed than concerned, dragged him closer to the fire while muttering, “If you die, you’ll be giving us all ideas.”

Another soldier sat in the corner, clutching a crumpled letter that didn’t make sense anymore. “It’s all falling apart,” he murmured to no one, though if the fire had ears, it surely would have agreed.

Across the room, a man tore his last piece of bread in two and handed half to the despairing soldier. “Eat it,” he said.

“Why?” the soldier asked, his hands trembling.

The bread-giver shrugged. “Because if I’m gonna suffer, I’m not doing it alone.”

The march to haul cannons from Fort Ticonderoga had started as a noble idea but had, by now, descended into fevered absurdity. Snow drifts rose higher than dignity, the wind screamed through the trees like it had a vendetta, and sanity was as scarce as rations. One soldier stopped mid-step, his eyes wide as he pointed into the swirling white.

“There,” he whispered. “Do you see him? The British general. Floating. Laughing at us.”

“Keep pulling,” Knox growled, though his voice carried less leadership and more desperation. “Nobody cares about your ghosts.”

A soldier behind them began laughing—a wild, unhinged sound that cut through the storm. “Dragging THESE giant metal beasts so we can blow each other up? What part of this sounds like freedom?”

Knox ignored him but felt quietly betrayed by reality itself. Yet, when another man collapsed into the snowbank, Knox watched someone else step forward to pull his rope. For one surreal moment, Knox thought: We’re not dragging cannons. We’re dragging each other. And if that isn’t ironic, I don’t know what is.

When Yorktown finally came, it arrived more like a question than an answer. Smoke hung heavy in the trenches, the kind of smoke that smelled like regret. Washington walked the line, stopping beside a soldier slumped over the body of his best friend.

“If you fall, we all fall,” Washington said with the conviction of a man who had run out of speeches.

The soldier didn’t look up. “Feels like we already did.”

Washington didn’t argue. Instead, he pulled the soldier upright, as though standing was itself a declaration of war.

When Cornwallis surrendered, the white flag waved faintly in the breeze, utterly embarrassed by its role in the whole affair. The men didn’t cheer; they stared at it, waiting for someone to yell “Just kidding!” But no one did.

The Revolutionary War wasn’t neat, triumphant, or even particularly logical. It was ridiculous, human, and tender. It was morale soup in the trenches, squirrels pretending not to care about morality, and soldiers sharing crumbs of bread just because.
