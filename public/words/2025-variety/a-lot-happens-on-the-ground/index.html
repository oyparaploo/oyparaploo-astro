Doing Something Together

A Lot Happens On The Ground

After the eviction notice, a child artist and her bankrupt neighbors discover that the real revolution isn't in the sky or in theory but right here on the pavement, where paper puppets and mausoleum ashes and inflatable dinosaurs become weapons of joyful disorder.

"We are so glad to land," the child whispered, sliding off the dumpster. Her name was Maybe. Maybe eight, maybe nine. She'd been counting by eviction notices instead of birthdays. This was her seventh.

"On earth soil?" Jean-Debris asked gently. He'd lost his house yesterday. Three generations in that house. His grandmother's roses still grew in the yard he couldn't visit anymore. Found Maybe crying behind the courthouse, her trash bag of possessions ripped, everything scattered.

"On concrete," added Moth Delacroix, sitting down beside them. The concrete was cold. November in Cleveland. Her unemployment ran out six weeks ago. "It's... it's solid at least."

"On asphalt," contributed Peal, whose car was home now. 1998 Honda Civic, 287,000 miles, heat didn't work. "On anything really."

"On wildflowers that... that aren't here but..." Maybe's voice trailed off. She pulled out a dandelion she'd picked from a crack in the courthouse steps. Already wilting. "Sorry. That's stupid."

"No," Jean-Debris said. "I see them too. If you squint. My grandmother said you could eat these. During the Depression. When she was your age."

They were behind the bankruptcy courthouse. Maybe had been sleeping here three nights, since Mrs. Patterson's house—foster home number seven—got foreclosed. Had this trash bag of things she'd found, things people threw away that still had use in them.

"I have stuff," she said quietly. "If you want? It might be dumb."

She pulled things out carefully. Glow makeup from a dollar store dumpster—expired but still glowed. An urn of something. ("Found it," she whispered. "By the old cemetery. Nobody was visiting anymore. Is that bad?") Burned papers from someone's backyard fire. Paper for folding she'd taken from the library recycling.

"What do we do with it?" Moth asked.

"I don't know. Maybe we could... paint? Or just sit? I'm not good at knowing. Mrs. Rodriguez said I don't follow directions right. Before she had to give me back."

She started painting Moth's hand with the glow paint. Small dots. Like stars. Like the stars you couldn't see in Cleveland anymore because of the light pollution.

"Is this okay?" Maybe kept asking. "Should I stop? I can stop."

"No, it's... nice," Moth said, starting to cry a little. Nobody had touched her gently in months. Not since the hospital. Not since the overdose. Not since recovery. Not since losing her kids.

A dog wandered over. Skinny. Ribs showing. Collar but no tags.

"I call him Revolution sometimes," Maybe said, sharing half her sandwich—peanut butter from the food bank, bread from the same. "Or Senator Woofington when I'm trying to be funny. Is that a stupid name?"

"It's perfect," Peal said. The dog settled between them, warm.

Maybe tried making a paper puppet but her hands were shaking. Cold or hunger or fear. All three probably. "They're supposed to have normal arms but I always mess up and make too many. Mrs. Chen said I don't do art right."

"I like the extra arms," Jean-Debris said, helping her fold. His own hands were steady. Carpenter's hands. Before the medical bills. Before the bankruptcy. Before yesterday.

A police officer walked over. Maybe froze. Last time, they'd taken her to another home. Another family that couldn't keep her.

"We're not doing anything wrong," she said quickly. Small. Making herself smaller. "We can move. We'll move."

The officer looked at them. Three broken adults and a terrified kid with glow paint and paper puppets. His own daughter was Maybe's age. His own house was in foreclosure—police salary wasn't what people thought.

"What are you making?" he asked, sitting down. The gun made Maybe flinch.

"We don't know," Maybe admitted. "Something? Nothing? I found this phone but it's broken so we can't even take pictures of it."

"That's okay," the officer said. Took off his belt, set it aside. The gun farther from Maybe. "Can I have some paint?"

Maybe painted one tiny stripe on his hand. "Is that too much? I can take it off. I'm sorry."

"It's just right."

More people drifted over. Other evicted folks. Some from the camps under the bridge—the city had cleared them out again, but people had nowhere else to go. Then—

"Oh!" Maybe gasped.

Three people walked up in inflatable costumes. A T-Rex. A unicorn. A pizza slice. Absurd in the gray November day.

"We drove up from Columbus," one said, voice muffled. "Heard people were gathering. Are we... is this okay?"

"I don't know what's okay," Maybe said honestly. "But you can sit with us?"

She tried painting the T-Rex but the paint wouldn't stick to the plastic. "Sorry. I'm messing it up. I mess everything up. That's why nobody keeps me."

"No, look—glitter might work?" someone suggested gently. The unicorn. Inside was a woman, a teacher, lost her job in the budget cuts.

Maybe found some glitter in her bag. Threw a tiny handful. It stuck to the T-Rex. Sparkled in the weak sunlight.

"Is that stupid?" she asked.

"No," everyone said.

The national guard arrived. One soldier with a rifle. Maybe started crying. Silent tears. The kind she'd learned in foster homes—crying that doesn't make noise, doesn't bother anyone.

"Please, we're not... we're just sitting. We're just—"

The soldier lowered his rifle. Saw the kid. Saw the paint. Saw the ridiculous sparkling dinosaur. His own son had been taken by child services. Different state. Different reasons. Same system.

"My daughter would love this," he said quietly. Lied. No daughter. But Maybe needed to hear something gentle. Sat down. "Can I... can I just sit?"

Maybe painted one tiny dot on his boot. "To make them less scary," she explained in a whisper.

Days became weeks. Word spread somehow. Not about a protest or revolution. Just about a place where you could sit. Where a kid would paint your hand if you wanted. Where inflatable costumes were welcome. Where the ground was hard but had people on it.

"Why do you keep saying no endpoints?" a reporter asked Maybe. Local news. Slow day. Human interest story about the "occupation" that wasn't really an occupation.

"I don't know what it means really," Maybe admitted. "Someone said it once. I think it means... we don't have to stop? We can just keep sitting here? Is that wrong?"

She was arranging things on the ground. Not directing anyone. Just moving rocks and papers into patterns. The way she'd arranged her few possessions in seven different foster homes. Making space feel like hers even when it wasn't.

"A lot happens on the ground," she said quietly. "Not big things. Just... things. Like ants. And dropped pennies. And people sitting. And dandelions in cracks."

The T-Rex person (Moth had climbed inside to keep warm) was dancing slowly with the soldier, who was crying about something. His son. The system. The rifle he carried for $31,000 a year.

"Is this revolution?" the reporter asked.

"I don't know what that is," Maybe said. "We're just... here? On the ground? Where stuff is?"

She pulled out the urn she'd found.

"I think someone was in here," she whispered. "Someone important to somebody. Should we... should we do something nice with them?"

Everyone helped sprinkle the ashes in a circle. Maybe planted the dandelion in the middle. It wouldn't grow. November. Concrete. But she planted it anyway.

"Is that okay?" she asked the sky. "Was that the right thing?"

Senator Woofington/Revolution barked once. Softly.

"We are so glad to land," Maybe said, barely audible.

"Where?" Jean-Debris asked.

"Here. Wherever here is. Even if it's nowhere special."

The cop was still taking pictures with the broken phone, pretending it worked to make Maybe happy. The soldier was teaching the pizza slice person a gentle dance—something his son had liked. Peal was making a paper puppet orchestra. Each puppet had too many arms. All of them broken in the right places.

"I don't know if we're doing this right," Maybe said to no one, to everyone.

"Neither do we," everyone answered.

"Okay," she said. "Then maybe we can be confused together?"

She painted one more thing on the ground. A smiley face. But lopsided. With worried eyebrows. Starting to fade already from foot traffic.

"Why the worried eyebrows?" someone asked.

"Because," Maybe said, eight years old and already seven homes deep into understanding the system, "even happy faces get scared sometimes."

The T-Rex nodded. Or maybe that was just the wind. Hard to tell. But it felt like understanding.

Winter came. They stayed. The city gave up trying to move them. Too much bad press about displacing a child. Maybe kept painting hands. Keep arranging things on the ground. Kept saying they were glad to land even when the landing was concrete, was cold, was nowhere that wanted them.

"A lot happens on the ground," she told a city official who came to negotiate. Wanted them to move to the official shelter. The one where families got separated. Where Maybe would go back into the system.

"Like what?" he asked. Frustrated. These people were making his job harder.

"Like this," Maybe said, and painted a tiny star on his shoe. "Like people being together when everything else falls apart. Like Senator Woofington getting fed. Like me having seven hundred parents instead of seven houses I don't belong in."

She wasn't wrong. Everyone had started taking care of her. Teaching her things. Math from Jean-Debris. Music from Peal. How to stay warm from Moth. How to make yourself small when systems came looking from everyone.

"This isn't sustainable," the official said.

"Neither is foster care," Maybe said. "Neither is seventeen fifty an hour. Neither is concrete. But here we are. On the ground. Where stuff actually is."

They stayed. Through winter. Through everything. On the ground where a lot happens, where eight-year-olds teach adults about landing, where paper puppets have too many arms like everyone needs, where glow paint makes minimum-wage hands look like stars, where the revolution isn't coming because it's already here, on the ground, where Maybe lives, where we all live, where a lot happens even when nothing is supposed to happen at all.

We are all landing somewhere between lost and found, between falling and sitting, discovering that what happens on the ground isn't revolution but something quieter—scared children and broken adults finding each other, painting small stars on hands, sharing ashes with dandelions, learning that maybe we don't have to know what we're doing to know we're better doing it together, here on the ground where everything actually happens, where foster kids teach physics, where concrete grows dandelions in November, where a lot happens especially when nothing is supposed to.
