When Opinion Becomes Gospel

The Dangerous Alchemy of Not Listening

← Words / 2025 Variety

Calcification

When no one challenges your opinions, they harden into "truths." Without the scrutiny that listening brings, thoughts go untested, unrefined. Facts can withstand examination; opinions need protection. So the non-listener builds walls, keeping opinions safe from the revelation of being merely... opinions.

Repetition becomes reinforcement. Say something a hundred times without contradiction and it starts feeling like universal law. The speaker who only broadcasts begins believing their own performance, forgetting they were performing.

I watched this happen to a podcast host who started with "I think" and "maybe" but after two years of monologue was declaring absolute truths about complex topics. No guests, no challenges, just him and his microphone and his increasingly rigid certainties.

Confidence Game

Social media rewards certainty. "Studies suggest possibly" gets three likes. "THIS IS THE TRUTH" gets three thousand. We're incentivized to dress opinions as facts, and when no one listens deeply enough to challenge them, the costume becomes permanent.

Without listening, we can't distinguish between someone sharing researched fact and someone sharing opinion with conviction. Confidence becomes truth's proxy—whoever sounds most certain must be most correct. Yet often the most certain have listened least, their views never complicated by other perspectives.

A journalist friend tracks this phenomenon. She found that people with the strongest opinions about immigration had the least actual contact with immigrants. Those who knew immigrants personally held more nuanced views. Experience through listening complicates; isolation allows simplification.

Those who listen extensively tend toward tentative statements—they've heard the complexity. But "it depends" doesn't win arguments. "I could be wrong" gains no followers. The careful distinguishers get drowned out by confident conflators.

House of Cards

When non-listeners meet, they trade opinion-facts like currency. "My facts versus your facts" when it's really opinions all the way down. Calling opinions "facts" makes them feel unchallengeable—a shield against having to defend positions.

People build entire worldviews on these foundations, unchallenged assumptions dressed as truth. Testing would require listening to counterarguments, contradicting evidence, different experiences. Instead, every personal experience becomes universal law. "This happened to me, therefore this is how the world works"—a sample size of one becoming cosmic truth.

Collective Delusion

Entire communities can share this conflation. Similar opinions echo in closed chambers, no outside voices penetrate, and groupthink transforms opinion into accepted fact. Agreement feels like verification though it's merely shared opinion.

I studied a investment club that lost everything in 2008. They'd convinced each other that housing prices could never fall—opinion that became fact through repetition and agreement. When reality contradicted their "facts," they couldn't process it. Several members still insist the crash was an anomaly, not proof they were wrong.

When reality doesn't match these "facts," the collision is devastating. Rather than revise, they deny or rationalize—revision would require listening to reality's feedback. Admitting facts were opinions shatters identities built on being "factual."

Crystallization

Opinions defended as facts become more rigid over time, crystallizing into dogma. Dogma cannot coexist with listening—it requires deafness to survive. One good question, one piece of contradicting evidence could bring everything down.

So contradicting views get labeled "fake" or "biased" while maintaining that personal opinions are objective facts. Without listening, there's no verification, no source-checking, no logic-testing. That work requires hearing experts, evidence, reasoning, challenges.

Information Ecosystem

Actual facts are often complicated, qualified, contextual. Opinion-facts are simple, absolute, universal—easier to tweet, more compelling to share. The information ecosystem rewards opinion-facts over actual facts, punishing careful listeners who say "Well, actually, it depends..."

In non-listening culture, compelling wins over correct. People catch the vibe, the confidence, the simplicity—surface passing for depth when no one dives deep.

Breaking the Cycle

Start by modeling the distinction. Be clear when sharing opinion versus fact. Use "I think," "I believe," "in my experience." Ask others genuinely: "Is that your experience or researched fact?" Not as attack but curiosity: "Help me understand what you're basing that on."

Listen to their answer—not to debunk but to understand their reasoning. Even flawed reasoning must be understood before it can be addressed. In the process, discover your own conflations. Some of your "facts" are opinions too.

This revelation threatens but also liberates. Opinions can change; facts are fixed. Recognizing opinions AS opinions means you can evolve, adjust, refine—but only if you listen to what challenges and enriches them.

Clarity

Otherwise you're stuck with opinion-facts growing more rigid and wrong over time—the calcification of ignorance prevented only by listening's softening force. Listening keeps opinions fluid and facts solid, instead of the backwards situation where opinions are solid and facts treated as fluid.

Stop broadcasting long enough to receive. Hear the distinction between "I think" and "it is." Honor both for what they are—opinions as opinions, facts as facts. The clarity that comes from listening.
