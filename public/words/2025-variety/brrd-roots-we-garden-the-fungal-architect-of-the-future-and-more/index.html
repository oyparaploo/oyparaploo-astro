Running Amok Along The Outskirts

Running amok along the outskirts symbolizes a state of untamed energy—a rebellion against structure, a chaotic impulse breaking away from the established center. It suggests movement beyond the boundaries of convention, where control dissolves and raw, primal forces take over. Uncertainty thrives in the outskirts, where new ideas ferment. If running amok along the outskirts describes chaos breaking free at the edges, then its opposite would be moving with measured precision at the heart of order. Instead of reckless abandon, we have deliberate control—a force that operates with careful intent rather than wild unpredictability. Instead of the outskirts, where boundaries dissolve, we shift to the center, where structure is strongest and rules are upheld.

Bread, Chaos, and the Philosophy of the Outskirts.

The agitated man leaned back on the worn bench, eyes flickering across the park’s controlled disorder—a place where civilization blurred at the edges, where reality frayed just enough to let absurdity slip through the seams. He sighed sharply, shaking his head.

"You talk as if all this—" He gestured broadly—at the duelists still nursing their metaphorical wounds, at the pigeons conducting their silent symposium, at the accordion player whose mournful tune seemed to mock the universe. "—has some grand purpose. But isn’t purpose just something we force onto randomness?"

The poet-gardener tilted his head, considering. He plucked a small leaf from the vine beside him, turned it in his palm. "Purpose isn’t forced—it’s discovered. And the outskirts are where discovery thrives." His voice was steady, slow, as if unearthing something delicate.

The agitated man exhaled sharply. "That sounds poetic, but not everything grows. Some things rot. Some things break and scatter and never find meaning at all." His fingers drummed against his knee, restless.

The poet traced the veins of the leaf absentmindedly. "Even decay has its place. The remains of something lost become the foundation for something new. Take this park—" He gestured at the space around them. "It’s messy, unpredictable. But isn’t that precisely why it breathes? Why it feels alive?"

The other man scoffed. "That’s the kind of thinking that lets everything spiral out of control." His voice tightened, sharp edges forming around his words. "If you let the outskirts take over, the city crumbles. If you let chaos run amok, order disappears."

A pause. The poet smiled, a knowing glint in his eye. "But are you sure you want order? Or just certainty?"

That landed. The restless man frowned, arms crossing as if bracing against something unseen. His gaze drifted to the duelists, now sharing a cigarette, their conflict resolved by nothing more than exhaustion. To the pigeons, unmoved by philosophy or fate. To the accordion player, playing not for applause, but simply because the sound needed to exist.

He shook his head. "Certainty is survival." His voice was quieter now.

The poet gently tucked the leaf into the soil beside him, pressing it down like punctuation at the end of a thought. "And yet, survival has never been certain." He wiped his hands on his coat, dirt smudging the fabric. "That’s why we plant, knowing some things won’t grow. That’s why we create, knowing some things will be lost. That’s why—" His gaze lifted, locking onto the other man’s. "That’s why people duel with bread. Because why not?"

A silence stretched between them.

And then, for the first time, the agitated man laughed—short, surprised, almost reluctant. But real.

The accordion hummed. The city loomed in the background, still polished, still structured. But here, on the outskirts, life ran amok—and maybe, just maybe, that was precisely how it was meant to be.

The Outskirts of Reason Wildflowers Baguettes and Life at the Edge

The countryside of southern France stretched wide, rolling in gentle waves of green and gold, dotted with wildflowers that swayed lazily in the breeze. Poppies burned red against the fields, lavender hummed in soft purple clusters, and the occasional burst of yellow broom flickered like sunlight caught in petals. The air carried the scent of earth warmed by the afternoon sun, mingling with the distant whisper of vineyards and the slow murmur of cicadas.

A man in a long trench coat wandered along a dirt path, his steps uneven, exaggerated, as if life itself were a theatrical performance and he, the reluctant protagonist, had missed the opening monologue. He adjusted his beret with the solemnity of a philosopher preparing to deconstruct existence, though his true aim was far simpler—a pastry.

Near a crumbling stone wall, a gathering of seasoned thinkers leaned into their conversations, their scarves wrapped like unfinished sentences, their words unfolding in delicate pauses. A man with a scarf far too ambitious for the season exhaled with gravity, his words landing like autumn leaves:
"Life is but a fleeting absurdity—like a baguette left uneaten in the rain."
A few nodded. Someone murmured, "Oui, oui."

Then—impact. A figure burst into existence, limbs spiraling, motion cascading like a thought that had outrun its logic. He was pursued, though not by consequence or fate, but by a mime whose silence had mastered the art of reprimand. Their chase traced the very borders of control—the neat rows of vineyards standing in polite formation, contrasted by the wild, untamed beauty of the countryside’s outer reaches. It was here, at the edges, where the duel would take place.

The outskirts had always been a place of disorder. The edges of villages, the fringes of thought, the margins of control—these were the spaces where things unraveled, where the rules weakened, where the unexpected thrived. And now, in the lawless expanse between civilization and bedlam, a duel was about to unfold.

The fugitive, desperate, reached for the nearest object—an abandoned baguette, balanced between decay and significance. He wielded it like a relic of a forgotten kingdom, while the mime, unfazed, unsheathed an invisible blade with the weight of tradition itself.

Spectators leaned forward. The accordion hummed in suspense. A single petal detached from a wildflower, swirling downward in perfect timing, as if nature itself had been pulled into the spectacle.

And then—the inevitable surrender. The mime threw his hands into the sky, conceding defeat to the sheer audacity of the moment. The duel, sprouted from nothing, returned to nothing.

The countryside settled. The accordion, burdened yet resilient, whispered back into its melody. Somewhere, a pigeon adjusted its posture, as if contemplating the meaning of sudden shifts in the universe.

And beyond the madness, at the far edge of a wooded expanse, Thich Nhat Hanh walked. His steps were effortless, the earth arranging itself with quiet reverence beneath him. Here, meaning was not chased. It emerged, like green leaves unfolding toward light, like thoughts arriving when no longer summoned.

The outskirts had run their course. The countryside sighed, breathed, grew—its chaos no longer disruption, but part of the rhythm.

Verbatim ~ A Word’s Unwavering Journey Through the Mild Chaos of Language (Poem)

Verbatim ~ A Word’s Unwavering Journey Through the Mild Chaos of Language (Poem)

Verbatim walks with steady feet,
Through whispered words and phrases sweet,
A traveler dressed in borrowed sounds,
Yet never lost—it stays unbound.

Spanish hums in rolling song,
French sighs soft, then moves along,
Japanese crafts it crisp and bright,
Yet meaning holds—its grip is tight.

But can a word remain the same,
When accents dance and tongues misframe?
Each voice a tide, each phrase a flight,
Yet verbatim stands upright.

A vow unbent, a thought intact,
Yet touched by hearts, in dialects stacked—
For language lives, it sways, it glows,
Yet through it all, verbatim knows.

Golden Dance of Innocence and Love

Imagine Jesus sitting by the water’s edge, the sun slipping golden ribbons across the sea. His laughter is light, like a breeze that stirs the wildflowers, inviting people not just to believe but to delight—to breathe deeply, to see the world as if it were freshly spun by love itself. Innocence, in this way, is more than purity; it’s a kind of openness, a willingness to stand in the wide, unfolding beauty of existence without walls around the heart.

And God’s love? It’s honey-warm and endless, pouring like sunlight over everything, filling every space where doubt once sat. It’s a love that doesn’t demand perfection but sings, “Come as you are.” It doesn’t clutch or control—it frees, like the way a meadow opens wide for the sky.

So innocence and God’s love walk hand in hand, like two dear friends wandering barefoot down a quiet, sun-dappled path, knowing that wherever they go, grace goes too.
