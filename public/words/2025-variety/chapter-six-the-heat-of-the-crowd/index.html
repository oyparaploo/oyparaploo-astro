Chapter Six

The Heat of the Crowd

← Words / 2025 Variety

Paris announced itself before they even stepped off the train.

Where Austria had been fog-throttled and Collioure storm-stung, Paris was noise swollen beyond reason. The Gare de Lyon bellowed with arrivals and goodbyes. Pigeons clapped their wings against the rafters in ungainly percussion. A busker’s accordion ribboned uneven through the din, its bellows squeaking on tired leather. Wheels screeched metal arcs across the tracks. Every sound arrived layered, uninvited, and insistent.

Troll Tearsen let it wash over her without resistance. To fight would be to drown. Beside her, Libaux Torrini elbowed through the crowd with his usual bluntness, muttering curses in a register too low to claim accountability.

“Different orchestra,” he said, glancing at the neon blur of taxis outside.

“Different conductor,” Troll replied.

They didn’t smile.

Pigalle at night smelled of wet plaster, frying oil, perfume bought with reckless wages. The drizzle had streaked every neon sign, turning colors into veils. The streets were bright but never bright enough; shadows lodged in every doorway, unwilling to be rented.

The club they found had no name, only a red door narrow enough to look temporary. Inside, mirrors fractured the space until it felt like walking into a kaleidoscope designed by someone with too much affection for knives. A singer leaned into her mic, her voice trembling at the edges, reverberant and ribboned like torn silk. Patrons laughed too loudly, glasses clinked with metallic force, and underneath all of it the bass acted like a second heart—thudding, steady, impatient.

Every sound demanded tribute. Troll remembered Helene Klausmun’s steady voice back in Fontanella—Detective Tearsen, we’ve held the scene for you—and felt the memory throb like a private metronome against this excess.

At the bar, Libaux ordered without looking. The shaker rattled like marbles colliding in cloth. Troll’s fingers tightened on the counter. She thought she heard glass ticking, small spheres moving where they should not be.

The bathroom was tiled in sterile white. Every sound grew teeth. Faucets dripped into choruses. Shoes squeaked into duets. A door hinge groaned into harmony with itself.

She leaned into the sink and splashed water across her face. The tiles returned the slap three times, like a delayed confession.

Then she heard it.

A marble rolling across ceramic.

tik-tik-tik… deliberate, measured, circling.

She turned, but the floor was empty. Her reflection stared from the mirror seam, fractured into two unsympathetic faces. The sound lingered beyond its source, stretched thin across tiles, refusing to vanish when logic said it should.

She crouched, hand sweeping along grout. Nothing. Only the echo remained, so long it felt recorded and replayed.

When it died, it did so reluctantly.

Back at the table, Libaux raised an eyebrow. “You look like someone told you a joke you weren’t supposed to laugh at.”

“I heard a marble.”

“Then we’re in the right theater.”

The singer reached too high on a note and her voice cracked like ice under weight. The crowd laughed in response, not cruel but complicit. Noise drowned failure, as if to erase it before it embarrassed anyone.

And then Troll saw him.

Fredda Stremler.

Older, hair disciplined to obedience, suit that did not acknowledge weather. His shoes were immaculate, gleaming in the half-dark like punctuation. He smiled while speaking to a young woman, but the smile was a mask pulled tight, too clean at the edges.

Libaux stiffened. “If we move now, we spook him.”

“If we wait, he evaporates.”

Stremler turned. His eyes passed over them and for an instant landed. He smiled wider, as if recognizing audience members in the back row. He placed something small on the table beside him, then guided the woman toward the exit.

Troll shoved through bodies. The room pressed back with elbows, laughter, bass. When she reached the table, she saw it.

A marble.

Clear, flawless, reflecting neon into shreds. The bass made it hum faintly—mmm… mmm—as though the music was inside it.

She picked it up. It was warm.

Libaux’s voice was nearly lost in the noise. “Calling card.”

“No,” Troll said, steady. “Surveillance.”

Outside, drizzle smudged neon into watercolor. Cars hissed past on wet asphalt. A group of pigeons scattered with a commotion that sounded almost orchestrated.

Libaux’s phone rang. He frowned, answered.

Uma Arouje’s voice cut crisp as glass: “Report. Now.”

He looked at Troll, then at the pigeons clattering their wings against a lamp-post. “Our only witnesses are pigeons,” he said dryly. “And they’re very percussionist.”

Uma didn’t laugh. The line went dead.

Troll looked at the marble in her hand. For a moment it was Nunito Volker’s again—the rigid hand in Fontanella, the green swirl under fog. Then Miller Corbal’s, cracked along the breakwater. Now Stremler’s, pristine, humming to the rhythm of Paris bass.

Bookends. And a middle.

She closed her fist around it.

They walked to the end of the street where the crowd thinned. Noise retreated into muffled pockets behind doors. Somewhere above them, a shutter banged, then banged again, irregular percussion that seemed like a signal.

Libaux lit a cigarette out of habit, then remembered, pocketing it half-lit. The ember hissed against drizzle and died with a sigh.

“Do we follow?” he asked.

“Not yet,” Troll said. “He wanted us to see him. And to see this.” She opened her hand to show the marble gleaming against her palm.

The streetlamp doubled its light in the glass. She could swear the echo of bass still vibrated inside it, though the club was already behind them.

Paris, she thought, was a city that never allowed silence to be true.

Libaux nudged her elbow. “So? Do we call it evidence?”

She closed her hand again. “No. We call it applause.”
