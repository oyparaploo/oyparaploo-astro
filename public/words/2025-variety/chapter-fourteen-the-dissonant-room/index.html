Echoes of Glass Stones

Chapter Fourteen

The Dissonant Room

The city outside Vienna’s conference quarter looked unchanged—trams shrieked their light curves, bicycles ticked their chains like polite metronomes, cafés folded linen over small tables. But Troll Tearsen carried the corridor’s E in her ear, the pure pitch that refused to leave. She could still hear it in the marble floor, in Meuller’s spectrum notes, in Stremler’s silence.

Libaux Torrini walked beside her, coat collar raised as if to keep words from following. “He’s not planting marbles anymore,” he said. “He’s tuning buildings. Whole cities if we let him.”

“Cities want to be tuned,” Troll replied. “They’re desperate for order.”

They turned down a narrow street where façades leaned like old conspirators. The air here had no conference polish—just damp brick and cigarettes. Somewhere behind shutters, a piano practiced scales, each note soft, searching, like a child reluctant to show its throat.

The safe flat Uma had arranged was meant for obedience: white walls, narrow beds, a desk too small for papers to argue on. They set their marbles out anyway. Clear. Red. Cracked. Blue. White-coil. Seeded. They looked like the only constellations anyone had permission to carry.

Troll rolled the white-coil across the desk. It gave its signature tik and a whisper like a breath too tired to finish. She logged it on a page already filled with Meuller’s spectrum data, arcs of pitch, decay times, resonant tails.

Libaux sprawled on the bed, shoes still on, watching the ceiling as if the plaster might spell something. “You’ve noticed he only leaves them where echo thrives. Shelters. Clubs. Breakwaters. Towers. Archives. Now corridors. Places that remember their own voices.”

“He’s writing a lexicon of spaces,” Troll said. “A grammar in glass.”

“And Vienna?”

She looked toward the marbles, lined like obedient children who would one day betray their teacher. “Vienna is the test. If marble can be tuned to marble, then policy becomes performance. He doesn’t need applause anymore—he builds it.”

The phone on the desk buzzed. Aïcha:

RIVAJE subsidiary controls a real estate fund. Guess where the properties cluster: transport hubs, concert halls, empty buildings ‘awaiting renovation.’ He’s buying resonance. He’s buying echo itself.

Another buzz. Mage Meuller:

If he plays buildings like instruments, then he’s composing across borders. You need to disrupt the score. Send me live captures. I’ll look for weaknesses in the harmonic net.

And from Helene Klausmun:

Fontanella is restless. More marbles. No bodies yet. The valley sounds wrong—like waiting. Please send me anything. We’re holding the scene again, but for how long?

Troll read them all. The desk seemed smaller.

Libaux sat up. “So, do we dismantle his instruments?”

“Or rewrite the music,” Troll said.

She picked up the cracked marble. It caught the weak lamplight and fractured it into pale nerves. “Noise becomes truth if repeated. Truth becomes noise if tuned. We need dissonance—something the building can’t digest.”

Libaux smiled, the kind of smile that carried tiredness like a second coat. “Finally, a job I’m qualified for.”

They tested the flat. Troll hummed a single note into the wall. It came back slower, fatter, warped by plaster. Libaux clapped once; the room gave him three different tails as if unsure which version of him it believed. The marbles rolled across the floor in small arpeggios, imperfect and impatient.

“This place already disagrees with itself,” Troll said.

“Good. We make it louder,” Libaux said.

Troll imagined the conference building across town, polished, tuned, perfected to carry Stremler’s myth without resistance. She imagined planting dissonance in its ribs—her mother’s name whispered into vents, the cough track replayed at the wrong time, marbles rolled where no chalk marks waited.

Her hand closed around the clear marble. It was cool and ordinary. She thought of her mother’s breath, the voicemail’s curtain theater. She thought: grief itself is dissonance.

She placed the marble back with the others. “Tomorrow,” she said.

“Tomorrow,” Libaux echoed.

The room returned the word in two pitches at once, as if deciding which version to keep.
