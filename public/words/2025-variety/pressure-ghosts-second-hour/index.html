Pressure Ghosts (second hour)

Words / 2025 Variety

Second Movement: When the Green Zone Burns and Language Fails

The all-heart moment came unexpectedly, the way genuine compassion always does. The woman in the Barney costume—her name was Desta, though few people had ever asked—knelt beside a child crying at the intersection. She didn't speak. She couldn't anymore, having lost her voice to illness months before. Instead, she communicated through gestures, through spatial elements that created a bubble of safety around them both, through body sensitivity that let her mirror the child's breathing until it slowed, steadied, became something survivable again. This is what the elder gardener had been trying to teach everyone: that connection doesn't require words, that sometimes the most profound communication happens in the space between language and silence.

But there was a mischaracterized discourse happening all around them, a conversation that insisted this moment was about individual kindness when it was actually about structural violence. People saw a woman comforting a child and thought "how sweet" when they should have been asking "why is this child alone and terrified at an intersection in the first place?"

The Goon Ox understood this better than most. From its wobbling vantage point, it could perceive the place of despair and destruction that undergirded everything—the invisible architecture of suffering that held up the visible world like scaffolding. It could hear the pressure ghosts of demolished homes, of families separated by borders drawn with imperial confidence on maps that bore no relationship to how people actually lived.

We need to revisit our host of assumptions about connection, the Goon Ox thought in its peculiar way, which wasn't quite thinking and wasn't quite sensing but something in between. Humans believed connection required proximity, shared language, mutual understanding. But the Ox knew better. Connection was what happened when pressure ghosts recognized each other across impossible distances, when the sound of one person's suffering created vibrations that another person's body learned to receive.

That's when the sweet alien voice entered the narrative, though calling it "alien" misses the point. This voice belonged to the elder gardener, and it sounded alien only because humans had forgotten how to hear languages spoken by roots and fungi and the patient stones that held soil in place. The voice said: "Your thoughts should linger with dignity, even when they're about terrible things. Especially then. The terrible things deserve witness, not dismissal."

There was one last resort for understanding what was happening here, and it required looking directly at the character nobody wanted to think about—the man who lived his entire life near blockade razor wire, in that narrow strip of land where sovereignty had been argued over for generations, where the very ground beneath his feet was contested territory.

His name was Karim, and he was reexamining the meaning of life while caught in what the occupying forces called a "green zone"—a designation that suggested safety, normalcy, permission to exist. But his green zone was experiencing a wildfire that was presently out of control, and he couldn't help but notice the bitter irony. The authorities who controlled his movement, who decided whether he could visit his sister three kilometers away, who required permits for him to harvest olives from trees his grandfather had planted—these same authorities now told him the wildfire was a "natural disaster" requiring his patience and cooperation.

The incident that described surreal carnage happened at dawn. Karim watched through his window as the fire consumed the checkpoint where soldiers had questioned him hundreds of times over his lifetime. The razor wire glowed orange, then white, then began to melt. In that moment, he witnessed a scene where rigorous interrogation tried to normalize extreme privilege—even as they fled, the soldiers demanded he help them, commanded him to assist with evacuation procedures he had never been trained for, expected his compliance with the same casual authority they'd always wielded, as if the fire cared about their permits and protocols.

The imagery that emerged conveyed sounds reaching a crescendo and then falling off: the roar of flames, the screams of soldiers realizing their weapons couldn't shoot fire, the collapse of the guard tower, and then... silence. Not peace. Just the absence of the particular violence that had structured every day of Karim's existence.

This moment questioned the established hierarchy that had placed spoken language at the pinnacle of meaning. Because in the fire, orders meant nothing. Commands disappeared into smoke. The entire apparatus of bureaucratic violence that operated through paperwork, verbal authorization, and official discourse simply... stopped mattering. What mattered was movement, survival, breath. The Goon Ox would understand: sometimes you have to lose stable footing to perceive truth.

Later, after the fire, a hopeful character told a brief story about a future where freedom, justice, and self-determination could be attained. Desta—still in her Barney costume, now smoke-stained and torn—wrote the story on cardboard because she still couldn't speak: "There will be a time when the razor wire stays melted. When the checkpoints remain ash. When the green zones are just gardens again, and people grow what they want to grow, and go where they need to go, and the only permits required are the ones we give each other through mutual respect and recognition of each other's humanity."

Now we need to shift this building narrative to defy a typical lens, because the typical lens wants to separate these things into categories: natural disaster versus human-caused violence. But what if we reduce both Mother Nature storm and human-caused violence to their common denominator—a humanitarian crisis that doesn't care about your taxonomy, your need to sort suffering into manageable boxes with different funding streams and appropriate responses?

The elder gardener, speaking in that sweet alien voice through seventeen senses humans don't have names for, explained it this way: "The fire that burns the forest and the fire that burns human dignity are temperature and intention away from each other. Both create refugees. Both destroy homes. Both leave ash that future generations will have to learn to grow in."

Linger there, the gardener instructed. Stay in this unfamiliar dream that includes deprivation intersections—those places where multiple forms of loss meet and compound each other. Where losing your home to fire meets losing your freedom to checkpoints meets losing your voice to illness meets losing your faith that any of this means anything.

This is where the novel we're building has to live: in that uncomfortable space where the Goon Ox wobbles, where pressure ghosts speak, where Desta gestures love into existence without words, where Karim watches razor wire melt and wonders if liberation can arrive through destruction, where the elder gardener tends to grief like it's a garden that will eventually bloom into something we don't yet have language to name.

The seventeen unnamed senses are starting to make sense now, aren't they? They're the senses we need to perceive what's really happening when official discourse tries to separate connected things, when privilege pretends normalcy, when language sits at the pinnacle claiming supremacy over other ways of knowing.

Listen. Can you hear the pressure ghosts? They're the sound that existence makes when it's forced into spaces too small to contain it, when barriers that shouldn't exist create vibrations of wrongness that some bodies learn to feel even when stable creatures miss them entirely.

This is the novel. This is what we're building. And it refuses to comfort us with easy categories or simple solutions, because the truth is wobbling, unstable, perceived differently depending on which senses you're using and how many you have access to.
