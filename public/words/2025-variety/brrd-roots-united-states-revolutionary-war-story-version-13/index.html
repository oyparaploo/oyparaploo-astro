Brrd Roots ~ United States Revolutionary War Story ~ Version 13

Words / 2025 Variety

War wasn’t just loud—it was a mix of chaos and comedy, like a poorly executed play where no one knew their lines. The farmer crouched low in the trench, gripping his musket like it might whisper something reassuring. Around him, cannons boomed with the enthusiasm of forgotten gods, soldiers shouted orders they barely believed, and faintly—persistently—there was the soft clinking of a tin pot.

“Surely not,” the farmer muttered, exhaustion dragging his voice low. He craned his neck toward the sound, squinting through the haze. Just down the line sat a soldier, cross-legged in the mud, calmly stirring a tin pot as though the war was none of his business. Steam rose from the pot with a sort of quiet dignity, indifferent to the surrounding carnage.

“Morale soup,” the soldier said solemnly, catching the farmer’s eye. He lifted the pot with the reverence of a priest presenting holy relics. “Mostly water. Sometimes salt. Hope floats on occasion.”

The farmer blinked. “Soup?”

“Would you like a spoon?” the soldier offered, as serious as one could be under the circumstances. The farmer did not dare ask where he was keeping a spoon.

Before he could respond, the officer’s whistle tore through the air—a sound so sharp it seemed designed to insult their very existence. Everyone scrambled to their feet—or tried to—boots suctioned into the mud like the earth itself wanted to keep them still. The farmer fired his musket out of instinct, though he suspected it would do no good. Across the field, a British soldier crouched awkwardly behind a crumbling wall, his hat tilted sideways like a sad cartoon.

For one absurd moment, the farmer thought, Does he hate this mud too? Does he dream of putting down the musket and picking up a pen? Does he even know his hat looks like a wilted cabbage?

The whistle screeched again, snapping him back to reality. “Reload!” someone shouted, their voice sharp but tired, like a parent scolding a child for the fifth time that day. The farmer sighed and complied because sometimes survival demanded blind obedience.

High in the gnarled branches of an old tree, a young sharpshooter muttered prayers to whoever might be listening. Below him, a British officer paced back and forth, barking commands about flanking maneuvers with the conviction of a man who wanted to be anywhere else. His red coat gleamed unnecessarily in the light, his buttons glittering like they were mocking everyone in attendance.

The sharpshooter raised his rifle. He steadied his aim. Then, just as he was about to fire, the thoughts came uninvited: What if he has children who wait for him every evening by the door? What if he writes poetry about flowers blooming in spring? What if he yells not because he’s angry, but because he’s afraid?

“Alright, God,” the boy whispered, his voice shaking. “If I miss, that’s You telling me to stop. If I don’t… well, that’s on You too.”

The rifle cracked. The officer fell. Below, squirrels scattered, utterly indifferent to the heavy moral weight of the moment. The sharpshooter slumped back against the tree trunk, letting the rifle fall from his hands. He stared at the sky, muttering something about futility and the unfortunate consistency of acorns.

Valley Forge wasn’t just cold—it was cruel. Snow piled high, smothering hope under layers of frost, while cabins leaned against each other as though seeking comfort. Inside, soldiers huddled around fires that coughed more than they burned. Their feet were wrapped in burlap, their spirits held together by sheer stubbornness.

One man coughed until he fainted. His friend dragged him closer to the fire, muttering, “If you die, you’ll give the rest of us ideas. Bad ones.”

Another soldier sat in a corner, staring at a letter so worn it looked ready to dissolve. He whispered to it as though the words might come alive and save him: “It’s all falling apart.” No one dared to ask whether he meant the war or himself.

Across the room, a man tore his last piece of bread in half and thrust it into the whisperer’s hands. “Eat it,” he said bluntly.

The whisperer stared at the bread like it might answer the questions life refused to. “Why?”

The bread-giver shrugged. “Because despair tastes worse.”

The march to haul cannons from Fort Ticonderoga began with dignity and ended in chaos. Snow drifts rose higher than ambition, the wind howled like it wanted to tear their hope to pieces, and somewhere in the storm, a soldier pointed toward the horizon.

“There,” he whispered, his eyes wide. “The British general. A ghost. He’s floating. Laughing at us.”

No one laughed, but no one dismissed it either. Behind them, another soldier burst into hysterical laughter, loud and sharp like it was trying to compete with the storm.

“Cannons? We’re dragging these monsters through THIS? For WHAT? Somebody pinch me. This has to be a fever dream.”

Knox, their leader, trudged forward without a word. When one man collapsed into the snow, too tired to rise, another stepped forward, picked up the rope, and pulled. Knox paused briefly, watching. It’s not the cannons that matter. It’s us. It’s always been us, dragging each other forward through the mess.

Yorktown didn’t feel like a victory—it felt like a question hanging unanswered in the smoke-filled air. The trenches reeked of sweat, failure, and something metallic no one dared name. Washington walked the line, stopping beside a soldier who sat slumped in the mud, his hand resting on the shoulder of his fallen friend.

“If you fall, we all fall,” Washington said, his voice heavy.

The soldier looked up, his face pale and hollow. “We’re already on the ground, sir.”

Washington didn’t argue. He pulled the man upright without ceremony because sometimes standing was the only defiance left.

When Cornwallis surrendered, the white flag fluttered weakly in the breeze, as if ashamed of itself. Nobody cheered. They stared at it, waiting for someone to call the bluff. But silence held. Silence always did.

What does war leave behind? Scars and scraps. A widow lighting a fire for her neighbor. A soldier ripping bread in half because giving up seemed too easy. Somewhere, on the edge of it all, morale soup simmered faintly—a pot of something fragile and fleeting, yet impossibly human.

War wasn’t just loud—it was loud and ridiculous. It was mud-drenched chaos with an air of mischief, like the universe was playing an elaborate joke no one understood. The farmer crouched low in the trench, gripping his musket like it might suddenly whisper reassurances. Around him, the world roared: cannons booming as if competing for attention, men shouting orders that were vague at best, and faintly—persistently—the sound of clinking metal.

“Surely not,” he muttered, his voice cracking under the weight of disbelief. He craned his neck toward the sound, squinting through mud, smoke, and pure exhaustion. Just down the line sat a soldier, stirring a tin pot with such focus it seemed like the fate of the world hinged on its contents. Steam rose lazily, unimpressed by the noise surrounding it.

“Morale soup,” the soldier explained solemnly, catching the farmer’s bewildered stare. He lifted the pot like it was a prized trophy. “Mostly water. Sometimes salt. Hope floats on occasion.”

“Soup?” The farmer blinked, momentarily too tired to process.

“Would you care for a spoon?” the soldier offered with unflappable seriousness. No one dared question where the spoon had come from.

Before the farmer could respond, the officer’s whistle tore through the air—a sound so sharp it felt like a personal insult. Everyone was scrambling to their feet—or attempting to—boots sucked deep into mud as if the earth was staging an intervention. The farmer fired his musket, though he was pretty sure nothing would come of it. Across the field, a British soldier crouched awkwardly, his hat crooked like he’d dressed in the dark.

For a brief, surreal moment, the farmer thought, Does he hate this mud, too? Does he wish he could abandon the musket and open a bakery? Does he even know his hat looks like a wilted cabbage?

The whistle screeched again, pulling him back to reality. “Reload!” someone shouted, their tone practically apologetic. The farmer sighed and complied. Some things didn’t require philosophy—just exhaustion.

Elsewhere, perched high in the twisted branches of a tree that seemed deeply unimpressed by mankind, a young sharpshooter tried to find logic in his task. Below him, an officer paced, barking about proper formations and flanking maneuvers as though either were relevant. His coat was aggressively red, his buttons gleaming like tiny golden insults.

The sharpshooter raised his rifle, aimed, and paused. What if he’s got kids waiting for him? What if he secretly loves gardening—flowers, not vegetables? What if he only yells because he’s drowning in doubt?

“Alright, God,” the boy whispered, his voice trembling. “If I miss, it’s your way of saying stop. If I don’t… well, guess that’s sorted.”

The rifle cracked. The officer fell with a flourish—a collapse so theatrical it felt rehearsed. Below, squirrels scattered, unimpressed by humanity’s ongoing moral dilemmas. The sharpshooter slumped back against the tree trunk, muttering something about absurdity and acorns.

Valley Forge wasn’t just cold—it was surreal. Snow piled high enough to swallow the cabins, which leaned against one another like conspirators. Inside, soldiers huddled around sputtering fires, their feet wrapped in burlap that barely qualified as fabric. It was unclear whether anyone was warmer or simply pretending to be.

One man coughed until he fainted. His friend dragged him toward the fire, muttering, “If you die, you’ll give us ideas. Bad ones.”

Another soldier stared at a crumpled letter that looked ready to disintegrate. He whispered to it like a prayer: “It’s all falling apart.” But whether he meant the war or himself was unclear.

Across the room, a man tore his last scrap of bread in half and shoved it into the hands of the whisperer. “Eat it,” he said bluntly.

The recipient stared at the bread as though it might explain itself. “Why?”

The bread-giver shrugged. “Because I tried bribing the snow with it and got nowhere. It’s your turn.”

The march to haul cannons from Ticonderoga started with quiet dignity and ended in chaos. Snow drifts rose higher than ambition, the wind screamed like it was offended, and somewhere in the storm, a soldier pointed toward the horizon.

“There,” he whispered, his eyes wide. “The British general. A ghost. Laughing at us.”

No one laughed, but they didn’t argue either. Behind them, another soldier burst into hysterical laughter, sharp and unhinged.

“Cannons? We’re dragging cannons? Across THIS? So we can blow up WHAT? Somebody pinch me; this has gone too far.”

Knox sighed deeply but trudged onward. When one man stumbled and fell, too weary to rise, another soldier stepped forward, picked up the rope, and kept hauling. Knox paused briefly, watching, and thought: The cannons don’t matter. It’s us. We’re dragging each other through this mess.

Yorktown didn’t feel like an ending—it felt like a question nobody wanted to answer. The trenches stank of sweat, failure, and something metallic that refused to name itself. Washington walked the line, stopping beside a soldier slumped in the mud next to his fallen friend.

“If you fall, we all fall,” Washington said with practiced seriousness.

The soldier sighed, his voice flat. “We’ve already fallen, sir.”

Washington didn’t argue. He pulled the man upright without ceremony, because standing sometimes felt like the only defiance left.

When Cornwallis surrendered, the white flag fluttered weakly in the breeze, like it regretted its involvement. Nobody cheered. They stared at it, waiting for someone to yell, “Just kidding!”

But silence won. Silence always did.

What does war leave behind? Scraps. Fragments. A widow lighting her neighbor’s fire. A soldier sharing crumbs because giving up felt too tedious. Somewhere in the distance, morale soup bubbled faintly—a pot of something fragile, existential, and oddly kind.

The smell of war was unforgettable—a mixture of damp regret, sour mud, and something that could only be described as "the death of optimism." The farmer crouched low in the trench, gripping his musket like a lifeline, though he doubted it had any actual plans to save him. Around him, the world was a cacophony: cannons shouting like frustrated giants, soldiers yelling things no one seemed to understand, and faintly—persistently—came the clink-clink-clink of a tin pot.

He craned his neck toward the noise, squinting through the smoke and haze. Just down the line, a soldier sat cross-legged in the mud, calmly stirring a pot perched over a flame so tiny it looked like it was giving up mid-burn.

“Morale soup,” the soldier said, catching the farmer’s bewildered stare. He lifted the pot with the dignity of someone holding a trophy. “Mostly water. Occasionally despair. Once, there was a beetroot, but it deserted us.”

The farmer blinked. “Soup?”

“Would you like a spoon?” the soldier asked cheerfully, as though that was the missing piece to the entire war effort. It was unclear where he’d been hiding a spoon, but he looked serious.

Before the farmer could respond, the officer’s whistle tore through the air, sharp enough to sever all coherent thought. Everyone stumbled upward—or tried to—their boots sucked deep into the mud like they were being politely but firmly detained by the earth itself. The farmer fired his musket without aim or conviction, feeling more like a man protesting circumstances than an actual combatant. Across the field, a British soldier crouched awkwardly, his hat tilted at an angle that might have been fashionable if not for the bloodshed.

For a moment, the farmer wondered: Does he hate this mud, too? Does he dream of hammocks and cider and quiet days spent not holding a gun? Does he know his hat’s on backward?

But before the universe could answer, the whistle screeched again, yanking him back to reality. “Reload!” someone bellowed, their tone somewhere between exasperation and existential despair. The farmer sighed and complied. What else could he do?

High in the branches of a tree that seemed wholly unimpressed by the war beneath it, a sharpshooter whispered his doubts to the air. Below him, an officer paced back and forth, shouting about proper formations and dignity as if those things still existed. His red coat shone obnoxiously in the sunlight, his buttons gleaming with an air of unnecessary self-importance.
