We're All Counting On Something

Words / 2025 Variety

A Compassionate Guide About Togetherness

---

This methodology introduces an entirely new physics of character creation: "suspension dynamics," where every marginalized character exists in a perpetual state of dangling—neither grounded nor flying but occupying the space between, turning that suspended state into its own form of movement. I call it the dangling methodology, a way of writing characters suspended between gravity and flight, because that's where most of us actually live. I discovered this principle at 4:17 AM when I watched a spider fail to complete its web between a wheelchair spoke and a door frame, starting over seventeen times, and realized this was the most honest metaphor for marginalized existence I'd ever witnessed. Sometimes I think I invented this whole theory just to justify why I've been hanging in the same place for ten years, afraid that landing means admitting this is my life now. No one has theorized character development as a form of aerial mathematics before, where the distance between harness and ground becomes the exact measure of authentic representation—though sometimes I wonder if we measure this distance just to know how far we have to fall.

The original framework I call "jacket consciousness"—where identity operates not as clothing metaphor but as empty garments that achieve sentience, whispering to each other about the bodies they used to contain. I keep calling it "jacket consciousness" like it's revolutionary, but really I'm just terrified of the navy blue blazer in my closet from my last job interview, where they said I was "inspiring" instead of "qualified." Last Tuesday, I swear I saw two official jackets switch hooks when no one was looking, as if they were choosing their next victims. These official jackets aren't symbols but autonomous entities that hunt for bodies to fill them, creating a horror-comedy of institutional diversity where characters must actively dodge the jackets trying to wear them. Sometimes the jackets leave Post-it notes: "Worn by inspirational amputee, 2019-2021, seeking new body for diversity photoshoot." The saddest notes are the ones that say "Never worn, still waiting." This reverses every existing paradigm: instead of people performing identity, identities perform people-hunting.

The "Failed Operations Principle" stands completely alone in narrative theory—where character development happens not through growth or change but through accumulating spectacular failures that become their own form of expertise. Consider Marcus, who has failed the same tactical mission forty-three times, each failure adding a new scar to his collection, which he's named like pets: "This one's Clarence, from the time I got stuck in the air vent." He doesn't tell people that Clarence is his favorite, the one he touches when he can't sleep. Each botched tactical mission, each trust fall that becomes a dance battle, each sporting event that devolves into bureaucratic performance art—these aren't obstacles to overcome but credentials being earned. One character in my workshop literally keeps a failure résumé, updating it weekly with new disasters, using Comic Sans font because "even my documentation should disappoint." She cried the first time someone took it seriously enough to laugh. Characters become heroes not by succeeding but by failing with such consistency that failure itself becomes mastery.

I'm introducing "Antagonistic Accommodation Theory"—where every institutional attempt at inclusion becomes a form of combat that characters must survive through malicious compliance. The one-armed police supervisor doesn't overcome her disability or work despite it; she weaponizes the institution's awkward attempts at accommodation, turning every badly designed assistance into evidence of systemic absurdity. She once spent six hours using the "accessible" automatic door system to trap her supervisor in an infinite loop of opening and closing, claiming she was "just following the accommodation protocols to their logical conclusion." Later, alone in her office, she stared at her employee ID photo from before the accident and couldn't remember if that person would have found this funny or just sad. This isn't resistance or acceptance but a third option: institutional jujitsu. Yesterday I couldn't get out of bed because someone called my work "brave." This methodology is supposed to protect against that. It doesn't. I just lie there, dangling horizontally.

The "Flirtation Wheelchair Dynamics" creates an entirely new narrative physics where mobility devices become instruments of seduction, not through inspiration porn's "seeing past disability" but through the active eroticization of adaptive equipment. One character installed underglow lights on his chair and plays "Careless Whisper" through hidden speakers during every approach. The wheelchair becomes a vehicle for attraction precisely because it disrupts expected narratives, creating a new framework of desire that has never been theorized in disability representation. Another character practices what she calls "strategic wheelchair choreography," having mapped the exact angle (47 degrees) at which her chair position makes everyone lean in closer to hear her. She doesn't tell them that she calculated this angle during the six months when no one would look at her directly, measuring the geometry of avoidance until she could weaponize it into intimacy.

"Comedy as Prejudice Exorcism" revolutionizes how we write marginalized humor. The comedian doesn't use jokes to deflect discrimination or educate audiences but to perform a kind of possession ritual, making prejudiced attitudes speak against themselves until they become gibberish. The comedian with his notebook of who laughs with versus at him? He's my brother. I created an entire theory about prejudice exorcism because I couldn't bear to tell him I see him counting, that I know why he sits in his car so long after shows. He once made an entire audience repeat a discriminatory phrase so many times it devolved into baby talk, then had them realize they were essentially speaking in tongues at a diversity seminar. Each routine is an exorcism where bias is forced to reveal its own absurdity through perfect mimicry that breaks down into nonsense. His signature move involves teaching audiences to laugh in languages that don't exist yet.

The "Trans Crime Novel Principle" establishes that marginalized characters must have passions completely unrelated to their marginalization, but these passions must be pursued with such abrasive competence that identity becomes background noise to personality. The Trans Crime Novel Principle sounds empowering until you meet Chen at 2 AM, deleting another manuscript because someone at a reading asked if the murder victim represents her deadname. She threw her laptop that night. I helped her pick up the keys. She writes crime novels not as metaphor for transition but because murder mysteries are more interesting than gender discourse, and anyone who tries to make it meaningful gets eviscerated by her superior plot analysis. She keeps a tally board of how many times people try to find trans metaphors in her work about Victorian poisonings—current count: 847. Her apartment contains no pride flags but does feature an extensive collection of vintage murder weapons, all legally obtained and labeled with their literary appearances. Sometimes she holds the Victorian letter opener and wonders if it's sharp enough to cut the invisible threads that tie every conversation back to her body.

"Gravitational Indifference" is my term for how these characters relate to inspiration—not through rejection or embrace but through a physics that makes inspiration impossible. Like objects in space, they exert their own gravitational fields that bend meaning around them, making it impossible for others to extract uplifting narratives because the narrative space itself has been warped. One character literally measures the "inspiration attempts per hour" rate in social situations, keeping a small clicker in her pocket that sounds like a cricket when pressed. The saddest sound, she says, is when the clicking stops—not because people stopped trying to be inspired by her, but because she stopped counting, which is its own kind of surrender.

The "Spectacular Mundane Method" requires each character to have one utterly boring trait that they pursue with earth-shattering intensity. The blind poet doesn't write about blindness or sight but about bathroom tiles, creating epic verses about grout patterns with such passion that disability becomes irrelevant noise compared to her ceramic obsessions. She's memorized the acoustic properties of 247 different tile types and can identify any bathroom by the sound of a single footstep. Her most celebrated poem, "Ode to Subway Station Hexagons," made three architecture critics cry. She refuses to discuss anything else at parties, derailing every conversation toward tile-related topics with the determination of a zealot. What she never mentions is that she started studying tiles the day after her vision went, needing something solid under her feet to believe in when everything else became darkness and guesswork.

"Palindromic Identity Syndrome" isn't about reversible words but about characters who exist the same way forward and backward in time—their future selves and past selves are identical, creating a temporal loop that makes character development impossible and therefore honest. They don't grow; they accumulate, like sediment, becoming more themselves through repetition rather than change. One character has worn the same outfit for fifteen years, not out of poverty but from a philosophical commitment to temporal consistency. Another greets everyone with "See you yesterday" and has never been corrected. There's something heartbreaking about refusing to believe in change when change is all anyone ever wanted from you. The truth? Sometimes I don't want to be suspended or grounded or flying. Sometimes I just want to sit in a chair without it meaning anything, to exist without being a metaphor for existence.

The "Dwarfism as Tactical Advantage in Love and War Theorem" posits that societal prejudices about height create exploitable blind spots in both combat and romance. The character keeps a detailed journal of "height-based assumptions to exploit," currently at volume twelve. The character doesn't overcome these prejudices but maps them like a terrain, using others' discomfort as camouflage for spectacular infiltrations of both buildings and hearts. His business card reads "Your Blind Spot, Personified" and includes a diagram of typical sight lines he can navigate undetected. Volume one of his journal, which he never shows anyone, is just drawings of all the tall people he loved who never thought to look down.

"The Harness Point System" creates a new metric where character authenticity is measured by how long they can dangle without seeking either ground or flight. I'm writing this with a cramp in my left shoulder from demonstrating the "mid-air rotation" yesterday. My physical therapist says I need to stop using my body as a metaphor. I told her the metaphor is using me. Points are awarded for mid-air rotations, for tangling other characters in your wires, for using suspension as a position from which to critique both ceiling and floor. The current record holder dangled for seventeen hours while delivering a dissertation on institutional failures, hydrating through a complicated straw system. She didn't mention that she stayed up there because coming down meant admitting the institution had won. The methodology requires tracking each character's HSP (Harness Suspension Points) throughout the narrative. Bonus points are awarded for dangling while eating soup (50 points), filing taxes (100 points), or conducting a romance (250 points, 500 if it's reciprocated while both parties dangle). No points are awarded for crying while suspended—that's just called Tuesday.

Finally, the "Institutional Slapstick Imperative" demands that every serious moment of inclusion must devolve into physical comedy, not to undermine progress but to reveal that progress itself is slapstick when performed by institutions that don't understand the assignment. The mandatory diversity ice-breaker where everyone had to share their "journey" ended with someone literally slipping on actual ice someone had brought to make the metaphor literal, creating a pile-up of wheelchairs, crutches, and well-meaning facilitators that resembled a medical supply store explosion. Characters navigate this comedy not as victims but as acrobats, turning every pratfall into performance art. One character now teaches a workshop called "Advanced Pratfalling for Social Justice" and charges institutions $500 per person to fall correctly. She donates the money to a fund for people who fell and couldn't get back up, which is somehow both a joke and not a joke at all.

This methodology has no precedent because it treats character creation as aerial mathematics, identity as empty clothing with agency, failure as expertise, and suspension as the only honest position for marginalized existence. It's not experimental—it's documentary, capturing the exact physics of dangling between who you are and who systems need you to be, then turning that suspension into its own form of flight. The spider from 4:17 AM finally completed its web on the eighteenth try, only to abandon it immediately for a better location, which might be the most honest ending this methodology could have. Sometimes I visit that empty web, still stretched between the wheelchair spoke and the door frame, catching nothing but light and serving no purpose except to prove that something once tried very hard to exist there. Sometimes, when the methodology fails its creator, I wonder if I'm the spider or the web.
