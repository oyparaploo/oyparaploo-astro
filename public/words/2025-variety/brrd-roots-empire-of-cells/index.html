Prison Imperialism and the Rise of Mass Incarceration

The United States, a nation often heralded as a beacon of freedom, paradoxically holds the highest incarceration rate in the world. Its sprawling prison-industrial complex, a system that thrives on punitive justice and systemic inequality, has not only shaped domestic policy but has also seeped into the global fabric. This phenomenon, aptly termed “prison imperialism,” reveals how America’s model of mass incarceration has been exported to other nations, reshaping their justice systems and deepening societal fractures.

The roots of prison imperialism can be traced back to the early 2000s, when the U.S. began actively collaborating with countries like Colombia to restructure their prison systems. Under the guise of combating drug trafficking and organized crime, the United States signed agreements to fund and design new prisons, train guards, and implement data management systems. These initiatives were part of broader efforts tied to the War on Drugs—a campaign that had already wreaked havoc domestically by disproportionately targeting marginalized communities.

In Colombia, the U.S.-backed Program for the Improvement of the Colombian Prison System marked the beginning of a new era. The construction of facilities like La Tramacúa Penitentiary in Valledupar showcased the American model: overcrowded cells, militarized security, and harsh punitive measures. The prison population surged, and human rights abuses became rampant. Political arrests increased, and prisoners were often transferred far from their families, severing vital support systems. What was sold as a solution to crime became a mechanism for control, mirroring the dynamics of America’s own prison-industrial complex.

But Colombia was just the beginning. The United States extended its influence to countries like Honduras and Mexico, exporting not only physical infrastructure but also the ethos of mass incarceration. These programs often came with strings attached, aligning recipient nations with American geopolitical interests. The prisons became tools for managing dissent and enforcing neoliberal policies, ensuring that social disruption and political resistance were met with force and intimidation.

The consequences of prison imperialism are stark. In many countries, incarceration rates have risen dramatically, mirroring the trajectory of the United States. Overcrowding, neglect, and inhumane conditions have become common features of these systems. The militarization of prisons and the use of isolation as punishment further dehumanize inmates, stripping them of dignity and hope. Families are torn apart, and communities are left to grapple with the fallout of mass incarceration.

Yet, the export of America’s prison model is not merely a matter of policy—it is a reflection of deeper systemic issues. The prison-industrial complex thrives on inequality, exploiting marginalized populations for profit. By exporting this model, the United States has effectively globalized its approach to justice, spreading the same inequities that plague its own system.

Resistance to prison imperialism has emerged in various forms. Human rights organizations and grassroots movements have called for an end to these programs, advocating for restorative justice and community-based solutions. In Colombia, groups like Lazos de Dignidad have worked tirelessly to expose the abuses within U.S.-funded prisons and support political prisoners. These efforts highlight the need for a paradigm shift—away from punitive measures and toward systems that prioritize rehabilitation and social equity.

America’s prison imperialism serves as a stark reminder of the interconnectedness of global systems. It reveals how policies rooted in inequality can transcend borders, shaping the lives of people far removed from their origins. As the world grapples with the consequences of mass incarceration, the question remains: can we dismantle the empire of cells and build a justice system that truly serves humanity?

Shadows of Conquest: The Hidden History of America’s Wars Against Native Americans

The mid-nineteenth century was a time of expansion, ambition, and bloodshed in the United States. Manifest Destiny—the belief that Americans were divinely ordained to spread across the continent—was not just a rallying cry; it was a justification for conquest. Behind the rhetoric of progress and civilization lay a darker truth: the systematic subjugation of Native American tribes, whose lands and lives were sacrificed to the relentless march of empire. This hidden history, often overshadowed by tales of pioneers and railroads, reveals the brutal reality of America’s wars against Indigenous peoples.

The wars to subjugate Native Americans were not isolated conflicts; they were part of a broader strategy to consolidate power and claim territory. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 had already set the stage, forcing tribes like the Cherokee, Creek, and Seminole to leave their ancestral lands and embark on the harrowing Trail of Tears. But by the mid-nineteenth century, the focus shifted westward, where vast expanses of land promised wealth and opportunity for settlers—and resistance from the tribes who called it home.

The Dakota War of 1862, also known as the Sioux Uprising, exemplifies the tensions of this era. The Dakota Sioux, pushed to the brink by broken treaties and starvation, rose up against the settlers who had encroached on their lands. The conflict was swift and brutal, culminating in the largest mass execution in U.S. history: 38 Dakota men were hanged in Mankato, Minnesota, under orders from President Abraham Lincoln. The message was clear: resistance would be met with overwhelming force.

Similarly, the Sand Creek Massacre of 1864 exposed the depths of cruelty in America’s campaign against Native Americans. A peaceful village of Cheyenne and Arapaho, led by Chief Black Kettle, was attacked by Colorado militia under Colonel John Chivington. Despite flying a white flag of surrender, the villagers were slaughtered—men, women, and children alike. The massacre was condemned by some but largely ignored by a nation focused on its own Civil War.

These wars were not just battles; they were campaigns of erasure. Native American cultures, languages, and traditions were systematically suppressed. Boarding schools, established in the late nineteenth century, sought to “kill the Indian, save the man” by forcing children to abandon their heritage. Lands were seized, reservations established, and autonomy stripped away. The scars of these policies remain visible today, etched into the fabric of Native American communities.

Yet, amidst the darkness, there were moments of resistance and resilience. Leaders like Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, and Geronimo fought valiantly to protect their people and their lands. The Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876, where Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors defeated General George Custer’s forces, stands as a testament to their courage. These victories, though fleeting, symbolized the enduring spirit of Native American resistance.

The hidden history of America’s wars against Native Americans is not just a story of conquest; it is a story of survival. Despite the efforts to erase them, Indigenous peoples have persevered, preserving their cultures and reclaiming their identities. Today, movements for sovereignty, land rights, and cultural revitalization continue to challenge the legacy of these wars.

As we reflect on this history, we must confront the uncomfortable truths it reveals. The expansion of the United States was built on the dispossession and suffering of Native Americans. Their stories, often relegated to the margins, deserve to be heard and honored. Only by acknowledging this past can we begin to understand the complexities of America’s identity—and the resilience of those who have endured its darkest chapters.

Frontiers of Empire: The Colonial Conquest of Western Territories

The colonial conquest of Western territories is a story of ambition, displacement, and transformation—a tale of how vast landscapes were reshaped by the forces of empire. It is a narrative that spans centuries, weaving together the dreams of settlers, the resistance of Indigenous peoples, and the relentless march of expansion. Behind the romanticized imagery of pioneers and wagon trains lies a complex and often brutal history, one that reveals the true cost of conquest.

The story begins with the doctrine of Manifest Destiny, a belief that Americans were divinely ordained to expand across the continent. This ideology, rooted in notions of racial superiority and economic ambition, provided the moral justification for the westward push. The Louisiana Purchase of 1803 and the annexation of Texas in 1845 set the stage, opening vast swaths of land for settlement. But these territories were not empty; they were home to diverse Indigenous nations, each with their own cultures, histories, and claims to the land.

The conquest of the West was marked by a series of violent conflicts, as settlers and soldiers clashed with Native American tribes. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 initiated a wave of forced migrations, most notably the Trail of Tears, which saw the Cherokee and other tribes uprooted from their ancestral lands and relocated to reservations. This policy of displacement was driven by the desire to clear land for agriculture and settlement, but it came at a devastating human cost.

As settlers moved westward, they encountered resistance from tribes determined to defend their homelands. The Dakota War of 1862, the Sand Creek Massacre of 1864, and the Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876 are just a few examples of the bloody confrontations that defined this era. These conflicts were not merely battles; they were campaigns of erasure, as Native American cultures, languages, and traditions were systematically suppressed.

The construction of railroads further accelerated the conquest, linking the East and West and facilitating the movement of goods, people, and ideas. Towns sprang up along the tracks, transforming the landscape and creating new economic opportunities. But the railroads also brought environmental destruction, as forests were cleared, rivers dammed, and wildlife hunted to near extinction. The buffalo, a symbol of the plains and a vital resource for many tribes, was driven to the brink, its disappearance emblematic of the broader ecological impact of expansion.

The conquest of Western territories was not solely a story of settlers and soldiers; it was also a tale of governance and control. The establishment of territorial governments and the imposition of laws sought to bring order to the frontier, but these systems often marginalized Indigenous peoples and excluded them from decision-making. The reservation system, designed to confine tribes to specific areas, stripped them of autonomy and subjected them to the authority of federal agents.

Yet, amidst the violence and displacement, there were moments of resilience and adaptation. Native American leaders like Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, and Geronimo fought valiantly to protect their people and their lands. Tribes found ways to preserve their cultures, passing down traditions and stories that continue to thrive today. The West itself became a symbol of opportunity and reinvention, attracting immigrants from around the world who sought to carve out new lives in its vast expanses.

The colonial conquest of Western territories is a story of contradictions—of progress and loss, of creation and destruction. It is a history that shaped the United States, defining its borders and its identity. But it is also a history that demands reflection, as the legacy of conquest continues to reverberate through the lives of Indigenous peoples and the landscapes they call home.

As we look back on this chapter of history, we must confront the complexities it reveals. The West was not simply won; it was taken, reshaped, and reimagined. Its story is one of resilience and resistance, a testament to the enduring spirit of those who fought to protect their lands and their ways of life. And in understanding this history, we find the seeds of a more inclusive and equitable future—one that honors the past while striving for justice.

Empire Forged in the Tropics: The Creation of an American Empire in Panama, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines

The dawn of the 20th century marked a turning point in American history. The United States, once a fledgling republic wary of entangling alliances, had transformed into a burgeoning global power. The Spanish-American War of 1898 was the spark that ignited this transformation, catapulting the United States into the ranks of imperial nations. Through its acquisitions of Panama, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines, the United States forged an empire that would reshape the geopolitical landscape and leave a lasting imprint on the lives of millions.

The story begins with the Spanish-American War, a conflict ostensibly fought to liberate Cuba from Spanish rule. But beneath the rhetoric of freedom and democracy lay a deeper ambition: the expansion of American influence. The war ended with the Treaty of Paris in 1898, which ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States. Cuba, though nominally independent, became a de facto American protectorate under the Platt Amendment. These acquisitions marked the birth of an American empire, one that would stretch across the Caribbean and the Pacific.

Puerto Rico: The Gateway to the Caribbean

Puerto Rico, a lush island in the Caribbean, became a key foothold for the United States. Strategically located, it served as a gateway to Latin America and a vital link in the defense of the Panama Canal. The Foraker Act of 1900 established a civilian government on the island, but ultimate authority rested with the U.S. Congress. Puerto Ricans were granted U.S. citizenship in 1917, yet they remained second-class citizens, unable to vote in presidential elections or elect voting representatives to Congress.

The American presence brought modernization and infrastructure development, but it also disrupted traditional ways of life. The sugar industry, dominated by American corporations, became the backbone of the economy, displacing small farmers and creating widespread inequality. Resistance simmered beneath the surface, culminating in the rise of nationalist movements that sought to reclaim Puerto Rico’s identity and autonomy.

The Philippines: A Distant Frontier

Halfway around the world, the Philippines presented a different challenge. The archipelago, with its thousands of islands and diverse cultures, was seen as both a strategic asset and a burden. President William McKinley famously claimed that the United States had a divine duty to “civilize” the Filipino people, a justification rooted in the paternalistic attitudes of the time.

But the Filipinos had their own vision of independence. Led by Emilio Aguinaldo, they had fought against Spanish rule and now turned their resistance against the Americans. The Philippine-American War (1899–1902) was a brutal conflict, marked by guerrilla warfare, atrocities, and the deaths of hundreds of thousands of Filipinos. The United States ultimately prevailed, establishing a colonial government and implementing reforms in education, health, and infrastructure. Yet, these efforts could not erase the scars of war or the resentment of a people denied their sovereignty.

Panama: The Canal and the Crown Jewel

In Panama, the United States pursued a different kind of empire-building. The construction of the Panama Canal was a monumental engineering feat, one that would connect the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans and solidify America’s dominance in global trade. But the canal’s creation came at a steep cost.

The United States supported Panama’s independence from Colombia in 1903, securing the rights to build and control the canal in perpetuity. The Panama Canal Zone became a de facto American colony, governed by U.S. officials and patrolled by American troops. The canal itself was a marvel of modern engineering, but its construction relied on the labor of thousands of workers, many of whom endured grueling conditions and disease.

For Panamanians, the canal was both a source of pride and a symbol of subjugation. The American presence loomed large, and the benefits of the canal were often felt more in Washington than in Panama City. It wasn’t until the 20th century’s latter half that Panamanians would reclaim control of the canal, a testament to their enduring struggle for sovereignty.

The Legacy of Empire

The creation of an American empire in Panama, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines was driven by strategic interests, economic ambitions, and a belief in the nation’s exceptionalism. But it also revealed the contradictions at the heart of American identity. The United States, a nation founded on principles of liberty and self-determination, became a colonial power, imposing its will on others in the name of progress.

The legacies of this empire are still felt today. Puerto Rico remains a U.S. territory, grappling with questions of identity and statehood. The Philippines gained independence in 1946, but its history of colonization continues to shape its politics and society. Panama reclaimed its canal in 1999, a victory that underscored the resilience of its people.

As we reflect on this history, we are reminded of the complexities of power and the enduring impact of empire. The stories of Panama, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines are not just chapters in American history; they are testaments to the resilience of those who lived under its shadow and the ongoing struggle for justice and self-determination.

Empire of Chains: America’s Prison Imperialism and the DNA of Mass Incarceration

The story of America’s prison system is not merely one of bars and cells; it is a tale of power, control, and the deliberate use of incarceration to subjugate populations deemed “restive” or “undesirable.” From the wars to subjugate Native Americans in the mid-nineteenth century to the creation of an American empire in Panama, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines, the use of prisons as tools of domination has been written into the nation’s DNA. This legacy has evolved into the modern era of mass incarceration, where the United States leads the world in imprisoning its own citizens.

The roots of America’s prison imperialism can be traced back to the mid-1800s, when the United States expanded westward under the banner of Manifest Destiny. Prisons were used to control Native American populations, who resisted the seizure of their lands and the destruction of their cultures. Fort Marion in Florida, for example, became a site of forced assimilation, where Native Americans were imprisoned and subjected to “civilizing” programs designed to erase their identities. The prison was not just a place of punishment; it was a tool for cultural genocide.

This pattern of using prisons to control subject populations extended beyond the continental United States. In the aftermath of the Spanish-American War, the United States acquired territories like Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Panama. In these regions, prisons became instruments of colonial governance, used to suppress dissent and enforce American policies. The construction of penitentiaries, the training of guards, and the implementation of surveillance systems mirrored the practices developed domestically. The prison system was exported as a model of control, aligning with broader imperial ambitions.

The Philippines, in particular, became a laboratory for America’s prison imperialism. Following the Philippine-American War, the United States established a colonial government that relied heavily on incarceration to maintain order. Prisons were used to detain political dissidents, suppress uprisings, and enforce labor policies. The Carcel de Bilibid, built during this period, became a symbol of American dominance, its walls holding not just prisoners but the weight of colonial oppression.

As the United States solidified its global influence in the 20th century, the logic of prison imperialism seeped into its domestic policies. The rise of the prison-industrial complex in the latter half of the century reflected the same principles of control and subjugation that had been applied abroad. Mass incarceration became a tool for managing “restive” populations within the United States, disproportionately targeting Black and brown communities. The War on Drugs, launched in the 1980s, served as a pretext for the expansion of the prison system, mirroring the rhetoric of “civilizing” and “pacifying” used in earlier imperial campaigns.

The consequences of this legacy are staggering. Today, the United States incarcerates more people than any other nation, with over two million individuals behind bars. The prison system disproportionately impacts marginalized communities, perpetuating cycles of poverty, disenfranchisement, and systemic inequality. Prisons have become sites of dehumanization, where individuals are stripped of their dignity and subjected to conditions that often violate basic human rights.

Yet, the story of America’s prison imperialism is not solely one of oppression; it is also a story of resistance. From the Seminole Chief Osceola, who defied his captors at Fort Marion, to modern activists like Assata Shakur, who challenge the carceral state, there is a rich history of opposition to the logic of incarceration. These acts of resistance remind us that the chains of empire are not unbreakable.

As we confront the realities of mass incarceration, we must reckon with the history that brought us here. America’s prison system is not an anomaly; it is the product of centuries of deliberate policy choices rooted in imperial ambition and systemic inequality. To envision a future without mass incarceration, we must first understand the empire of chains that binds us—and the resilience of those who have fought to break free.

Chains of Defiance: Prison Resistance from Seminole Chief Osceola to Assata Shakur

The history of American prisons is not just a story of confinement—it is also a story of resistance. From the 19th-century wars against Native Americans to the modern struggles for racial justice, prisons have been wielded as tools of control, designed to suppress dissent and subjugate marginalized populations. Yet, within these walls, acts of defiance have flourished, challenging the systems that sought to silence them. The stories of Seminole Chief Osceola and Assata Shakur, separated by more than a century, illuminate the enduring spirit of resistance against the carceral state.

Osceola: Defiance in Chains

In the early 19th century, the United States waged a brutal campaign to remove Native American tribes from their ancestral lands. The Seminole people of Florida, led by figures like Osceola, resisted fiercely. Osceola, a charismatic and strategic leader, became a symbol of defiance against the U.S. government’s efforts to force the Seminoles onto reservations.

Captured under a flag of truce in 1837, Osceola was imprisoned at Fort Marion in St. Augustine, Florida. The fort, with its cold stone walls and iron-barred cells, was a stark symbol of the U.S. government’s power. Yet, even in captivity, Osceola refused to submit. He wore his traditional clothing and maintained his dignity, becoming a source of inspiration for his people. His resistance was not just physical but symbolic—a refusal to let imprisonment strip him of his identity.

Osceola’s death in 1838, while still imprisoned, marked the end of his personal struggle but not the end of Seminole resistance. His legacy lived on, a testament to the resilience of a people who refused to be erased. Fort Marion itself would later become a site of forced assimilation for other Native Americans, but Osceola’s story remained a reminder of the power of defiance.

Assata Shakur: A Modern Freedom Fighter

More than a century later, another figure would emerge to challenge the carceral state. Assata Shakur, a member of the Black Panther Party and the Black Liberation Army, became a symbol of resistance during the civil rights and Black Power movements of the 1960s and 1970s. Her activism, rooted in the fight against systemic racism and police brutality, made her a target of government surveillance and repression.

In 1973, Shakur was arrested after a shootout on the New Jersey Turnpike that left a state trooper dead. Convicted of murder in a highly controversial trial, she was sentenced to life in prison. But Shakur’s imprisonment did not silence her. She continued to write, organize, and inspire others, using her words to expose the injustices of the prison system.

In 1979, Shakur escaped from prison with the help of allies and fled to Cuba, where she was granted political asylum. Her autobiography, Assata: An Autobiography, became a rallying cry for activists around the world, highlighting the intersections of race, gender, and incarceration. Shakur’s story, like Osceola’s, is one of resistance—a refusal to accept the legitimacy of a system built on oppression.

The Thread of Resistance

The stories of Osceola and Shakur are separated by time, geography, and context, yet they are bound by a common thread: the use of prisons as tools of control and the defiance of those who refuse to be broken by them. Both figures represent broader struggles—the fight of the Seminoles to preserve their land and culture, and the fight of Black Americans to achieve racial justice and equality.

Their acts of resistance challenge the narrative that prisons are places of submission. Instead, they reveal the potential for resilience and rebellion, even in the face of overwhelming power. Osceola’s dignity in captivity and Shakur’s escape to freedom are reminders that the human spirit cannot be confined by walls and bars.

A Legacy of Defiance

The legacy of prison resistance continues to resonate today. From hunger strikes in detention centers to movements for prison abolition, the fight against the carceral state remains a central part of struggles for justice. The stories of Osceola and Shakur remind us that resistance is not futile—it is transformative. Their defiance, born in the darkest of circumstances, continues to inspire those who seek to dismantle systems of oppression and build a more equitable world.

As we reflect on their stories, we are reminded that prisons are not just places of confinement—they are battlegrounds for freedom. And in these battlegrounds, the spirit of resistance endures, lighting the way for future generations.

Breaking the Chains: One Historian’s Quest to Reimagine Prisons and Justice

Benjamin Weber sat in his study, surrounded by stacks of books, maps, and archival documents. The room was dimly lit, the soft glow of a desk lamp illuminating the pages of history he had spent years piecing together. For Weber, the story of prisons was not just a tale of confinement—it was a narrative of power, control, and resistance that spanned centuries and continents. His mission was clear: to tell the full story of incarceration, from its roots in America’s imperial ambitions to its global impact, and to envision a future where prisons no longer existed.

Uncovering the Hidden History

Weber’s journey began with a question: How did the United States, a nation founded on principles of liberty, become the world’s leading incarcerator? The answer, he discovered, lay in the country’s history of expansion and empire. From the wars to subjugate Native Americans in the mid-nineteenth century to the creation of an American empire in Panama, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines, prisons had been used as tools of control, designed to suppress dissent and manage “restive” populations.

In his groundbreaking book, American Purgatory, Weber traced the evolution of America’s prison system, revealing how it was exported to other nations as part of a broader strategy of imperialism. He uncovered the deliberate use of prisons to enforce colonial policies, suppress resistance, and maintain social order. The construction of penitentiaries, the training of guards, and the implementation of surveillance systems mirrored the practices developed domestically. Weber’s research highlighted the global geography of “prison imperialism,” showing how America’s approach to incarceration had shaped justice systems around the world.

The Voices of Resistance

But Weber’s work was not just about documenting oppression—it was about amplifying the voices of those who had resisted it. From the Seminole Chief Osceola, who defied his captors at Fort Marion, to Assata Shakur, who escaped from prison and exposed the injustices of the carceral state, Weber uncovered a rich history of prison resistance. These stories, often relegated to the margins, revealed the resilience of individuals who refused to be broken by the systems designed to suppress them.

Weber’s research also highlighted the role of grassroots movements in challenging mass incarceration. He documented the efforts of activists, scholars, and communities who had fought to dismantle the prison-industrial complex and advocate for restorative justice. Their work, he argued, was a testament to the enduring struggle for freedom and equity.

A Vision for the Future

As Weber delved deeper into the history of prisons, he began to imagine a world without them. His vision was bold and transformative: a justice system that prioritized rehabilitation over punishment, community-based solutions over confinement, and equity over control. He envisioned a society where resources were invested in education, healthcare, and social services, addressing the root causes of crime rather than perpetuating cycles of incarceration.

Weber’s vision was not utopian—it was grounded in the lessons of history and the successes of restorative justice programs around the world. He pointed to examples like Norway’s prison system, which emphasized dignity and reintegration, and community-based initiatives in the United States that had reduced recidivism rates. These models, he argued, demonstrated that a world without prisons was not only possible but necessary.

Benjamin Weber’s work has left an indelible mark on the field of history and the fight for justice. His book, American Purgatory, is more than a chronicle of incarceration—it is a call to action, inviting readers to rethink the scope of America’s long freedom struggle and envision a more equitable future. Through his research, Weber has illuminated the connections between past and present, showing how the legacy of prison imperialism continues to shape our world.

As we reflect on Weber’s journey, we are reminded that history is not just a record of what has been—it is a guide to what can be. The story of prisons and incarceration is one of oppression and resistance, but it is also a story of possibility. By understanding the past, we can begin to imagine a future where justice is not defined by chains and cells but by dignity and humanity.
