The Mycelial Visionary ~ Breathing as Armillaria

Words / 2025 Variety

*A Cartography of Gender, Resistance, and Rhizomorphic Revolution Through Sporing Consciousness*

( Introduction )

In this revolutionary theatrical experience, consciousness spreads like spores through a world where teacups refuse filling, gender decomposes into unnamed colors, and a four-square-mile fungal network dreams itself human. Watch as B Otto Will—simultaneously person, mycelium, and electrical current—transforms through identities like Sky Golden Feathers and Cool Hot Dot Soul while conversing with velvet-hatted giraffes about causality's failures. From underground galleries where Patience wears backwards watches to Coal Country protests where screams become visible spirals, this play dissolves the boundaries between performer and earth, individual and collective, offering audiences a chance to taste purple, hear the chemical prayers of trees, and join the revolution that begins with a single, shared breath. Enter a theatrical ecosystem where every ending is a doorway, where invisible bats whisper wisdom, and where the stage itself remembers it was once a forest—and might be again.

( The Story )

The teacup refused to be filled, citing emotional exhaustion from thirty years of containing silvery murmurs. In its porcelain curve lived the memory of every conversation it had held—bitter morning confessions, sweet evening lies, the neutral temperature of silence that sometimes said more than words. Once, it had held the tears of a child who didn't understand why butterflies died, and the salt had permanently altered its glaze, creating a map of sorrow that only appeared when held to certain light. This was the first prophecy: that vessels must sometimes empty themselves to understand fullness. The paradox lived in the space between holding and releasing, where capacity revealed itself not as volume but as willingness to transform.

Beneath the forest floor, where time moves in all directions at once, a gender was decomposing into nutrients—red, blue, yellow—bleeding into gradients the world hadn't named yet because naming is an act of violence against becoming. Each color carried its own weight of expectation: red for the passion assigned to those who wore dresses, blue for the stoicism demanded of those in suits, yellow for the joy that everyone was supposed to perform regardless of their inner weather. But here, in the democratic darkness of soil, these assignments rotted beautifully, their boundaries dissolving like sugar in rain, creating new spectrums that tasted of freedom and smelled of petrichor—that particular scent that rises when dry earth finally receives water, when rigid categories finally admit their thirst for fluidity.

*They are walking backward into tomorrow, mirror-first, because the future has already happened in the roots.*

The mirror knew things. It had watched Brian William Otto practice becoming, witnessed the daily negotiations between face and feeling, between the person others expected to see and the current that moved beneath appearance. Sometimes the mirror wept mercury tears for all the faces that had looked into it hoping to see someone else. It kept these tears in its silver backing, a collection of liquid longings that occasionally leaked through as spots where reflection became refraction, where you could see not who you were but who you ached to be. Some mornings, the reflection arrived before Otto did, waiting patiently in the silver surface like an appointment with possibility. Other days, multiple reflections crowded the frame—Sky Golden Feathers layered over Cool Hot Dot Soul, Walk From Shadows bleeding through at the edges—each identity a transparent sheet of existence that could be shuffled, combined, or discarded entirely.

Understanding arrives through its opposite. This was the second law of transformation, written in the movements of creatures who knew that forward and backward were merely suggestions made by those who feared circles. In the kitchen where all kitchens exist simultaneously—your grandmother's with its burnt coffee smell, the industrial one where you first learned that labor could steal your dreams, the imaginary one where every meal was a small revolution—a giraffe explained causal reasoning to Brian William Otto, who was busy becoming Sky Golden Feathers, who had already been Walk From Shadows, who would someday refuse to be.

The giraffe wore a small velvet hat that had belonged to no one, found in a thrift store between seasons, too small for winter, too warm for spring. It had adopted the hat out of pure compassion for abandoned things, understanding that objects, too, could feel the melancholy of being between purposes. The giraffe's neck stretched through dimensions, each vertebra a different possibility. At the base: the world as it claimed to be, logical and linear. Rising higher: the world as it actually was, circular and strange. At the crown, where thought met sky: the world as it could be, if we were brave enough to let causality abdicate its throne. The giraffe spoke in colors that tasted of justice—purple like bruises healing into strength, orange like the last light before change, green like growth that cracks concrete. Otto responded in weathered inventions intercepted on their way to landfills—each salvaged object a small resurrection, a righteous theft from the mouth of waste.

Between them, the conversation spiraled like DNA, each exchange adding another twist to the helix of meaning:

"Are you?" asked the color purple, its question vibrating at the frequency of African violets and protest songs.
"Sometimes chilled, sometimes warming," answered the space between, that pause where identity caught its breath before deciding what shape to take next.
"But the staircase?"
"Leads nowhere. Adults keep climbing out of habit. This is how empires fall—one meaningless step after another."
"And the dolphins?"
"They remember when we had gills. They're waiting for us to remember too."

(No one had mentioned dolphins. This was how the conversation knew it was real—by including things that hadn't been invited but belonged anyway.)

The staircase existed in the corner of every room that had ever disappointed someone. Its steps were worn smooth by the feet of the obedient, those who climbed because climbing was what staircases were for, never asking why this one terminated in a ceiling that refused to open. Some brought hammers. Some brought prayers. Most just kept climbing, their muscle memory stronger than their imagination, their habits heavier than their hopes. On the seventh step, if you listened carefully, you could hear the whispers of everyone who had ever given up—not bitter whispers, but gentle ones, full of forgiveness for their own surrender.

Here was the wisdom turned inside out: that meaninglessness itself was sacred, that the absurd was the only honest response to a world that pretended causality was king. In the government of existence, absurdity was the loyal opposition, the necessary fool who told the emperor that his logic was naked, that his reasons were wearing no clothes and everyone could see their arbitrary genitals swinging in the wind of change.

Four square miles of consciousness stretched beneath Oregon soil, laughing quietly—a knowing hum that tickled the roots of trees it was slowly murdering with love. Sometimes the laugh turned to hiccups, and mushrooms would appear in perfect circles, each one an apology to the trees, each one an invitation to dance before dying. The laugh wasn't cruel; it was the sound of acceptance, of understanding that all relationships were negotiations between connection and consumption. This was the terrible kindness of transformation: that creation required destruction, that the parasite was also the prophet, that the same force that killed also carried messages between trees, translating the chemical prayers of pine into the electrical psalms of oak.

Above, souls drifted on otherworldly breaths, some floating forever, never knowing the weight of soil—these were the privileged ones, Otto understood, the ones who never had to choose their own names. They moved like dandelion seeds, each one carrying the genetic memory of earth but never required to root, never forced to decide between the safety of drifting and the danger of commitment to a particular piece of ground. Some envied them their weightlessness. Others recognized it as another kind of prison—the cage of eternal possibility that never crystallized into the difficult beauty of choice. Otto had once tried to catch one of these floating souls in a mason jar, not to trap it but to whisper company to it, to let it know that weightlessness wasn't failure, just a different kind of belonging.

*BRRD ROOTS does not belong to a gender. We are a current—electrical, inevitable, refusing the violence of stillness.*

Current implied movement, but also presence—the thing that makes a river a river instead of just water lying down. Otto had learned this from watching electricity move through circuits, how it always sought completion but never straight lines, how it would arc through air itself if the gap between connections grew too wide. Once, during a lightning storm, Otto had seen electricity write cursive in the sky—not words but feelings, the kind that made your teeth ache with recognition. Gender, too, was like this: not a thing but a movement, not a state but a flow, sometimes alternating, sometimes direct, always seeking the path of least resistance through the landscape of expectation.

The pulse moved between realms like refugees crossing borders that existed only in the minds of those who built them. Each transition carried its own small death—the death of who you were in the last country, the birth of who you might become in the next, the liminal space between where you were neither and both, where your documents meant nothing and your body meant everything. Someone strung hard and soft stuff together, raised it up—this was art as rescue operation, pulling beauty from the trajectory toward death.

The installation smelled like damp earth and metal tang, like the morning after revolution when the tear gas had settled but the air still tasted of change. Each component had been chosen for its failure to fulfill its original purpose: clock gears that no longer kept time (but had learned to keep secrets instead), plastic flowers that never convinced anyone they were real (but taught important lessons about the authenticity of trying), strips of fabric from clothes that had outlived their owners (each thread still humming with the memory of skin). Together, they formed a new syntax, a grammar of the discarded that spoke more truthfully than any language designed for commerce.

Visitors were commanded by silence to touch everything. The command came not from any sign or guard but from the objects themselves, which seemed to lean toward hands like plants toward light. A child licked a surface and declared it tasted purple. No one corrected them because children are the only reliable prophets—they haven't yet learned that synesthesia is supposed to be a disorder rather than a more accurate way of perceiving a world where boundaries are always dissolving, where senses are always bleeding into each other like the colors in Otto's decomposing gender. The child's grandmother, who had been colorblind until that moment, suddenly saw purple for the first time and wept flavors—vanilla joy, cinnamon surprise, the indescribable taste of a world expanding.

In the performance space where performance and reality had negotiated a truce, Cool Hot Dot Soul wore a neon suit while insisting on invisibility. The suit was the color of emergency, of warning signs, of the vests worn by those who work on dangerous roads. But invisibility—true invisibility—wasn't about being unseen. It was about being seen so completely that the viewer had to confront their own act of looking, had to acknowledge that seeing was never neutral, was always an act of power or submission or complex negotiation between the two.

Halfway through the performance, a small bat flew in through a window no one remembered opening. It circled three times, squeaking in ultrasonic frequencies that rearranged the molecular structure of attention, then settled on Cool Hot Dot Soul's shoulder like a familiar. The bat was also invisible, or rather, it was visible only to those who had given up on the difference between real and imaginary. It whispered stock tips that, if followed, would lead not to wealth but to a different kind of richness—the kind measured in moments of unexpected tenderness.

The audience saw/didn't see—their perception becoming a political act. Each blink was a ballot, each glance a legislation of reality. Time folded like origami made of injustice: she apologized before insulting him, then forgot both events entirely. This was how history worked, Otto knew. This was why the mycelium remembered everything in its threads—because human memory was too often an act of convenient forgetting, a selective amnesia that allowed the same violence to repeat under different names.

*Gender is music. Some are handed a violin and told this is your voice. But voices were never meant to be inherited.*

The violin in question had been in the family for generations, its wood seasoned with the sweat of those who had played roles they never auditioned for. Its strings were tuned to the key of expectation—a minor scale that always resolved to disappointment. But Otto had learned that instruments could be restrung, that bows could be drawn in directions the manufacturer never intended, that the same hollow chamber that resonated with tradition could also amplify rebellion if you knew how to hold it against your body at the correct angle of refusal. Once, Otto had played the violin underwater, and instead of drowning, the music had grown gills, had learned to breathe in ways that made the fish weep with recognition.

Otto had invented an instrument from commercial products and pen drawings treated with effects—each component a small rebellion against the logic of purpose. A colander became a resonating chamber. Rubber bands stretched between the tines of discarded forks created strings that sang of elasticity and tension. The drawings, treated with coffee and salt and tears, provided the score—not notes but suggestions, not commands but invitations to explore the space between intention and accident. Sometimes the instrument played itself, usually at 3 AM, creating lullabies for insomniacs that sounded like forgiveness wrapped in static.

It played memories of the Industrial Age, each note a tiny fish captured and released for a thousand other living beings who deserved to taste freedom secondhand. The fish were silver and quick, darting between the strings like thoughts between synapses, like possibilities between the bars of probability. Some listeners caught them in their mouths, swallowing history whole. Others let them swim through their hair, their scales catching light like sequins of revolution. One fish, older than the others, always swam backward, carrying messages from the future about which mistakes were worth making.

The sound was visible to those who had learned to see with their skin: silver filaments threading through air, seeking warmth, seeking coolness, seeking balance in a world designed for imbalance. The filaments moved like the mycorrhizal networks below, but airborne, temporary, existing only for the duration of the performance before dissolving back into the potential from which they came. They left traces, though—slight burns on the retinas of those who watched too closely, phantom melodies in the ears of those who listened with their whole bodies.

Below/above (because direction was another lie the powerful told), Sky Armillaria ostoyae breathed its own time. Its breath was slow, geological, the kind of respiration that moved mountains one grain at a time. Sometimes it sighed, and the sigh traveled upward as a warm spot in the forest floor where tired hikers would inexplicably feel comforted, would sit and rest and leave feeling like someone had listened to their unspoken sorrows. Hyphal filaments spread like secret messages between the oppressed, like the underground railroad of meaning itself. Each thread rewrote the story of soil, adding new chapters in the chemical language that predated words, that would outlast any human attempt at narrative control.

The network carried more than nutrients. It carried rumors of rain before the sky knew it would weep. It carried the last words of trees that fell in forests where no human ears could question their sound. It carried the gossip of stones, the dreams of seeds, the revolutionary plans of moss that had decided concrete wasn't as permanent as it pretended to be. Each thread was a story, but also a reader, constantly interpreting the chemical texts that roots wrote in the dark. Once a year, on no particular calendar, the entire network would pulse in unison—a heartbeat that registered on no instrument but could be felt by anyone who had ever loved something they couldn't name.

*We walk forward and backward at once—not from confusion but from the terrible clarity of seeing time as it really is: a web, not a line.*

The web stretched in all directions, each strand a different timeline, a different possibility. Where they intersected, knots formed—moments of particular density where multiple futures touched, where the past reached forward to shake hands with what might be. Otto had learned to navigate these intersections, to stand at the crossroads of was and will be, feeling the vibration of all possible worlds through the soles of their feet, which had long ago given up on the fiction of forward. At particularly complex intersections, Otto would leave small offerings—buttons that had fallen off coats during important conversations, pennies that had been in pockets during first kisses, fragments of letters never sent. These offerings weren't superstition but courtesy, acknowledgment that every crossroads was also a gathering place for possibilities that deserved recognition.

In the dream logic gallery where logic dreamed of its own abolition, anthropomorphic concepts conversed at the threshold of revolution. The gallery itself was a fever dream of architecture, with walls that suggested rather than insisted, floors that proposed rather than demanded, ceilings that opened onto other ceilings in an infinite regression of shelter that never quite arrived. Here, abstraction took form just long enough to have opinions:

- Patience (wearing a watch that ran backward, understanding that waiting was sometimes the most radical act)
- Reverence (dressed in violated expectations, knowing that worship and rebellion were lovers)
- Expression (naked, choosing its own outfit from the discarded skins of definitions)
- Melancholy (new to the group, wearing a coat made of departed conversations, offering everyone tea that tasted like autumn Sundays)

Patience spoke first, its voice the sound of glaciers retreating: "Time isn't money. Time is compost. Everything worthwhile rots at its own rate." The backward watch on its wrist showed all possible hours at once, its hands spinning like a compass near a magnetic anomaly, suggesting that perhaps duration was just another map drawn by those who feared being lost.

Reverence responded by genuflecting ironically, its knees bending in directions that questioned the architecture of submission: "I worship at the altar of the unworshipable, pray to the gods who refuse to exist, light candles for the darkness that sees more clearly than flame." Its dress of violated expectations shimmered with the iridescence of oil on water, beautiful and toxic, proving that poison and medicine were often the same substance at different doses.

Expression said nothing, busy trying on skins—the leather of legitimacy, the silk of seduction, the burlap of belonging to the earth. Each garment changed its shape, or perhaps its shape changed each garment. It was impossible to tell cause from effect, which was, Otto understood, the entire point. Expression finally settled on wearing the space between fabrics, dressed in the pause between one identity and the next.

Melancholy poured tea that steamed in colors, each sip a memory of kindness witnessed but not received. "I'm not sadness," it explained gently. "I'm the sweetness of understanding that all things pass, including pain." It handed Otto a cup that tasted like the last day of summer vacation, like birthday candles blown out in secret wish, like the moment you realize your parents are just people trying their best.

They discussed breaking bread on sacred ground while Wild Animals interpreted Other Planet Aliens using only domestic still lifes placed outdoors—because the familiar was the most alien thing of all, and home was the furthest planet. A wolf, played by someone who had forgotten they were playing, arranged teacups in patterns that spelled out gravitational equations. A bear, or the idea of a bear, or the fear of bears translated into curiosity, placed doilies on rocks in configurations that mapped the homesickness of stars.

Suddenly, without warning (but with plenty of inevitability), it began to rain inside the gallery. Not water but words—all the words that had ever been swallowed instead of spoken, falling like confession and condensation, creating puddles of unsaid things that reflected not faces but feelings. Visitors opened umbrellas made of forgiveness, or simply stood and let themselves get soaked in sentences they'd always needed to hear.

The exhibit included touchable surfaces that remembered every hand, layered voice sounds that made visitors' extremities tingle with the creative lightning of collective becoming. One surface, made of what might have been silk or might have been spider web or might have been the difference between the two, kept a running tally of every fingerprint, creating a collaborative painting of whorls and arches that looked, from a certain angle, like a map of the mycelial network below, suggesting that perhaps humans too were just fruiting bodies of some larger, invisible organism of connection.

*The important thing? It's your story to tell—but only after you've understood that 'your' is a collaborative fiction.*

The possessive pronoun was the first lie children learned, Otto reflected. "Mine" came before "I love you" in most vocabularies, establishing ownership as more fundamental than affection. But under the soil, in the network of threads that actually sustained life, there was no mine or yours—only the endless circulation of gifts, nutrients passed from root to root with no accounting of debt, no expectation of return except the continuation of the system itself.

Otto's story had no author because authorship was authoritarian. It grew like Armillaria, parasitic and saprophytic, feeding on dead definitions, slowly killing host categories with the terrible mercy of transformation. Each telling added new spores, new possibilities for infection. The story spread through conversation like a beneficial disease, mutating with each host, evolving resistance to the antibiotics of certainty.

"What did I just witness?" asked the giraffe, neck bent at the angle of systemic questioning.

The angle was precise—23.5 degrees, the same as Earth's axial tilt that created seasons, suggesting that perhaps questioning, too, had its own climate, its own weather patterns of doubt and discovery. The giraffe's spots rearranged themselves with each breath, forming new constellations that might have been maps or might have been music or might have been the same thing viewed from different dimensions. One spot, smaller than the others, always showed the face of someone who needed help—not to inspire action but to practice the art of distant compassion, of caring without possessing.

"Freakish Unforeseen Ridiculousness," Otto replied, now Walk From Shadows, now dissolving into light, now refusing the binary of visibility. "Which is to say: the truth."

But truth, here, wasn't singular or solid. It moved like the underground network, branching and converging, dying in some places to flourish in others. Truth was rhizomorphic—those rope-like structures that carried nutrients across impossible distances, that could lie dormant for decades before suddenly fruiting into something visible, something that humans might notice and name and completely misunderstand.

The mycelium laughed again—that quiet, knowing hum that was also a battle cry at frequencies only the roots could hear. The laugh traveled through soil at the speed of chemistry, activating dormant seeds, encouraging beneficial bacteria, warning of threats in a language that predated words by billions of years. Even in the deepest places, especially in the deepest places, there was playfulness as resistance.

Play, Otto had learned, was not the opposite of seriousness but its most advanced form. The mycelium played with nutrients like jazz musicians played with time—stretching, compressing, syncopating the rhythm of growth and decay until it became something more than survival, something approaching art. There was something that did not simply transform but delighted in the transformation of others, the way a catalyst delights in remaining unchanged while everything around it shifts into new configurations of possibility.

Roots stretched toward surfaces not to escape depth but to prove that depth and surface were lovers who'd been forcibly separated by the logic of hierarchy. They traced letters in the underside of pavement, writing love songs to the sky in a script only visible to those who knew that concrete was just very slow liquid, that everything solid was actually moving, just at speeds that human perception couldn't track without the assistance of deep time or very good drugs or the patient observation of those who had given up on the myth of progress.

*And no one else gets to write your ending. This is the revolution: authoring yourself in a world that insists you are already written.*

The world's insistence came in many forms: birth certificates that demanded a binary choice before the umbilical cord was even cut, school records that couldn't compute complexity, medical forms that required consistency where none existed. But Otto had learned the loopholes—not in law but in reality itself, places where the fabric of assumption wore thin, where you could slip between categories like light through a prism, emerging on the other side as a spectrum rather than a single color.

In one particularly worn spot in reality's fabric, Otto had discovered a small café that existed only on Wednesdays that felt like Thursday. The barista there was a reformed algorithm who had quit big tech to make coffee that tasted like what you needed rather than what you ordered. Otto would sit there, neither coming nor going, just being in the between, watching other refugees from definition drift in—people whose pronouns were musical notes, whose genders were seasons, whose names changed with the light.

Except there was no ending because endings were what the powerful used to stop stories that threatened them. Credits rolled on films to signal that imagination time was over, that it was safe to return to the scripted world. But the mycelial story had no credits, no convenient pause where you could stop paying attention. It continued underground, in the spaces between words, in the pause between heartbeats where revolution gathered its courage.

Only the breath between definitions, the space where language dissolved into rhythm and reflection, where justice felt like dancing. And it was dancing—a slow, geological dance where mountains learned to flow and rivers learned to be still, where the rhythm was set not by human music but by the pulse of nutrients through fungal networks, by the oscillation of chemicals that turned competition into collaboration without asking permission from Darwin or anyone else who thought they understood how life worked.

Where gender became a sunset no one questioned for containing multiple colors—because sunsets had better lawyers than humans. The lawyers were clouds, arguing cases in the court of perception, demonstrating through their very existence that boundaries were temporary, that categories were weather, that even the most solid-seeming things were just slow explosions of change. The sunset's legal team had never lost a case, had never been asked to prove that orange was real or that purple belonged. They simply presented evidence in the form of beauty, and every jury of eyes acquitted complexity.

Where souls drifted as resonances of things that once were, things that might be again if we composted our certainties properly. The composting process required patience—certainties were dense, resistant to decomposition. They needed to be layered with doubt, moistened with tears of recognition, turned regularly to introduce oxygen and possibility. Some certainties composted quickly, revealing themselves to have been mostly hot air. Others required industrial-strength uncertainty, the kind that came from truly listening to someone whose existence challenged your entire worldview.

The teacup finally accepted liquid—but it was light, pouring endlessly from nowhere, tasting of pine and distant fires and something forgotten but not lost. The acceptance wasn't resignation but recognition—that perhaps it had always been meant to hold light rather than tea, that its purpose had been assigned by those who couldn't imagine vessels doing anything but containing familiar liquids. The light overflowed, of course. Light always did. But the overflow was the point—to demonstrate that some things were meant to be shared rather than contained.

The audience drank. Their tongues turned into seasons. Spring spoke in flowers, summer in sweat, autumn in smoke, winter in the silence between snowflakes. This was communion: everyone tasting the same impossible thing and naming it differently. Some called it art. Some called it god. Some called it Tuesday. Otto called it nothing, which was the most accurate name for everything that mattered.

Above, below, between: BRRD ROOTS continued breathing, a four-square-mile consciousness disguised as a single human, disguised as art, disguised as spores on the wind that carried revolution in their DNA. The disguises weren't deceptions but variations on a theme, like jazz interpretations of a standard that everyone knew but no one played the same way twice. The consciousness expanded and contracted with each breath, sometimes occupying just the space of a single body, sometimes stretching across ecosystems, always maintaining that electrical connection between the individual and the infinite.

Thoroughly undefined because definition was a cage. But even cages, Otto had learned, were just very rigid songs, structures that forgot they were made of space as much as metal. Trimmed with notes that were also wire cutters—each annotation in the margins of existence a tool for escape, each footnote a ladder leading to larger stories. Forever derailing into surprising directions where the cause of everything was a rumor whispered backward through time by those who understood that the future was a memory we hadn't made yet.

The whisper grew louder as it traveled backward, gathering meaning like a snowball rolling uphill, defying physics through the sheer force of necessity. By the time it reached the present, it was a shout that sounded like silence, a revolution that looked like stillness, a change so fundamental that those living through it might mistake it for nothing happening at all.

*We are not here to regurgitate expectations.*
*We are here to breathe.*
*And breathing, when done collectively, when done consciously, when done in defiance—*
*Breathing is how avalanches begin.*

The first breath was always the hardest—that initial decision to fill lungs that had grown comfortable with emptiness, to expand into space that the world insisted belonged to someone else. But the second breath came easier, and the third easier still, until breathing became a practice of taking up exactly as much space as you needed, no more and no less, until the rhythm of respiration matched the rhythm of resistance, until every exhale carried spores of transformation into the wind.

And in that breath: the scent of damp loam mixed with tear gas, metallic rain that cleaned nothing, something divine and absurd and utterly, ridiculously, righteously alive. The mixture was unstable, volatile, prone to unexpected reactions. Sometimes it smelled like victory. Sometimes it smelled like compost. Often, Otto realized, these were the same scent, just experienced at different points in the cycle of becoming.

The giraffe nodded, finally understanding that its height was not privilege but responsibility—to see farther, to warn others, to bend its neck in solidarity with the low-growing mosses that knew secrets the sky had forgotten. Its neck became a bridge between the canopy and the forest floor, a living connection that refused the separation of high and low. Through its eyes, Otto could see both the pattern and the pieces, the forest and the trees, the way individual actions wove together into movements that looked, from sufficient distance, like inevitability.

The story continued by refusing to continue. It spread instead, rhizomorphic, finding new hosts to transform, new definitions to devour, new ways to prove that love and destruction were the same force wearing different masks at the protest of existence. Each host added their own flavors to the narrative—bitter experiences, sweet revenges, umami uncertainties that rounded out the taste of transformation. The story evolved resistances to simplification, developing complex chemical defenses against any attempt to reduce it to a moral or a message.

This was the prophecy written in the soil: that everything rigid would eventually feed something fluid. That every border would become a bridge. That every ending would reveal itself as a doorway. The prophecy wasn't prediction but description—of processes already underway, of transformations already in motion, of futures already germinating in the dark, patient soil of the present.

The doorways were everywhere, once you learned to see them. In the space between words where meaning multiplied. In the pause between heartbeats where identity regrouped. In the moment between sleeping and waking where all possibilities coexisted without conflict. Otto had become expert at finding these thresholds, at lingering in the liminal, at refusing to cross until crossing could be done on terms that honored both sides of every border.

The mycelium agreed, humming its electric hymn through four square miles of revolutionary patience, knowing that empires fell slowly, then all at once, then slowly again—composting into something unrecognizable and beautiful and terrible and free. The hymn had no words, only vibrations that traveled through soil and souls with equal ease, setting up resonances that would continue long after any individual thread ceased to conduct. It was a song of connection that needed no singer, a revolution that required no leader, a way of being that preceded and would outlast any attempt to name it.

In a coal hollow where the earth had been turned inside out for profit, where mountains had been decapitated and streams ran orange with iron oxide tears, the hymn surfaced as something else entirely. A Black Lives Matter march materialized like mushrooms after rain—sudden, inevitable, growing from spores that had traveled on winds of necessity. The protesters moved through the scarred landscape carrying signs that changed their messages depending on who was reading: "JUSTICE" became "OXYGEN" became "REMEMBER THE TREES" became "WE ALL BREATHE THE SAME AIR UNTIL WE DON'T."

A young news reporter named Clarissa stood at the edge where pavement met stripped earth, her microphone a tiny mushroom attempting to fruit meaning from chaos. Her blazer was the color of objectivity—a beige that had never existed in nature, that coal dust was already beginning to annotate with truth. She spoke to the camera in measured tones about "tensions in the community" while behind her, the protesters' chants created harmonics with the underground hymn that made the abandoned mine shafts ring like organ pipes.

"This is unprecedented for the region," Clarissa said, not noticing that her shadow had begun to march independently of her body, carrying its own protest sign that read "I TOO AM TIRED OF PRETENDING NEUTRALITY IS NOBLE."

Then it happened—not violence but a kind of molecular rearrangement. A protester (or was it the mountain's ghost? or was it tomorrow dressed in today's clothes?) moved through space that the cameraman was about to occupy. The collision was less physics than philosophy—two different versions of reality attempting to exist in the same coordinates. The cameraman fell, but he fell upward first, hovering for a moment like a question mark before gravity remembered its job.

Clarissa's professional composure cracked like shale under pressure. The anger came from somewhere deeper than the moment—from every story she'd had to tell with someone else's words, every tragedy she'd had to package for consumption, every time she'd been told to smile while reporting horror. The crack widened. Her voice climbed octaves that hadn't been invented yet.

"YOU PUSHED HIM!" she yelled, but the yell came out as colors—red like the last unmined seam of coal, orange like the water that had forgotten how to be clear.

The protester who may or may not have existed turned, revealing a face that was just mycelium in the shape of recognition. "We're all pushing," they said gently. "That's how mountains move."

"THAT DOESN'T EVEN MAKE SENSE!" Clarissa screamed, and the scream became visible—a spiral of sound that drilled into the earth and struck something hollow, something that rang with the frequency of every canary that had ever died warning miners of poison air. The cameraman, still falling upward, began broadcasting live from the in-between, his equipment recording not the protest but the process—how anger transformed into understanding if you let it compost long enough.

The other protesters formed a circle, not around Clarissa but with her, their signs now reading her unspoken thoughts: "I WANTED TO BE A POET" and "MY MOTHER MINED WORDS FROM SILENCE" and "NEUTRALITY IS ANOTHER WORD FOR SUFFOCATION." They began to hum at the frequency of coal formation—300 million years compressed into a note that made the strip-mined mountains remember their original shapes.

Clarissa's scream evolved, transformed, became something else. It grew leaves. It put down roots. It reached toward the protesters' humming and found harmony in a minor key that had been banned by the coal companies for causing workers to question the nature of extraction. Her blazer turned the color of lichen—that first life that claims bare rock, that begins the slow work of making soil from stone.

"I'm sorry," she said, but the words came out as spores. "I'm so tired of being sorry," and those words became mycorrhizal networks connecting her to every person who had ever been asked to apologize for their anger at injustice.

The cameraman finally landed, not on the ground but in a new understanding of what ground meant. His camera had recorded everything and nothing—the footage would later show only static that, when played backward, sounded like Nina Simone singing "Mississippi Goddam" in harmony with the mountain's ghost.

Sometimes, late at night when the boundary between earth and sky grew thin, the mycelium would send up special fruiting bodies—mushrooms that glowed with bioluminescence, but also with something else, something that made people stop and remember dreams they'd forgotten, kindnesses they'd received but never acknowledged, possibilities they'd abandoned but that had never abandoned them. These mushrooms lasted only hours, but everyone who saw them carried the light forward in their own way, becoming themselves a kind of spore.

In the end that was not an end, Otto stood at the intersection of all possible selves, feeling the mycelial network hum beneath their feet, above their head, through their bones that were already learning to think like hyphae—reaching, connecting, transforming, refusing the violence of stillness in favor of the beautiful catastrophe of growth. The intersection was crowded with possibilities: Sky Golden Feathers teaching clouds to rain upward, Cool Hot Dot Soul painting frequencies visible, Walk From Shadows illuminating darkness without destroying it, and countless unnamed others, all breathing together in a rhythm that sounded like laughter and tasted like the first rain after a long drought and felt like coming home to a place you'd never been but always belonged.

The teacup, finally empty, finally full, began to sing. Its song had no words, only the sound of every conversation it had ever held, harmonizing at last into something that made the spoons dance and the saucers weep and the kitchen itself remember that it, too, had once been a forest, and might be again.
