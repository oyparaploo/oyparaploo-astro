Poem · We Garden · 8 minutes

Soil and Sky

Sir Loam Surrealist Symphony of Meandering Entropy

Les Jardins du Chaos et du Charme

(un poème)

In the heart of my Jardin Potager,

I kneel where the thyme grows thick,

Where basil whispers its secrets to the breeze,

And rosemary dreams in the warmth of the sun.

In my Jardin de Curé,

I brew healing with every leaf,

A touch of sage for wisdom,

A sprig of lavender for peace.

This is my table, my garden, my kitchen,

Where the soul and the soil entwine,

Where pots simmer like stories unfolding,

A sweet dénouement in a bowl of golden broth.

Oh, my Jardin Remarquable,

A feast for the eyes, a stage for the seasons,

Where sunlight threads through ivy-laced walls,

And history lingers in the scent of old roses.

Here, among the symmetry of my Jardin à la Française,

I craft beauty with measured steps,

I trim the edges of my pastries like my hedges,

Sharp lines softened by the rhythm of care.

But then—let us wander—

Into the wild, into the rolling breath of my Jardin Paysager,

Where lilies bow toward murmuring streams,

And geese chase winds through fields of golden wheat.

In my Jardin Ouvrier,

The hands that shape dough also shape the land,

Every beet pulled from the earth,

A promise of labor turned into delight.

In my Jardin Botanique,

I preserve more than petals—I preserve memory,

I save the past in jars of apricot preserves,

I keep the future in the seeds resting on my palm.

And oh, my Jardin Sauvage,

Where the kitchen door swings open,

And nature spills into the halls,

Where nettles lace my soup with wild rebellion.

In my Jardin Méditerranéen,

I bask in the éclatement of olives beneath my press,

Of lemons surrendering their golden juice,

Of wine pooling into glasses—deep, dark, endless.

Life, like a garden, must be démêlage,

Untangled thread by thread, like dough folded into layers,

Must be détricotage, unraveled when time calls,

Must be effilochage, pulled gently apart before reweaving.

In my kitchen, in my garden, in my world,

We do not fear what comes undone—

We let it se défaire, we let it s’effilocher,

And from the soil, from the pot, from the table,

We begin again.

A garden does not simply grow—it shifts, expanding and contracting with every breath of light. The soil does not hold memory—it trades secrets with the rain, learning the weight of each season before releasing it to the roots below. The gardener does not tend—she translates, pulling meaning from the hum of the earth, the tilt of a lavender stalk, the slow conversation between stones.

She moves through the Jardin Potager, where vegetables lean toward the warmth in slow devotion, where basil folds itself into whispered reflections. Nothing here is hurried—time gathers in the hollows of leaves, settling like dust on thyme. Somewhere nearby, mint runs wild, laughing at the boundaries imposed on the rest.

Her Jardin de Curé is not merely tended—it is studied, each herb a lesson, each flower a thought unraveling. Here, rosemary teaches resilience, fennel speaks of the past, and the damp earth beneath her knees presses against her palms like a pulse, steady, unchanging.

The precision of her Jardin à la Française is softened by movement—ivy climbing despite direction, shadows stretching beyond symmetry. Lines become suggestions, and in the quiet of dusk, the structure dissolves into something looser, something less held.

She steps into her Jardin Paysager, where reflection is a habit and walking is a kind of ritual. Paths lead nowhere in particular, only further into thought, deeper into the quiet hum of space made for lingering. Here, water does not rush—it considers, pooling where stone allows, whispering back to the roots in slow admission.

Her Jardin Botanique is an archive of possibility, a collection of patience and wild persistence. Some plants flourish under careful hands, others under careful neglect. Each leaf is an answer to a question not yet asked.

And in her Jardin Sauvage, nature writes in its own hand, untamed, unbothered. There is no sense of arrival, only continuous unfolding—a season shifting into the next, a quiet moment suspended between the curve of vine and the sharp tilt of grass.

Her Jardin Méditerranéen stretches itself wide beneath the weight of sun and stone. Lemons hang golden and heavy, olives gather in thick clusters. The light changes the language of the garden, softens edges, warms the silence until the evening hums with the slow rhythm of things fully formed, fully present.

A garden is not kept—it is understood, it is followed, it is allowed to shift without warning, change without reason, exist without expectation.

She breathes in the scent of thyme, of sun-warmed lavender, of soil damp with last night’s rain. She watches the wind move through the olive trees, listens to the quiet stretch of time folding into itself.

At the table, guests lean in, wine fills waiting glasses, and conversation turns like ivy toward light.

The garden is not merely grown—it is coaxed, bribed, and occasionally threatened into existence. The soil does not passively hold memory—it negotiates terms, deciding which secrets to bury deep and which ones to whisper to the roots. And the gardener, elegant and endlessly patient, does not merely cultivate—she converses, contemplates, and occasionally pleads with the stubborn whims of nature.

She does not labor over her Jardin Potager, no—she flirts with it, persuades the basil to behave, reasons with the rebellious tomatoes, and eventually resigns herself to the fact that the mint has staged a coup.

Ah, her Jardin de Curé, a sanctuary of wisdom, where medicinal herbs stretch toward the sky, where rosemary stands in solemn meditation. It is here she brews concoctions that heal, that soothe, that inspire—and it is also here that the neighbor’s goat sneaks in at dawn, devouring the sacred lavender in what can only be described as an act of theological defiance.

Her Jardin Remarquable is officially recognized—a marvel of design, a tribute to history—until a squadron of pigeons decides that it should also be recognized as a public restroom.

In the formal elegance of her Jardin à la Française, she weaves her mastery, pruning hedges into crisp perfection, shaping symmetry with the precision of a pastry chef crafting delicate mille-feuille. And yet—nature, mischievous and unrepentant, conspires with the wind, sending unruly vines to mock her structure, her meticulousness, her sense of order. She sighs. She prunes. She pours wine.

She steps into her Jardin Paysager, where everything flows—paths meander, ponds hum, lilies bow gracefully toward the water. She marvels at the serene beauty, the harmony, the balance—until her cat leaps into the koi pond, declaring war upon serenity itself.

Her Jardin Ouvrier is a testament to resilience, a place where hands meet earth, where labor is nourished, where the honest act of growing sustains the soul. But even here, there is anarchy, for the carrots refuse to commit to straight lines, and the beets have conspired with the neighbor’s chickens to disrupt the natural order of things.

Ah, the Jardin Botanique, where the rare, the delicate, the extraordinary are preserved—until her great-aunt visits and decides the ornamental orchid would look better trimmed into a “nice shape.”

Her Jardin Sauvage is a wild dream, where plants choose their own destiny, where nature writes its own narrative, where order is no longer law but suggestion. Some would call it overgrown—she calls it liberated. The nettles, defiant and thriving, thank her for this revolution.

And then, her Jardin Méditerranéen, bathed in golden light, infused with olives, with sun-drenched lavender, with vines that whisper promises of wine. She stands amidst lemons bursting with brightness, she presses olives beneath her palms, she listens to the breeze—until the mistral laughs and tosses her prized fig tree sideways, a gentle reminder that nature belongs to no one.

Life unravels, then weaves itself again—dénouement, the untangling, the unfolding. It frays at the edges (effilochage), pulls apart like old thread (détricotage), demands patient untangling (démêlage). Some things must se défaire, must s’effilocher, must fall into disarray before finding their shape anew.

She gathers her guests, she pours wine, she allows the garden to do as it pleases. A table, a meal, a conversation—this is hospitality. This is a garden. This is life.

The garden does not unravel—it weaves itself anew. The soil does not forget—it remembers in layers. The gardener does not abandon—it listens, waits, and welcomes.

She stands at the edge of the field, watching the earth pulse beneath her feet. It does not contract—it breathes, expanding with quiet certainty. The wind does not stagnate—it moves, carrying whispers of change, pressing against her skin like something that refuses to be ignored.

She kneels, pressing her hands into the dirt. It yields. It whispers. It remembers.

The garden is not a place—it is a promise, a space where everything holds, where nothing is lost. She walks, and her steps touch the ground. The soil expands beneath her, welcoming the distance, shaping the path. The blossoms flicker in and out of visibility, appearing when the wind moves. And the wind moves.

She reaches for a flower that does not yet exist. Her fingers close around possibility.

She does not undo—she creates. She does not abandon—she nurtures. She is not a witness to dissolution—she is a keeper of renewal. The soil does not devour—it transforms. It does not consume—it gives. It is not a void—it is a foundation.

She presses her hands deeper into the earth, searching for something that is waiting to emerge. The roots whisper—they connect. The garden does not vanish—it blooms.

They do not fade—they grow. They are not remnants—they are beginnings. She stands in the center of the field, surrounded by silence. The blossoms pulse, flickering in and out of existence, their petals forming before they touch the air.

She reaches for them, and they are already here.

The wind does not stagnate—it stirs. It does not refuse to speak—it sings. It does not reject change—it carries it forward. She listens, and there is everything to hear. The wind presses against her skin, light and moving, thick with something that belongs.

She breathes, and the air fills her lungs.

She kneels, pressing her hands into the earth. It yields. It whispers. It remembers.

She rises.

The garden does not demand attention—it endures.

The audience does not vanish. They do not sit in stillness.

They grow.
