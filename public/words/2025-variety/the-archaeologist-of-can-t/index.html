The Archaeologist of Can't

← Words / 2025 Variety

There's a particular kind of morning light that only comes after loss—thin, uncertain, asking nothing of you except that you witness it. I learned to paint in that light. Not because I could paint, but because I couldn't bear not to. My hands, still trembling from grief, mixed colors that had no names: the grey-green of absence, the ochre of almost-remembering, that terrible shade of blue that lives between goodbye and gone. The canvas became a map of everything I couldn't do, which, it turns out, was the only way to begin doing it.

Took me forty-three years to figure this out: we are all archaeologists of can't. We dig, not because we know what we're searching for, but because our hands need something to do with all this living. Every morning, we excavate ourselves from yesterday's failures, brush the dust off our impossible longings, and catalogue the broken pieces as if they might someday form a whole. We become capable only by attempting what we cannot yet do. Not tomorrow. Not when ready. Now, with our unready hearts, our unprepared hands, our stumbling tongues still learning the language of survival.

My grandmother, before the dementia took her words, used to make bread every Thursday. After she forgot how, she would still stand in the kitchen, her hands moving through empty air, kneading nothing into something. "I'm practicing," she'd say, and we'd let her, because who were we to say that her invisible bread wasn't feeding something essential in her? She was doing what she couldn't do, and somehow, in that space between capability and attempt, she was still teaching us about sustenance. Even now, years later, when I'm lost, I find myself kneading empty air. Practicing.

Grief makes archaeologists of us all. Suddenly, you're sifting through conversations for missed meanings, excavating voicemails—playing them at 2 AM with the phone pressed so hard against your ear it leaves a mark—just for the sound of a laugh. Digging through photo albums for evidence that yes, there was a time before this weight settled into your chest like sediment. You become someone who can't. Can't sleep, can't eat, can't imagine tomorrow. And yet, you do. Badly, messily, with tears that come at inappropriate times and laughter that surprises you with its sudden arrival. You do these things you cannot do, and slowly, impossibly, you get to them.

The daily practice becomes this: each morning, choose one impossible thing. Today, I will write a letter to my sadness. Tomorrow, I will have a conversation with my fear, let it stumble over its words, let it make no sense. Thursday, I will paint the sound of my mother's voice, even though I don't paint and sounds have no color. (They do, though. Hers was burnt sienna mixed with honey.) Friday, I will be the archaeologist who digs without knowing what I'm looking for, trusting that the searching itself is the finding.

When my son was three, he decided he could fly. Not metaphorically—literally. He would climb onto the couch, spread his arms, and jump. Each time he landed, he'd say, "Almost." Never "I can't," just "Almost." He did this for months. One day I asked him if he was disappointed. He looked at me like I'd asked if water was disappointed about being wet. "I'm practicing being brave," he said. "Flying is just what happens after."

God.

That child.

The medicine: treat your limitations as temporary weather, passing through. Yes, today you can't imagine being happy. Yes, today you can't remember what it felt like before the world cracked open. Yes, today you are stumbling through conversations, making messes, painting grief with all the wrong colors. But can't is not your permanent address. It's just where you're standing while you practice being brave.

Mix the disciplines that don't mix. Let your science be poetic, your poetry be archaeological. Write prescriptions for wonder. Excavate joy from Tuesday's dirt. Paint with the ashes of what you thought your life would be. Make bread from air if that's what your hands remember. Jump from the couch with your arms spread wide. The flying, or something equally impossible and necessary, comes after.

We don't wait for readiness because readiness is a myth told by people who've forgotten what it feels like to begin. The preparation isn't separate from the doing—it is the doing. Every stumble teaches balance. Every failed attempt at happiness is still movement toward light. Every day you survive what you cannot survive is proof that you already are.

The passages were never walls. Never even doors. Just the space between who we were yesterday and who we're becoming by doing what we cannot yet do. In that stupid, necessary space—that terrifying space where nothing makes sense—we practice being human. We practice until practice becomes presence. We attempt the impossible daily, gently, with great tenderness for our trying, until one morning we wake up and realize we've been flying all along. We just called it falling, then floating, then surviving, then living, then—

Almost.

And almost is everything.
