The smoke rises like a whisper unanswered, curling through the morning air with the patience of all forgotten prayers, and I think of the old archivist in her room, bent over recording equipment that glows with tiny red lights—each one a small beacon against the coming darkness. She's mapping the city's sounds before the dust storms arrive, catching birdsong and traffic hum, the particular way metal expands in heat, the last conversations of people who don't yet know they're saying goodbye. Everything will be buried in silt, she knows—every familiar echo, every routine noise we've stopped noticing—but still she works, her steady hands transcribing what remains, fingers moving across controls with the muscle memory of decades. This is determination, perhaps, or something more primal: the human need to say *this existed, this mattered*, continuing when the ash clogs the atmosphere like cotton in the lungs, when even breathing becomes an act of will, each inhalation a small rebellion against the erasure of everything we've known.

I drive east, watching the speedometer hover at exactly sixty-five, ahead of the megafire wall that fills my rearview mirror like a tidal wave frozen in time, and remember my grandmother's stories of sandstorms in the old country—how the sky would turn yellow as old paper, how the air would taste of earth and endings. She would stuff cloth under the doors with the methodical care of someone preparing for siege, cover the windows with dampened sheets that dried and cracked like skin, then settle in to wait with the particular patience of those who understand that some forces cannot be outrun, only endured. The ritual of it, the practiced movements passed down through generations of women who knew how to read the wind. Now the climate reporter beside me films everything, her phone held steady despite the car's subtle vibrations, turning disaster into data with each captured frame, trauma into contemporary art that will trend for exactly forty-eight hours before the next catastrophe takes its place. But I understand her compulsion, the way her fingers clutch the device like a lifeline—we each find our own way to bear witness, to insist against all evidence that someone, somewhere, will remember this moment, will know we were here when the world changed.

The insulin shortages worry me in ways I can't quite articulate, a dread that sits heavy in my stomach. My neighbor, diabetic and pragmatic, counts his remaining vials like precious stones each morning, the small glass bottles catching light like promises he's not sure he can keep. I've seen him through the window, holding each vial up to check for cloudiness, for any sign of degradation—time made visible in pharmaceutical form. The ticking clock of his condition mirrors the larger countdown we all face but refuse to name, the way we're all rationing something now: clean air, water, hope, time. In the odd-numbered diary I keep—pages 1, 3, 5, 7 filled with cramped handwriting while the even numbers remain blank, as if leaving space for better news that never comes—I transcribe the alerts that interrupt our days, these electronic interludes that have replaced birdsong, church bells, all the sounds that once marked time's ordinary passage. Emergency broadcast systems test their signals at 2 PM sharp, and we've learned to set our watches by disaster drills.

Can you still hear us? This refrain follows me through the static-filled days, appearing in graffiti on overpasses, in the subject lines of emails that may never be delivered. The elliptical sentences that end mid-thought remind me of conversations with my mother, how her voice would fade on the telephone when storms took down the lines, distance swallowing words the way darkness swallows light. I'd press the receiver harder against my ear, as if proximity could resurrect meaning from silence. Now it's smoke that swallows sound, carbon particulate levels that determine when we can speak clearly to one another, when our voices carry more than a few feet, when the air allows for anything beyond the most essential communications. We've developed a new sign language for bad air days, gestures that mean *I love you* and *check your filters* and *is today the day we leave?*

The weight of unfinished conversations hangs between us all now, words we're saving for clearer skies that may never come.

Here's the thing about writing climate disaster fiction—everyone wants the elevator pitch until they realize it's literally about elevators stopping mid-floor when the power grids fail, strangers trapped in small boxes practicing the intimacy of shared fear. High-level lens hooks, my editor says, tapping her manicured nails against a desk made from reclaimed wood, the irony lost on her. Make it sellable. Make it sing. As if eco-grief could be packaged into a neat three-act structure, as if the slow violence of environmental collapse followed the rhythm of commercial breaks, as if the market could metabolize its own destruction and excrete entertainment.

So I'm driving east, the same route as before but through a different lens now, ahead of a megafire wall that makes every Hollywood disaster look quaint and understated, and I've picked up this climate-reporter hitchhiker who won't stop filming, her arm extended out the window like she's trying to catch the apocalypse in her palm. "This is content," she says, pointing her phone at the ash-clogged atmosphere that turns noon into a perpetual golden hour, every influencer's dream lighting made nightmare. "This is data versus contemporary art." The distinction seems important to her, this categorization of catastrophe, this need to file disaster under the correct heading. I want to tell her: honey, we're all just gig-economy artists navigating disaster logistics now, invoicing for apocalypse between evacuation orders, itemizing our existential dread for tax purposes. Even storytelling is a hustle when the world's on fire and everyone wants their tragedy with a redemptive arc.

The character constellation arcs are obvious to anyone who's taken a workshop, written out on index cards and pinned to corkboards in air-conditioned rooms: pragmatic diabetic protagonist (insulin shortages = ticking clock, the kind of immediate stakes that make editors nod approvingly), the old archivist recording city sounds before dust storms bury everything (the wisdom figure, our Tiresias, bearing witness to what others cannot yet see), her childhood friend turned air-filter smuggler (classic moral foil setup, the survivor versus the keeper, pragmatism versus principle). But here's what publishers don't understand—the real tension isn't in these plot mechanics, these cause-and-effect chains they can diagram. It's in watching language suffocate under smoke, syntax breaking down like lung function, metaphors gasping for clean air while meaning dissolves into the space between words.

My odd-numbered diary entries transcribe emergency alerts between attempts at blank verse, the juxtaposition probably too heavy-handed for workshop but perfect for a reality that's forgotten subtlety. Pretentious? Maybe. But when NOAA alerts bleed into your poetic stanzas like spilled ink spreading across paper, when feedback loops layer with emergency tones until you can't distinguish art from alarm, when your morning pages become survival documentation, you realize all art is just organized panic now, formatted fear given line breaks, aesthetic anxiety dressed in its Sunday best. The stylistic signatures of our time: elliptical sentences that end mid-thought, mimicking human breath masked by smoke, the way we speak in fragments now to conserve oxygen, conserving meaning for when it matters most.

Can you still hear us? That's the refrain that editors want me to cut, their red pens circling it like vultures. Too repetitive, they say, their track changes bleeding across my screen. But repetition is the point—the way we check and recheck, call and recall, reaching through static for confirmation of continued existence. Air-quality index numbers become our heartbeats, rhythmic reminders that we're still alive: 150, 200, 350, 500, still breathing particulate matter, still converting poison into language, still insisting on story when the world insists on ending.

The megafire wall advancing from the west measured 47 miles across its leading edge when the climate reporter first noticed the ash beginning to fall, each flake a gray snowflake from hell's winter, descending with the kind of grace that makes destruction beautiful and beauty terrible. This was Tuesday, 3:47 PM Pacific Standard Time—she noted it precisely, this journalist's habit of pinning chaos to chronology—air quality index 367: hazardous, though the word hardly captured the reality of lungs laboring against an atmosphere that had declared war on the living. The particulate matter wasn't just smoke in any simple sense; spectrographic analysis would later reveal a catalog of the consumed: traces of vinyl siding from dreams of suburban permanence, roofing tar that once sheltered three generations of the same family, and the distinctive chemical signature of burned insulation—pink fiberglass transformed to gray ash, efficiency become affliction. Someone's home, atomized and airborne, now settling on windshields and watersheds with the democratic indifference of fallout, each particle a fragment of someone's everything.

The driver, heading east on Highway 20 at a calculated 55 miles per hour to maximize fuel efficiency, understood the mathematics of evacuation with the clarity of someone who'd run the numbers until they invaded his dreams: fuel capacity minus consumption rate multiplied by distance to safety, with variables for traffic density, road closures, and the x-factor of human panic. His passenger, the reporter, filmed everything—the apocalyptic light that photographers would later call "unprecedented" though it was becoming precedent, the way smoke moved like a living thing with intentions we couldn't fathom, with a logic parsed only in lung tissue and fear. She kept calling it "data versus contemporary art," this need to categorize even as categories collapsed, but he knew better. This was physics made visible, the invisible hand of thermodynamics finally showing itself. Convection columns reaching 40,000 feet into the stratosphere like middle fingers extended at the sky, generating their own weather systems—pyrocumulonimbus clouds that spawned fire tornadoes, creating winds that could throw burning debris miles ahead of the main fire front, seeding new disasters with the efficiency of dandelions dispersing destruction, each ember a potential apocalypse waiting for the right conditions to bloom.

Carbon particulate levels peaked at 2.5 micrometers—small enough to penetrate deep into lung tissue, to cross the blood-brain barrier with the ease of thoughts becoming nightmares, of fears becoming flesh. The tape hiss that replaced clear communication wasn't metaphorical anymore; it was the literal degradation of audio quality as ash interfered with electronic signals, static increasing proportionally with particulate density, the air itself becoming an enemy of connection. Music stored on cloud servers crashed not from heat—data centers were climate-controlled, bastions of cool in a burning world—but from power grid failures cascading through cooling systems like dominoes falling in precise sequence, each failure triggering the next in a pattern systems engineers called "beautifully inevitable," finding aesthetics even in infrastructure collapse.

"Can you still hear us?" The question appeared first in emergency broadcasts, those authoritative voices suddenly uncertain, then in texts between separated families—the messages coming through in fragments, letters dropped like breadcrumbs in a digital forest—finally in the last transmissions from fire crews overrun by flame fronts moving at 50 miles per hour, faster than humans can run, faster than deer can bound, faster than last words can be spoken. Sound versus silence became a binary indicator of survival, the presence of voice confirming life, its absence suggesting the alternative no one wanted to name but everyone understood. In the space between signal and static, we learned to read the silence, to parse the particular quality of quiet that meant gone rather than waiting.

We all eventually oscillate between zeal and nihilism—unfortunately, this psychological pattern has been documented in disaster response research, graphed and peer-reviewed, our despair made data for future grad students to cite. Initial adrenaline-fueled action—the first 72 hours when everyone becomes a hero, sharing resources, checking on neighbors—gives way to exhaustion, not just physical but existential, bone-deep and dream-poisoning. The recognition creeps in like smoke under door frames: that individual effort cannot match systemic failure, that we are bailing out the Titanic with teaspoons while arguing about spoon design, that the game was rigged before we were born and we're just now seeing the dealer's marked cards. Hope fatigue sets in like a chronic condition, diagnosed but untreatable, spreading through communities with its own R-naught value, each person's despair infecting three others in an exponential curve of surrender.

Pour sand slowly onto hot metal—this is the sound of time passing in its most honest form, each grain a second that can't be recovered, of memory fading particle by particle like landscapes being unmade. The tape hiss that replaces dialogue when the air quality drops too low for speech, when every word costs breath we can't spare, when conservation of oxygen takes precedence over conversation, over confession, over all the things we meant to say. But even in this suffocated language, with its numerous line breaks where breath should be, where thoughts pause mid-flight like birds suddenly aware of predators, we find ways to speak. We develop new grammars for disaster, new syntaxes for survival, morse code tapped on windows, meanings compressed into glances, whole love letters contained in the sharing of a working filter mask.

This is why it matters, why I keep driving, keep writing, keep trying to transform information into meaning: it humanizes wildfire statistics, makes every number someone's nephew, someone's mother, someone's first love. It shows us ourselves in the storm-chasers and activists, the lobbyists and TikTokers, each playing prescribed roles in this panoramic saga we never auditioned for. We are all part of this story now, following the ricochets through escalating disaster like pinballs in a machine no one knows how to stop playing, learning to live with smoke as metaphor for unprocessed trauma, for all the things we cannot metabolize but must somehow breathe through, must somehow carry in our cells until they become part of us, until we can't remember what clean air tasted like.

Clear skies versus particulate opacity—this is how we measure honesty now, how we gauge the distance between what we're told and what burns in our lungs. The weather report as polygraph test, visibility as virtue. In the end, after all the science and all the art, after the data and the despair, after the insulin runs out and the servers crash and the last birds stop singing, we return to the simple question, gentle but persistent as smoke itself, as essential as breath: Can you still hear us?

The answer comes not in words but in the space between breaths, in the pause before the next emergency alert, in the silence that holds both ending and beginning, both question and response, both cry and echo. We are still here, still breathing however we can, still asking, still recording the sounds of a world transforming one particle at a time, one loss at a time, one small act of witness at a time. The old archivist in her room, the driver on the highway, the reporter with her phone, the neighbor with his insulin—we are all part of the same story now, breathing the same smoke, asking the same question, waiting for an answer that comes only in the fact that we're still here to ask it.

Can you still hear us? We ask the smoke, the sky, the silence.

Can you still hear us? We ask each other, ourselves, tomorrow.

Can you still hear us? The question becomes prayer, becomes promise, becomes the only words that matter in a world that's learning how to end.
