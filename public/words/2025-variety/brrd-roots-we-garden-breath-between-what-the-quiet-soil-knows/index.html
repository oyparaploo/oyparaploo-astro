Poem · We Garden · 3 minutes

Soil and Sky

Breath Between What The Quiet Soil Knows

The page curls slightly at the edges now—its fibers touched by honeyed smoke, amber-warm and spiraling. The kind of smoke that doesn’t choke, but coaxes. It rises from a beeswax offering, lit at dusk, spiraling through rosemary shadows and the faint chime of distant goats.

It smells like something forgiven.

And then—beneath that sweetness—comes it.
The unspeakable from the fig grove.

It doesn’t arrive loudly. It is a hush with weight, like the moment right before your name is called in a dream. The grove exhales something thick and green, humid with knowing. Sap clings to your ankle. You feel watched—not by threat, but by ancient things taking note.

There is musk.
There is overripe fruit splitting quietly in the heat.
There is the sound of something remembering you first.

You do not speak in the fig grove.
The fig grove speaks you.

And the honeyed smoke weaves it together—what can be named, and what has always refused.
The breath between.

Let’s scent the whole page, then. Not with ink, but with musk and murmur. Every paragraph a pulse. Every comma a mycelial gasp. The page doesn’t lie flat anymore—it exhales.

Margins smell of petrichor and first light over moss.
The header holds a breath of violet stems snapped under thumb.
Its footnotes smuggle cinnamon mold, a trace of sweet rot.

The top-left corner? Apricot fuzz and nettle.
The bottom-right? A smudge of truffle sigh and glove-lining.
Where the paper curls, it smells of cedar tired of pretending to be furniture.

Every sentence sprouts lichen.
The em dashes hold mushroom spores.
A semicolon cracks open and out spills warm loam and one beetle that once loved a sycamore.

The ink, of course, has fermented—
mixed from squid memory and rainwater strained through mulch.

And you?
You don’t read it. You inhale it.
This isn’t a page.
It’s a spored reliquary of unsaid things.

Fold it once. Place it in your pocket.
By sundown, your hip will remember how soil dreams of roots.

Yes—let’s lift the canopy, crack a few windows in the prose, and let the wind pass through. Not to dilute it, but to aerate: to make room for breath, sky, and space between the root-thoughts. Here's a lifted version of your dream-manifesto—same roots, same loam, but now with air stirred gently between the lines:

The soil breathes first.

You feel it before waking—an exhale through your ribs, cool and veined, scented faintly with crushed violets and something almost apricot. The ground is not beneath you. It includes you.

You are seed, swaddled and swayed, composing your becoming.
Each perfume is written in root-language.
Lavender sighs blue.
Tomato glows with sun-leak.
Scent-threads curl outward, touching mole-fur, stone, wormtail.

Somewhere in this dreaming:
A sunflower remembers sky.
A bean hums an old dialect.
A mole forgets, then remembers again.

The roots stretch toward each other in cold grammar.
Yarrow listens.
Mint chatters.
The oak hums frost to the raspberry’s spine.

Aboveground: Maélys.
Barefoot, coffee-steamed, apricot-scented.
She plants in conversations, not rows.
Fennel leans toward invisible windowlets.
She doesn’t question them.
She lets the thyme translate.

When she sings, the water remembers.
It travels downward—
into the unplumbed, into the root-braille.
Softly, it touches a pipe.

And far below:
The catacomb exhales.

A booth flickers.
Elder Mabel weeps into garlic bread.
A server glides by, dropping mints and soft absolution.
You are there, seated.
You are always there.

A ravioli-shaped weather vane spins once above you.
The pipe trembles.
Something is coming.
Or maybe... leaving.

And then—
in the part of you that is moss-lined and cheek-down in loam,
something opens.

It does not surface.
It listens.

Glove-warm soil. Bruised chanterelle. Moth-wing musk.
A breath summoned, not sprayed.
A memory not lived but lived through you.

A page no longer flat.
An ache that smells of cedar and belonging.

Welcome back.
The garden has been waiting.
We never left it.

We root.
We rot forward.
We remember.

And the soil—
it remembers everything.
