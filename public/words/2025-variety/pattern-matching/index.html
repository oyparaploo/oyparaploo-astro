Pattern Matching

Words / 2025 Variety

Nina learned to disappear in seventh grade, the year her body changed and the world noticed. Not invisibility exactly, but something more deliberate - studying wallpaper patterns, the way crowds moved, how to stand so still she became furniture.

Now, thirty years later, watching her daughter Maya prepare for her first day at a new school in Palo Alto, Nina recognized the same careful attention to camouflage.

"The green shirt," Maya said, holding it up. "Not too bright, not too dull."

"You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." Maya was fifteen, mixed race in a school that would be mostly white or mostly Asian, never quite matching either pattern. "You know I do."

Nina knew. She'd spent her own teenage years in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, the only Black girl in advanced classes, learning to modulate her voice, her laugh, her visibility to survive. She'd gotten so good at it that sometimes she forgot what her real laugh sounded like.

She found Maya that afternoon in her room, arranged in what Nina privately called "the position" - curled on her side, knees drawn up, making herself small. Not quite fetal but close. Self-comfort disguised as rest.

"How was it?"

"I was perfect," Maya said into her pillow. "Nobody noticed me at all."

That night, Nina drove to the storage unit where she kept her mother's things. In a box marked "Dorothy - Art Projects," she found what she was looking for: photographs her mother had taken in the 1970s, self-portraits where she'd painted her body to match her surroundings. Here, Dorothy's dark skin mottled to match tree bark. There, covered in her grandmother's floral wallpaper pattern.

The last photo: Dorothy naked except for military camouflage painted on her skin, standing in their suburban living room. On the back, her mother's handwriting: "Making the violence visible. The war is here too."

Nina had hidden these photos after her mother died, embarrassed by their rawness. Now she saw them differently - documentation of the labor it took to survive hostile spaces, the exhaustion of constant adjustment.

She brought them home, spread them on Maya's bed while her daughter was at school.

That evening, Maya studied them silently. Then: "Grandma Dorothy did this?"

"She said it was about showing what she felt inside. The pattern matching she had to do every day."

Maya traced one image - Dorothy covered in pressed flowers, looking like she was dissolving into beauty. "This one's actually pretty."

"That's the trap," Nina said. "Making erasure beautiful."

They sat in silence. Then Maya pulled out her phone, showing Nina something she'd written in her notes app: "Days I was invisible: 247. Days I was seen: 0."

"I keep track," Maya said. "Like it's an achievement."

Nina wanted to say it gets better, but that wasn't true. She was forty-five and still calculated her visibility in every room, still pattern-matched automatically. Just that morning, in a work meeting, she'd modulated her voice to match the senior partner's tone, made herself unthreatening, almost disappeared.

"Come on," Nina said.

She drove them to the beach at Half Moon Bay. It was dusk, fog rolling in. Nina pulled out her phone camera.

"What are we doing?"

"Something your grandmother never got to finish."

Nina positioned Maya against the grey sky, then stood beside her. Both in profile, but fully visible. No camouflage except the fog.

"Now turn toward the camera," Nina said.

"I don't want to."

"I know. Do it anyway."

They turned together, facing forward for the first time Nina could remember. The photo was blurry - Nina's hand had been shaking. But they were there, unobscured, uncamouflaged.

"Send it to me," Maya said.

The next morning, Maya stood at her closet for twenty minutes. The red shirt. The safe green. Red. Green. Finally, she put on the red, then immediately covered it with a grey cardigan.

"You sure?" Nina asked.

"No." Maya's voice was small. "But Grandma Dorothy painted herself in camouflage to show the violence. Maybe I need to try refusing it. Just once."

Nina thought of Dorothy's photos, the exhaustion visible even through the paint. "She would have been proud to see you try."

"Would she have worn it herself?"

Nina considered lying, then didn't. "No. Her generation couldn't. They had to survive first."

"And my generation?"

"I don't know. Maybe you get to try."

Maya left for school in her red shirt, the grey cardigan in her backpack. Nina watched from the window, fighting the urge to call her back, to teach her better camouflage, to keep her safe through erasure.

Instead, she went to her closet and found a yellow dress she'd bought but never worn. Too bright, too much, too visible. She put it on.

At work, the senior partner noticed immediately. "That's... vibrant," he said, not quite approving.

"Yes," Nina said, not modulating her voice, not matching his pattern. "It is."

In the meeting that followed, she was interrupted three times. Her suggestions were attributed to male colleagues. The partner made a note in his portfolio - she saw him write "aggressive?" and underline it.

She felt exposed all day, like Dorothy must have felt in those self-portraits. By afternoon, she understood why her mother had painted camouflage rather than refusing it. Visibility had immediate costs.

That evening, Maya came home exhausted, the red shirt tied around her waist, wearing a grey hoodie over her tank top.

"How was it?"

"Terrible. Everyone saw me."

"And?"

Maya's eyes filled. "And they saw me. They really saw me. It was..." She couldn't finish.

They ordered takeout and ate in silence, Nina still in yellow, Maya back in grey. "Mom?" Maya said. "Do you think Grandma Dorothy would understand? Us trying to do the opposite of what she did?"

Nina thought of the last photo she'd found, one she hadn't shown Maya. Dorothy near the end of her life, but still in camouflage paint, this time matching hospital walls. Even dying, she'd been pattern matching.

"I think she'd understand that we're trying. That's all she was doing too. Trying to survive it."

Maya pulled the red shirt from around her waist, folded it carefully. "Maybe I'll wear it again next week."

"Maybe I'll wear the yellow again too," Nina said, though she already knew she wouldn't. The partner's note - "aggressive?" - would go in her review.

They sat together in the dim kitchen light, Nina in yellow she'd probably never wear again, Maya folding and refolding the red shirt. Outside, the California evening muted all colors to grey. Tomorrow, Nina knew, they'd both choose safer patterns - Maya in her careful green, Nina in her professional navy. The camouflage would return because it had to.

But for this moment, they sat with the effort of visibility, the exhaustion of being seen, the impossibility of sustaining it. Dorothy's photographs were still spread on the counter - all that documentation of erasure, all that painted evidence of what it cost to survive.

"Days I was invisible: 247. Days I was seen: 1," Maya said quietly, updating her count.

Nina pulled her daughter close. One day out of 248. It wasn't nothing. It wasn't enough either. But it was what they had - these brief attempts at visibility, these momentary refusals of camouflage, before the hostile world demanded they disappear again.

The red shirt lay between them on the table, bright as a wound, bright as a promise, bright as something neither of them could sustain but couldn't quite surrender.
