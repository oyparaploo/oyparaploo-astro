Between Guilty and Innocent

← Words / 2025 Variety

In courtrooms where verdicts destroy lives, a court reporter who types everything but sees more begins teaching others how to look through events rather than at them, discovering that the real justice happens in the spaces between guilty and innocent.

Véronique Spool had been typing other people's words for seventeen years. Court reporter, they called it, though she wasn't reporting anything. Just capturing. Every lie, every truth, every pause where truth and lie touched. $31,000 a year. Brooklyn rent: $2,100 a month for a studio with a bathtub in the kitchen. The math never worked but she kept typing anyway.

"You type everything?" the new prosecutor asked. Fresh from Columbia Law. $190,000 starting salary. Shoes worth two months of Véronique's rent.

"Everything said. Nothing... well. Not nothing." She looked at her steno machine. Twenty-three years old, held together with rubber bands. Like her.

She was doing something else. Seeing through her third eye—not mystical, just that place in the center, in the between, where vision integrated what others separated. The teenage defendant was criminal AND child—seventeen, tried as adult for selling fentanyl that killed someone, but also still had Pokemon cards in his pocket. The victim was broken AND breaking others—dead from overdose but had been stealing from his grandmother's Social Security to buy. The judge was authority AND some exhausted guy who kept Tums in his desk drawer, whose son was the same age as the defendant.

"Unpack the layers," she'd mutter while typing, fingers flying at 260 words per minute. "Unpack the whole construction."

This trial was brutal. Mother killed her baby. Or saved it from worse. Depends how you look. Or how you look through. Baby had been born addicted, seizing, 4 pounds 3 ounces. Mother had been using throughout pregnancy. Also had been sex trafficked since age twelve. The baby died at four months. Suffocation. Or SIDS. Or the inevitable conclusion of a story that started before the mother was even born.

Troy Rousseau, public defender, kept glancing at her. Public defender salary: $65,000. Law school debt: $198,000. Something about how she typed—like she was catching what wasn't being said. After the verdict (guilty, twenty-five to life, the mother collapsed so quietly you could hear her bones giving up), he found her in the hallway.

"You see it different, don't you?"

"See what?"

"The... I don't know. The thing underneath. Like you're typing one trial but watching another."

Véronique looked at him. Really looked. Through him almost. Saw his father in his stance—disappointed accountant who'd wanted a doctor for a son. "You defending her or defending yourself from her?"

"What?"

"You kept looking at the jury like they were your parents. Like you were eight and got caught with something. Like when you stole forty dollars from your mom's purse to buy a Gameboy game and she made you return it."

Troy's mouth opened. Closed. "How did you—"

"Spiritual and political converge in there. Every time. We're all trying to make these crucial ethical moves but we're also just... broken kids playing dress-up. Your suit's too big. Your father's?"

"Jesus."

"Yeah, him too probably. If he was real, he'd be in the defendant's chair. Radical who threatened the state. They killed him for it."

They started talking. Not meetings. Just bumping into each other between trials, talking too long. Véronique explaining her thing without really explaining it.

"God doesn't look toward heaven," she said once, eating vending machine crackers. B4. $1.75. Lunch. "And we don't look inward for personal enlightenment. We just... reinterpret those who are overlooked."

"Like who?"

"That mother's other kids. In the back. You see them?"

Troy hadn't. Three kids. Foster care. Watching their mom get destroyed. The oldest, maybe eleven, had the same eyes as his mother. Already seeing through.

"They're the real verdict. Watching their mom get twenty-five to life. That's what I'm typing even when I'm typing 'guilty.' The oldest will be in this same courtroom in six years. Different charge, same story."

"But you can't type that."

"Can't I?" She smiled, strange. In her transcripts, she'd started adding brackets: [defendant's child begins crying] [prosecutor avoids eye contact] [judge takes third antacid]. "My observing is an organ. Witness and solidarity. What's discarded becomes focus."

More people started noticing. Jennifer from the DA's office—$95,000 a year but drinking wine from a box—caught Véronique in the bathroom, crying after a child abuse case.

"You okay?"

"I see it all like a ghostly rainbow. Visible but not... material? Present and translucent. This kid was abused but also... god, the way he looked at his dad. Love and terror just... mixed. Still wanted his approval. Still loved the monster."

"That's fucked up."

"That's reality. We perceive with these sharp categories—guilty, innocent, victim, perp. But it's really all gradations. Mixtures. That's the truth of it."

"That's depressing."

"No! It's... it's astute vision. Refuses binary thinking. Makes change possible. If people are only monsters or victims, nothing changes. But if we're all both..."

Jennifer started watching different. Seeing different. The courthouse became something else—not temple of justice but this weird machine that sorted people into boxes that didn't fit, that took $20 billion in city budget to destroy families that could have been saved with $20,000 interventions.

Marcus the bailiff got it next. Just from proximity, from watching Véronique type like she was playing some complex musical instrument. Twelve years on the job. $44,000 a year. Still believed in justice until he started seeing through.

"You operate through invisibility," he said one day.

"What?"

"Like... reproductive labor? Essential work that keeps society functioning but nobody sees it. You're keeping the record but also keeping... something else."

"The real record. Sound and silence. Presence and absence. It's not about the specific words but the relationships between them." She typed as she talked, fingers never stopping, catching every word plus what lived between them.

Marcus started watching different. Started seeing how defendants' families were the real criminals and victims both—how poverty was the perpetrator, how the system was the violence, how every verdict created more crime than it prevented.

Troy started defending different. Not trying to prove innocence but showing the through-ness of everything. How every criminal was criminalized first. How every crime contained its own victim story.

"Your honor," he'd say, "my client is guilty. Also innocent. Also neither. Also both. He sold drugs that killed someone. He was also born into a life expectancy of 61 years in this zip code versus 85 years six miles north."

Judges hated it. Juries... juries got it sometimes. Saw through to something more true than verdicts.

Véronique kept typing. Faster now, like she was trying to capture not just words but the spaces between them. Started adding her own punctuation: ellipses where the truth trailed off, parentheses around lies, brackets around the unsaid but visible.

"Slow down," the judge told her once.

"Can't. This is the speed of seeing through. You consume it quick or you miss it. Like that moment where the defendant's mother mouths 'I love you' but you rule against her anyway. [Three second pause] [Judge touches photo of own daughter]"

The day Véronique quit, she left her machine still typing. Just clicking away at nothing, making its own record of silence.

Troy found her in the parking garage. Her car—1997 Honda Civic, 267,000 miles, driver door didn't open from inside.

"Where you going?"

"Nowhere. Everywhere. The third eye is tired of courtrooms. Wants to see through other things."

"You changed how I see."

"No. You always saw through. I just... typed while you did it. Made it real. Made it count. Put it in the record that nobody reads but exists anyway."

She left, but the seeing stayed. Troy, Jennifer, Marcus—they all kept looking through instead of at. Kept refusing the binary. Kept seeing the ghostly rainbow where others saw black and white, the gradations that were the real truth, the way every verdict was both justice and injustice, every criminal both guilty and innocent, every system both necessary and destroying what it claimed to protect.

In court, where spiritual and political converged every day at the intersection of poverty and punishment, they became the new note-takers. Not typing but seeing. Not recording but transforming. Operating through their own invisibility to witness what was overlooked.

The real justice, they learned, was never in verdicts. It was in the refusal to stop seeing through. In the unpacking. In the space between guilty and innocent where everyone actually lived, where mothers killed babies they loved, where children sold drugs to buy school supplies, where judges took antacids and thought of their own children, where court reporters typed everything and nothing, making $31,000 a year to record the dissolution of humanity at 260 words per minute, catching every word except the ones that mattered most, the ones that lived in the pauses, in the looks, in the space between guilty and innocent where everyone was both and neither and the only real verdict was that the system itself was guilty but would never be tried.

We are all through-seers in the trials of existence, capturing the official record while witnessing the gradations and mixtures that constitute actual truth, operating through invisibility to focus on what's discarded, discovering that justice lives not in verdicts but in the continuous act of seeing through every construction to the active principle of change beneath, typed at 260 words per minute into a record nobody reads but everyone lives.
