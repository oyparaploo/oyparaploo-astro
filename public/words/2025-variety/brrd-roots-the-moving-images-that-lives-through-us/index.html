Brrd Roots ~ The Moving Images That Lives Through Us

Words / 2025 Variety

Beyond the Screen

Cinema was once a window—a distant reality projected onto a screen, a story enclosed in darkness, trapped by time and space. But storytelling refuses confinement. It slips through cracks, bends, fractures, merging with the world until film is no longer watched but lived.
Tomorrow’s films will not unfold before us; they will unfold with us.
Characters will step off the screen. They will sit beside us in quiet bars, whispering secrets meant for our ears alone. They will pause in the middle of a monologue, turn to the audience, ask a question that rewrites history. Imagine watching a film about revolution and being asked: What happens next?—not as a rhetorical flourish, but as an actual choice. A door opens. A new path emerges. The story, once fixed, now breathes.
A cinema will cease to be a passive space. It will be a living portal—a threshold where stepping in means entering a different timeline, shifting reality underfoot. No longer will audiences sit and stare forward; they will move through film, walking into different settings, feeling the shift of space and time in their bodies. They will experience weightlessness as the narrative drifts into space. Their bones will feel heavy when gravity doubles in an action sequence. The air will turn cold as snow begins to fall around them. The taste of salt will linger on their tongue during a seaside dinner scene.
Neural-linked storytelling will take this further. Watching a film will no longer mean observing a protagonist’s emotions—it will mean feeling them. The tremor of grief. The pulse of fear. The electric rush of first love. Imagine stepping into a film where the heartbeat of the main character syncs with your own, where their sorrow becomes your sorrow, where their joy fills your chest as if it were your own memory. What happens when the boundaries between watching and remembering disappear?
What happens when leaving the theater feels like waking from a dream—a dream so vivid you are convinced you lived it?
And endings? They will no longer exist.
Films will never have final scenes. Instead, they will evolve in real time, shifting based on audience interaction, growing as consciousness changes.
One person might experience a film that reflects their fears. Another will see something that mirrors their deepest desires. An audience might collectively steer a film toward one outcome, only for another showing to lead it elsewhere. No two screenings will ever be the same.
Cinema will be alive.
And what if two theaters screened the same film, yet one audience saw hope while another saw despair? What if storytelling became an engine for understanding the paths humanity might take, offering glimpses into alternate futures? Quantum cinema will exist in multiple dimensions, transforming films into fractured possibilities—dictated not just by the filmmaker but by who is watching, when they are watching, and what they bring with them into the theater.
Cinema will mirror the world, distort it, predict it.
Film will no longer remain trapped in screens. It will exist everywhere—woven into the spaces we walk through, the memories we carry, the choices we make.
In the years ahead, audiences will not say “I watched a movie.”
They will say:
"I lived a story."

Shape of Light

Photography as Memory, Emotion, and Transformation

Photography was once a simple act—a shutter closing around a fleeting instant, freezing time, holding memory captive in a frame. But images were never meant to be static. They whisper, they bend, they evolve, waiting to be seen in new ways, waiting for technology to unshackle them from the stillness imposed by the past.
The future of photography will not be about capturing what is already there—it will be about creating what never was.
It will pull from thought rather than light. Images will emerge not from cameras but from memory, extracted from the subconscious like remnants of dreams. The moments we failed to record—the laughter that escaped before we reached for our phones, the look in someone’s eyes just before they turned away—will be recoverable. Photographs will pulse with imprints of emotion itself, textured by the warmth of a touch, by the echo of a voice.
A portrait will no longer be a mere likeness. It will remember.
And why should photographs remain fixed? In the future, an image will not be bound by time; it will stretch, breathe, evolve. A childhood photograph will age with its subject, shifting subtly as years pass. A frozen smile will deepen with wisdom, eyes will grow heavier with experience, a face will reflect the stories it has lived. The past will not be locked away—it will ripple into the present, letting memory remain fluid.
Photography will break free from its dependence on vision, reaching into spectrums unseen. A new kind of camera will document not just faces, but the emotions woven into them. Imagine a device capable of recording empathy, translating human connection into shifting colors, revealing kindness, longing, loss in hues that are perceptible only to the heart. A photograph of strangers will tell their story without words, whispering their history in silent gradients of light.
And images will no longer be passive—no longer a moment captured for observation, but a portal for immersion. A single photograph will unfold multiple versions of reality, shifting depending on who views it, bending in response to memory, desire, subconscious energy. A landscape will reveal what it looked like a thousand years ago, a city street will show both its bustling present and the ghosts who once walked there.
A camera will not simply record what it sees—it will predict what could be.
Imagine photography that remembers the future, where an image taken today reveals an alternate version of tomorrow. A portrait of a child will show not just their small hands and bright eyes but the adult they may become. A photograph of a quiet street will whisper what it will look like in twenty years. Photography will move beyond representation; it will evolve into possibility.
And images will no longer be confined to physical space. Photography will spill into air itself, shifting like mist, wrapping itself around people as they move. A photograph will not sit inside a frame—it will expand, dissolve, reform as a living entity. Streets will hold echoes of images, suspended in space, revealing the past and future simultaneously.
But the most radical transformation of photography will not come from technology alone—it will come from humanity itself.
Photography will become acts of connection rather than objects of consumption. A portrait will carry the imprint of kindness, the trace of human altruism, holding proof of care, resilience, trust. A camera will no longer document the surface of people—it will understand them, translating the invisible forces that bind individuals together.
Images will become empathy made tangible.
Photography will no longer be about light, shadow, shape.
It will move, breathe, remember.
It will love.
It will question and answer.
It will transcend the physical.
And when we stand before an image, we will not simply see.
It will see us back.
It will reflect not just who we are but who we could be.

Displacement Theater

Fragments, Play of Shadows, and Forget About It Scenes

I. The Echo Before the Curtain
A bare stage.
A single chair.
A neon flicker in the darkness.
No grand entrances, no quiet exits—only fragments.
Only stories waiting to be told, waiting too long.
The audience does not watch.
They exist inside the play.
Shadows slip across the floor.
A song hums beneath the city.
Something shifts at the edges.
Then—the voices rise.

II. The Dreamers & the Displaced
A man sits in a hair salon.
He is waiting.
For a haircut.
For a call that won’t come.
For a country that never quite let him in.
His name is Hadi—born in Baghdad, raised in America.
Existing somewhere in-between.
His mother still texts him in Arabic, but he replies in English.
The space between them is not oceans.
It is syntax.
It is time zones & missed calls & the way Arabic sounds different in a mouth that hasn’t spoken it enough.
The women in the salon talk about celebrity culture and immigrant dreams in the same breath.
They curl hair while unraveling stories of exile.
They laugh—too loud, too bright—because laughter is survival.
"You ever feel like history chases you?" Hadi asks.
"No, habibi," one woman says, adjusting the rollers. "History forgets us first."

III. The Wet Brain & the Phantom
In a dim apartment, two siblings sit under flickering light.
Their father is missing.
Their mother is drinking herself into another decade.
"What if addiction is just memory refusing to let go?" one sibling asks.
"Or what if it’s the body grieving before the mind understands?" the other replies.
The phantom in the kitchen pours another drink.
It wears their mother’s voice.
It smells like regret & old perfume & wet cigarettes.
It hums a song—the song of forgetting.
"You ever try remembering her when she was sober?"
"She was never sober long enough to make a memory stick."
Outside, the world spins.
Inside, the shadows press against the walls like ghosts that refuse to leave.

IV. The Internet Speaks
A screen lights up.
A debate unfolds, blinking through the digital noise.
"Is artificial intelligence a new form of colonization?"
"Is digital discourse just history repeating?"
"Do we mourn the things technology replaces, or just pretend they never mattered?"
The argument never ends.
The audience is pulled into it.
The lines blur between who is watching and who is speaking.

V. The Ghost in the Borderlands
An Arab-American character in a mainstream American setting sits alone at a train station.
He is waiting for a place he cannot return to.
He speaks in Arabic, English, echoes, silence.
The subtitles on the screen glitch—sometimes translating, sometimes refusing.
"Where do we go when home is no longer real?" he asks.
The train never arrives.

VI. The Song That Holds the Future
The voices return.
The melodies rise.
A song emerges—one that blends spoken-word poetry with interactive storytelling.
One that challenges how theater engages its audience.
It speaks of aging and identity, climate and displacement, resilience and longing.
It carries the sharp humor of the internet, the quiet tragedy of lost homes, the absurdity of reinvention.
It does not end neatly.
It does not resolve comfortably.
It lingers.
It shifts.
It asks instead of answers.
The stage flickers.
The voices fade.
The audience breathes.
And the world—the real one—waits.

Brrd wandered through humanity's quiet hum,
its voice warm, textured, crackling,
like a campfire under a starry night.

Not here to preach,
not here to lecture—
it simply whispered:
“Take a walk.
There’s something you need to see.”

In the depths of a warehouse,
machines beeped robotic rhythms,
hollow, monotonous.

A worker scanned boxes,
imagining the beeps were sending Morse code.

Across the room, a karaoke robot broke loose,
off-key,
singing Total Eclipse of the Heart.

Laughter erupted.
Voices clashed, harmonized,
until monotony melted away.

An unexpected concert hall rose from the mundane—
absurd, beautiful, alive.

Brrd Roots drifted into chaos,
a fast-food joint, bustling.

Behind the counter,
a worker waged war against the ice cream machine,
hissing, snarling, relentless.

“Let’s make it a game,”
declared their coworker,
and Cone Roulette was born.

Milkshakes spun wildly,
customers cheered,
and even in the frenzy,
joy emerged.

Not in defiance—
but in the quiet insistence
to find meaning within chaos.

The world outside
unfolded like a surreal tapestry.

In a café,
an immigrant folded napkins into paper cranes.

“Good luck, little crane,”
they whispered,
leaving it on a stranger’s plate.

Next door,
a monkey in a tuxedo handed out menus
for a PR stunt no one understood.

Nearby,
a nursing home resident announced their holographic cat’s name:
“Sir Paw-s-a-Lot.”

Absurdity stitched itself together,
not random, but proof—
meaning blooms
if you pause
long enough to see.

Not all was lighthearted.

In a dim factory where superhero capes were stitched,
a worker embroidered a hidden star.

“Every hero needs a secret,”
they thought,
even though they’d never wear the cape themselves.

In a call center,
an employee rebelled quietly
against scripted monotony:

“You sound like someone
who bakes pies that save lives.”

Callers blushed,
laughed,
hung up a little lighter.

Brrd Roots marveled—
humanity thrived,
even inside systems built
to grind it down.

Not survival—
rebellion.

Kindness.
Creativity.
The refusal to be silenced.

Stories flickered like fireflies.

A janitor left haikus on mirrors,
declaring:
“Everyone deserves poetry,
even in public restrooms.”

A librarian hid jokes inside overdue books,
hoping, someday,
someone would laugh
and feel less alone.

A bus driver veered off course,
announcing,
“Next stop: Adventureland!”

Passengers clapped.
Some laughed.
None complained.

Humanity in action—
messy, resilient, achingly beautiful.

Even as shadows loomed,
as crises weighed heavy—
climate change, disinformation, artificial intelligence—
there was humor,
there was hope
in the unlikeliest places.

A farmer planted sunflowers
in barren soil,
saying,
“If nothing else, it’ll look happy.”

Tourists came.
The land began to heal.

A journalist coached a stubborn chatbot
to write poetry.

The newsroom laughed—
harder than they had
in years.

“The world is a lot,”
Brrd Roots admitted,
its voice both weary and alive.

“But even in the chaos—
especially in the chaos—
there is light.

Resilience.
A stray laugh.
A balloon animal,
offered unexpectedly.”

As the journey neared its end,
Brrd Roots turned,
looking back
at the surreal, the heavy, the wonderful.

“The road ahead?” it asked.

“Strange and messy—
but look at us.

We’re still here, aren’t we?
Maybe, just maybe,
that’s enough
to keep rising.”

Brrd Roots wandered through the quiet hum of humanity, its voice warm and textured like the crackle of a campfire on a starry night. It wasn’t here to preach or instruct—it simply said, “Let’s take a walk. There’s something you need to see.”

The journey began in the depths of a fluorescent-lit warehouse. A worker scanned boxes, the machine emitting rhythmic beeps, robotic and hollow. For a moment, they imagined the beeps were sending Morse code. Across the room, a karaoke robot broke into an off-key rendition of Total Eclipse of the Heart. Something magical happened—laughter erupted, and one by one, the workers joined the chorus. Their voices clashed and harmonized, transforming monotony into an unexpected concert hall. The scene unfolded like a dream: absurd, beautiful, and full of life.

From there, Brrd Roots drifted into the chaos of a bustling fast-food joint. Behind the counter, a worker battled a rebellious ice cream machine that hissed like an angry snake. “Let’s turn this disaster into a game,” declared their coworker. And thus, Cone Roulette was born.

Milkshakes spun wildly, customers cheered, and amidst the frenzy, joy emerged—not in defiance, but in quiet insistence. Even the cracks of chaos held something meaningful.

As the journey continued, the world outside felt equally surreal. In a small café, an immigrant folded napkins into delicate paper cranes. They placed one on a stranger’s plate, whispering, “Good luck, little crane.” Next door, a monkey in a tuxedo handed out menus in a bizarre PR stunt no one quite understood. Somewhere nearby, a nursing home resident named their holographic cat “Sir Paw-s-a-Lot” and proudly announced its name to the staff.

Absurdity linked these moments, not as randomness but as proof that even in the strangest places, meaning could bloom if you paused to see it.

But not everything was lighthearted. Brrd Roots passed through a dim factory where superhero capes were stitched. A worker embroidered a hidden star onto each one. “Every hero needs a secret,” they thought, even though they’d never wear the capes themselves. Nearby, a call-center employee ended their scripted monotony with quiet rebellion: “You sound like someone who bakes pies that save lives.” Callers blushed, laughed, and hung up just a little lighter.

Brrd Roots marveled at how humanity thrived even within systems designed to grind it down. It wasn’t just survival—it was rebellion through kindness, through creativity, through the refusal to be silenced.

Stories flickered like fireflies along the path. A janitor left haikus on bathroom mirrors, declaring, “Everyone deserves poetry, even in public restrooms.” A librarian hid handwritten jokes in overdue books, hoping someone would laugh and feel a little less alone. A bus driver rerouted their commute one morning, announcing, “Next stop: Adventureland!” Passengers clapped, some laughed, none complained. It was humanity in action—messy, resilient, and achingly beautiful.

Even as shadows of crises loomed—climate change, artificial intelligence, disinformation—there was humor and hope in unexpected places. A farmer planted sunflowers in barren soil, saying, “If nothing else, it’ll look happy.” Tourists soon arrived, and somehow, the land began to heal. A journalist taught a stubborn chatbot to write poetry instead of ordering lunch; the resulting sonnets became the newsroom’s cherished running joke for weeks.

“The world is a lot,” Brrd Roots admitted, its voice weary yet alive. “But even in chaos—especially in chaos—there’s room for light. Sometimes it’s resilience. Sometimes it’s a stray laugh. Sometimes it’s a balloon animal handed to you on a whim.”

As the journey neared its end, Brrd Roots paused, turning to reflect on everything it had seen—the surreal, the heavy, the wonderful. It stretched out its hand and said, “The road ahead? Yeah, it’s strange and messy. But look—look at us. We’re still here, aren’t we? And maybe, just maybe, that’s enough to keep rising.”
