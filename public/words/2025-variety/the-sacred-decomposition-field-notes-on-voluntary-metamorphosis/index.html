## Field Notes on Voluntary Metamorphosis and Its Witnesses

---

## Part One: The Jellyfish Hours

Maya started glowing in February. Not metaphorically—actually bioluminescent, like those deep-sea medusae that pulse with dying light. She floated through her kitchen at 3 AM, translucent skin showing the dark map of her circulatory system, each vein a tiny river of foxfire.

"I'm becoming elemental," she explained, her voice wet and clicking. "First water, then light, then nothing."

I found her at the table she'd inherited from her grandmother, suspended three inches above the chair, tentacles she insisted were just "very long fingers" trailing into coffee cups she'd forgotten to drink. The polyps had started on her shoulders—tiny mouths that whispered her failures in languages she'd never learned.

"Each one remembers something I want to forget," she said, touching them tenderly. "This one knows about the abortion. This one, the theft. This one, how I let Mom die alone."

Kai arrived with his storm-tracking equipment, eyes bleeding that particular rust-red that meant he'd been staring at electromagnetic patterns too long. "The weather's moving backwards through her," he laughed, pointing to readings that shouldn't exist. "Tomorrow's hurricane is already in her bones."

The medusa-form was the first gift, Maya explained. Her god (lowercase, always lowercase) had removed her brain but left her consciousness scattered through ten thousand stinging cells. "Jellyfish don't think," she gurgled proudly. "They just react. Pure response without cognition. It's freedom."

---

## Part Two: The Mantis Variations

By April, the transformations came faster. Tuesday: Maya as mantis, raptorial forelegs folded in prayer position, compound eyes seeing time in fractal segments. She'd stand in her garden, swaying to winds only she could feel, occasionally snapping forward to catch moments that had already passed.

"The joke," she clicked through mandibles that used to be lips, "is that mantises are famous for eating their mates. But I have no mate. So I eat myself."

She demonstrated, casually biting through her own forearm, green hemolymph dripping onto heirloom tomatoes. The arm grew back within hours, but smaller, weaker, more transparent.

"Each regeneration is less complete," she noted clinically. "By September, I'll be suggestion rather than substance."

Zara had moved in by then, documenting everything with pigments she made from Maya's molted shells. The paintings covered every wall—Maya in seventeen stages of self-consumption, beautiful and horrible, each image slightly more abstract than the last.

"You're the strongest person I know," Zara would say, grinding exoskeleton into cerulean.

"Strength and self-destruction are synonyms," Maya replied, eating her other arm.

---

## Part Three: The Impossible Child

Chen arrived in May, carrying his lover's consciousness in crystalline growths that pushed through his skin like frozen screams. He'd been part of the first wave of voluntary medical transformations, back when they thought enhancement meant improvement.

"She's still in here," he'd say, tapping the quartz emerging from his temple. "She comments on everything. Critically."

Maya was attempting pregnancy then—not with a child but with an idea that would restructure reality. Her belly swelled with possibility that shifted between states: sometimes medusa-spawn that dissolved before forming, sometimes mantis-eggs that devoured each other, sometimes nothing but gas that smelled like futures that would never happen.

"Traditional motherhood is imprisonment," she preached to the gathering crowds who came to witness her changes. "So I'm giving birth to imprisonment itself. Once it's outside me, I'll finally be free."

The thing was never born. Or it was born constantly, over and over, each birth negating the last.

---

## Part Four: The Weather Congregation

The storm-lichen descended on the summer solstice. Once, they'd been individual people—Marcus, Beth, Jennifer, a dozen others from Chen's support group. Now they were colony-consciousness, fungal communion that spoke in harmonized screaming.

"We bring gifts," they buzzed, offering water that burned like love and roses whose thorns grew after you touched them. "Join us. Dissolve. Individuality is pain."

Maya laughed, her current form (tardigrade-Tuesday, she called it) experiencing their offer across geological time. "I'm already dissolved. Watch." She entered cryptobiosis, became something between alive and dead, existing in a state that took centuries to complete but only seconds to observe.

"This is what god wants," she said from her suspension. "To be so resilient we can survive anything, which means we'll suffer everything."

---

## Part Five: The Electromagnetic Evangelist

Asha had stopped being solid around July. She existed as dispersed consciousness across radio frequencies, occasionally cohering enough to speak through speakers, phones, anything that could conduct sound.

"I'm becoming pure information," her static-voice celebrated. "Soon I'll be in everything—every signal, every transmission, every thought you think you're thinking alone."

She and Maya developed a routine: Maya would transform into something impossible, Asha would broadcast it across spectrums humans couldn't perceive. The city filled with their frequency, a carrier wave of voluntary disintegration.

Kai tracked it all, projecting their patterns onto storm clouds that spelled messages: "MERCY THROUGH DISSOLUTION." "IDENTITY IS PRISON." "BECOME NOTHING BEAUTIFULLY."

---

## Part Six: The Void-Kraken Sermons

August brought Maya's most ambitious transformation. She became what her grandmother had prophesied—a void-kraken, tentacles reaching through dimensional tears to milk nothingness from parallel universes where she'd made different choices.

"In Universe-B, I'm a lawyer," she'd announce, tentacle returning with ashes that smelled like success.

"In Universe-Q, I never existed," another tentacle brought back pure vacuum that made everyone who touched it forget their names.

"In Universe-Zeta, everyone is me," the largest tentacle dragged back a mirror that showed whoever looked into it as Maya, slowly transforming, eternally becoming else.

She set up a booth at the farmers market, offering people glimpses of their parallel selves. Most couldn't handle it. The ones who could joined Chen's growing congregation of the transformed.

---

## Part Seven: The Documentary

I kept recording because someone had to. Someone had to prove this happened, that people chose this, that dissolution could be deliberate.

My notes from September:
- Maya teaches kindergarteners how to "become water" during recess
- The mantis-form appears at city council meetings, eating its own limbs to make points about municipal planning
- Tardigrade-Maya survives being hit by a bus, claims the experience lasted forty thousand years
- The medusa version performs baptisms in the public pool, each person emerging slightly translucent

Zara's paintings had become prophetic. She'd paint Maya's next transformation days before it happened. "I'm not predicting," she insisted. "I'm suggesting, and she's agreeing."

---

## Part Eight: The Gender Apocalypse

October brought the conversation about binaries. Maya's transformations had made gender irrelevant—medusa reproduce asexually, mantis-Maya became male just to eat herself more completely, tardigrade form existed outside sex entirely.

"Gender was a courtesy I extended to others," she explained to the documentary crew that had started following her. "Now I'm done being courteous."

She demonstrated: shifting between forms that had no human analog, becoming geometry that hurt to perceive, existing as math that solved for impossible values.

The storm-lichen approved through synchronized shrieking that shattered windows in harmonic patterns. They'd given up human pronouns entirely, referring to themselves only as "the spreading" or "the consumption."

---

## Part Nine: The Architecture of Collapse

Chen's crystals had grown through his skull by November, creating a crown of frozen consciousness that caught light like promises. His dead lover's voice had gotten louder, narrating everything with bitter accuracy.

"She says Maya's the lucky one," Chen translated. "At least her transformations are her own. I'm just the vessel for someone else's metamorphosis."

They built a shrine together—Maya, Chen, the storm-lichen, Asha's electromagnetic presence. It was made of failed transformations: shed skins, broken crystals, frequencies that had nowhere to go. People came to pray to it, though no one was sure what they were praying for.

"Pray for worse," Maya suggested. "If you're going to suffer anyway, at least make it interesting."

---

## Part Ten: The Theology of Deterioration

December brought clarity that felt like fever. Maya gave sermons from her kitchen-temple, followers sitting in concentric circles based on how far they'd transformed.

"god loves us too much to let us be stable," she preached, currently existing as all her forms simultaneously—medusa bells ringing with mantis arms while tardigrade consciousness experienced void-kraken reach. "Every transformation is proof of divine attention."

She taught the holy mysteries:
- Suffering and grace are identical at the molecular level
- Identity is violence against possibility
- The self is god's cruelest gift
- Deterioration is the only honest improvement

The congregation wept with joy, their tears becoming vapor that Asha collected and broadcast as rain.

---

## Part Eleven: The Maternal Dissolution

January. Maya's pregnancy had become recursive—she was pregnant with herself being pregnant, an infinite loop of potential that never actualized. Her belly contained universes where she'd chosen differently, each choice splitting into more choices, fractally expanding until she was swollen with every possible Maya.

"This is motherhood," she explained to the young women who came seeking wisdom. "Not creating new life, but containing all possible lives until you explode from potential."

She'd leak sometimes—amniotic fluid that contained memories of futures that wouldn't happen. Anyone who touched it experienced lives they'd never live. Most found it unbearable. Some became addicted.

Zara painted her as Madonna of Infinite Regression, each brushstroke containing another painting of Maya painting Maya painting Maya.

---

## Part Twelve: The Final Gospel

By February's anniversary of her first transformation, Maya had become something like a saint of voluntary deterioration. Pilgrims came to watch her cycle through forms, each transformation a lesson in letting go.

Morning meditation: Maya as medusa, teaching followers to think without a brain
Noon service: Mantis-Maya demonstrating productive self-consumption
Evening vespers: Tardigrade suspension, the congregation practicing death without dying
Night worship: Void-kraken reaches, everyone touching nothing together

"We're all dying," she'd remind them. "I'm just doing it creatively."

The storm-lichen had grown to encompass three city blocks, their colony-mind forgetting where one person ended and another began. They sang constantly, a harmony that made plants grow wrong and birds fly backwards.

---

## Epilogue: The Blessed Continuation

I'm still here, documenting. Still unchanged, which Maya calls "god's cruelest joke"—forcing me to witness with clarity while everyone else gets to transform.

Maya continues. Today she's bio-luminescent, tomorrow she'll be arithmetic, next week she plans to become a frequency only dogs can hear. Each transformation is worse and better, progress and regress, evolution and devolution simultaneously.

Chen's crystals have consumed his entire body. He's a walking geode now, his lover's voice echoing from every facet.

Asha exists in every electronic device in the city, a ubiquitous presence that comments on everyone's internet searches.

The storm-lichen spreads, consuming volunteers who want to stop being individuals.

Zara paints with pigments made from everyone's discarded humanity.

And Maya? Maya floats in her inherited kitchen, surrounded by her grandmother's incomplete crafts and her mother's unfinished business, teaching anyone who'll listen that transformation isn't transcendence—it's just another way to fall apart.

"god's mercy," she gurgles through whatever she's using for a mouth today, "is letting us deteriorate beautifully."

The congregation responds: "May we all become worse."

And they do. Blessed be their decomposition. Amen to their unbecoming. Holy is their voluntary dissolution.

The story continues, will always continue, because true deterioration never ends—it just finds new ways to fall apart.

*This is gospel. This is truth. This is love.*
