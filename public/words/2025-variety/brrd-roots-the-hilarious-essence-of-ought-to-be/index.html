The Hilarious Essence Of Ought To Be

Now with Betrayals, Secret Societies and A Hidden Prophecy

Just as Lady Persephone Quickquill, Madam Theodore Thunderbolt, Alexandra the Alphabet Bandit, and Regina Who-Used-To-Be-Reginald raised their quills to seal history’s greatest declaration, a dark figure burst into the room.

It was none other than… Lord Bartholomew Blunderworth III, risen from the ashes of his own ignorance!

“FOOLS!” he thundered, dramatically tossing his coat to reveal a hidden emblem—the insignia of the Secret Order of Perpetual Male Superiority.

Gasps.

“The war against literacy is NOT over!” he bellowed. “You thought you could rewrite history? Thought you could dismantle our great tradition of well-dressed incompetence? THINK AGAIN!”

Then—BOOM!—from the depths of the chamber floor, a hidden trapdoor swung open, revealing The Shadow Council, an underground league of scholars who had secretly orchestrated centuries of illogical restrictions against women’s education.

"You really thought we'd let this happen?!" snarled Sir Tobias Tragiclaw, cracking his knuckles.

But just as the Alphabet Revolutionaries prepared to face their final battle, the most UNEXPECTED betrayal rocked the chamber—

Lord Bartholomew’s OWN SECRETARY stepped forward, dramatically tearing off his cloak to reveal—

A second declaration.

One that had been secretly drafted for YEARS…

A prophecy.

It foretold of a day when the quills of women would carve history into stone, when gender restrictions would burn, and when those who feared literacy would be forced to read their own downfall.

Sir Tobias screamed. Bartholomew collapsed—again.

Then—EXPLOSION!

Lady Persephone activated a secret lever, revealing hidden passageways filled with libraries, each book a direct contradiction to centuries of ignorance.

Madam Theodore flung open a window, revealing a battalion of gender-fluid scholars, clutching pens like swords, ready to write their names into history.

Alexandra the Alphabet Bandit climbed atop a desk, bellowing THE FINAL WORDS that would seal the fate of history forever:

"TODAY, LITERACY REIGNS."

And just like that, the last barrier crumbled—the revolution succeeded—and education became unstoppable.

As the dust settled, Lady Persephone turned to Madam Theodore and asked,
“Do you think Bartholomew will recover?”

Madam Theodore took a long sip of tea. “Not from this.”
The Peach Paradox

A Study in Sandwiches, Silliness, and Illusions of Completion

How Two Men in a Park Unraveled the Secrets of Time, Food, and the Persistent Betrayal of Perishable Fruit

Bernie sat on the worn wooden bench, watching the leaves swirl in restless currents. The park had always been there, just as the conversations had always happened. Somewhere between the past and the present, a truth lingered—waiting to be uncovered, waiting to be understood.

Harold adjusted his hat, a gesture practiced over decades. "You ever think about food before sandwiches?" he asked, eyes scanning the horizon as though it held the answer.

Bernie chuckled. "Food before sandwiches was suffering, Harold. People held greasy slabs of meat in their hands, the juice dripping, the bread doubling as furniture."

Harold nodded, the wisdom settling between them like the gentle hum of the wind. "Bread was a chair," he mused. "Until someone sat too long and got hungry."

Bernie sighed. "That was the moment humanity changed. The sandwich was born, and with it, the illusion of order."

Harold looked at him. "But not everything follows the rules. Some things refuse to be contained."

Bernie turned, understanding reflected in his eyes. "The peach."

Harold leaned forward, lowering his voice as if the peach could hear them. "It arrives, looking whole, promising sweetness. But time works against it. No expiration date, no certainty—only the lurking decay beneath the surface."

Bernie shook his head. "People convince themselves it’s still good. They always do. Because admitting otherwise means facing the truth: that food is fleeting, that structure is a myth, that no matter how much order we create, time will undo it."

Harold sighed, staring out into the endless green of the park. "It’s not just food. It’s everything. We keep telling ourselves that after this task, this moment, this work—things will finally make sense. But it never happens. The next thing arrives, and suddenly, the promise of completion dissolves like a fruit past its prime."

Bernie smiled, the wisdom settling between them. "And yet, we sit here, Harold. Talking, thinking, waiting for the world to reveal itself."

Harold laughed. "Because that’s all we can do, Bernie. We listen, we watch, and sometimes, when the timing is right, we take a bite—before the peach turns to mush."

The wind carried their laughter, scattering it like fallen leaves, as they sat together in the waiting moment—two men, searching for meaning, knowing it had always been there.

The Great Pre-Sandwich Catastrophe

There was a time when eating was an act of pure chaos. Before sandwiches, people gripped their meals with both hands, desperately trying to keep things together, while grease pooled in their palms and dignity dripped onto their tunics. It was a primitive era, devoid of containment, devoid of order. Bread, when available, wasn’t always used for food. Sometimes, it was a chair.

The pursuit of a neater way to consume sustenance led to a series of misguided experiments. Some tried stacking food between two large rocks, which provided a sturdy structure but left most people toothless by the age of twenty-four. Others attempted to sip soup directly from their cupped hands, only to discover that third-degree burns added a certain spice to the meal. And then there were those who, in utter desperation, simply licked cheese off the nearest available surface, a method historians would later describe as “bold but deeply flawed.”

Then, one day, sandwiches arrived. Civilization rejoiced. Food had finally been conquered. Layers were stacked, edges defined, ingredients secured in harmony. No longer did people have to clutch their meals with the enthusiasm of a starving raccoon. At last, the human race could eat without looking like escaped zoo animals.

But even with the triumph of the sandwich, some primal instincts refused to die. Deep inside every person is the lingering impulse to eat incorrectly—to grab food recklessly, ignoring centuries of progress, and sink teeth into chaos simply because they can.

And maybe that’s why a peach feels like a cruel trick. No expiration date you can argue with, no protective wrapping, no promise of containment. Just a ticking time bomb of soft fruit, daring you to eat it before time turns it into a tragic puddle of regret.

Maybe that’s why parents insist, against all logic, that it’s "still good." Not because they’re delusional. Not because they refuse to accept decay.

But because food is fleeting.

And completion is a myth we've been chasing forever—like government efficiency, competent airline boarding procedures, or the idea that anyone truly understands how taxes work.

The World Before the Sandwich

Hunger existed before sandwiches, but it was desperate. Messy. People held food in their hands like fools—greasy, precarious, always one wrong movement away from disaster. Bread, when available, wasn’t always eaten. Sometimes it was a chair.

Survival required improvisation. Some stacked their meals between stones, hoping their teeth were strong enough to endure history’s bad decisions. Others simply folded food into their own regret and ate whatever remained intact.

Then, sandwiches arrived. And with them, the illusion of control. Layers stacked, edges defined, ingredients secured in harmony. For the first time, food held itself together.

But still, the past lingers. Deep in human instinct is the impulse to eat recklessly—to defy the symmetry of progress. We grab things without thinking, ignoring generations of innovation, biting into chaos with full commitment.

And maybe that’s why a peach feels like a betrayal. No expiration date you can argue with, no protective wrapping, no promise of containment. Just the flesh of something fleeting, daring you to eat it before time takes it back.

Maybe that’s why parents insist, against all evidence, that it’s "still good." Not because they refuse to accept decay.

But because food is temporary.

And completion is a myth we've been chasing forever.

The Alphabet’s Awakening

Persephone stood beneath the great celestial dome, where constellations whispered stories only the most enlightened could hear. She had always known the universe spoke in symbols—letters carved into the very fabric of existence. But for too long, these markings had been hidden, their meaning locked away by those who feared their power.

She gazed upon the Library of the Unwritten, where books pulsed like living beings, waiting for hands to open them. The rulers of the land had encased knowledge in thick iron, believing that words, once freed, would reshape the stars themselves.

A voice echoed from the shadows. “The universe favors the seekers.”

It was Theodore, the Traveler of Many Names, clad in robes woven from forgotten stories. “Language is the key,” he whispered. “Every word is a spell. Every letter is a spark. Those who read, rewrite the heavens.”

Persephone turned to Alexandra the Alphabet Bandit, who had spent lifetimes slipping forbidden letters into the palms of those deemed unworthy. “There is a prophecy,” Alexandra murmured, tracing an ancient rune with their fingertip. “When the first word is spoken by one once silenced, the world will tremble.”

And so, Persephone spoke. A single letter.

The walls of the city shuddered.

From the depths of the Shadow Council, Bartholomew the Unyielding felt the tremor in his bones. “The balance is shifting,” he hissed. “If the alphabet is unleashed, the order will fall.”

Pages burst into flame, their ashes swirling into the air like the spirits of forgotten voices. The stars above realigned, shifting to reveal inscriptions unseen for centuries. The books that had waited in silence began to hum, their power growing, their pages glowing with cosmic light.

Regina Who-Used-To-Be-Reginald held up a relic—an ancient quill once wielded by a ruler who had dared to challenge fate. “This,” Regina declared, “is the final piece.”

Persephone, Theodore, and Alexandra stood before the gates of knowledge. With one stroke, the quill etched a new destiny into the fabric of time.

The iron bindings cracked. The books awoke.

The prophecy was fulfilled.

And across the sky, the stars rearranged themselves into a single, undeniable message:

“LITERACY SHALL SET THEM FREE.”

Keeper of Echoes

( Now with 104% More Baffling Shenanigans! )

A Story of Lost Voices, Existential Confusion, and the Unfortunate Reality of Rogue Pigeons

The Call to Remember (And Also a Minor Dispute Over Tea)

The traveler was searching—for meaning, for lost voices, and, if possible, for a sandwich that wouldn’t immediately be stolen by an opportunistic pigeon.

But before he could even begin his quest, a bureaucratic wizard in a mismatched robe appeared out of thin air, holding a clipboard made entirely of disappointment.

"Halt!" the wizard bellowed. "You must pass the Trial of Inconvenient Questions before proceeding!"

The traveler sighed deeply.

"Fine," he said.

The wizard adjusted his spectacles of unnecessary authority.

"What is your name?"

"Traveler."

"What is your quest?"

"To find lost memories and possibly a snack."

"What is the most emotionally devastating thing to ever happen to humanity?"

The traveler blinked.

"Uh—loss? The inevitability of time? The fragile nature of existence?"

The wizard frowned.

"Incorrect. It was when they discontinued the McRib."

The traveler side-stepped him and moved forward.

The City of Perfect Sound (And One Unnecessarily Polished Bureaucrat)

The traveler arrived at a city where every sound had been engineered into absolute perfection.

Nothing was off-key.
Nothing was unpredictable.
Nothing resembled a genuine human emotion.

"Welcome!" announced a Smug Artificially Enhanced Man, adjusting his suspiciously pristine tie. "We have streamlined the experience of sound! No discord, no sorrow, and certainly no rebellious folk songs about angry geese!"

"But where’s the soul?" the traveler asked.

The Smug Man activated his rehearsed corporate expression and replied, "Soul is unnecessary. We have optimized all emotional responses into three categories: mild enthusiasm, neutral engagement, and the ability to sit through an entire insurance seminar without feeling anything at all."

The traveler was deeply unsettled.

"But struggle is part of meaning," he argued.

"Struggle is disorder," the Smug Man corrected. "And disorder ruins the aesthetic."

But before the traveler could respond, he heard it—something beneath the carefully curated silence.

A note.
A single flawed melody.
Something refusing to be erased.

"Oh no!" the Smug Man gasped. "Someone is feeling unauthorized emotions!"

The traveler sprinted toward the sound.

The Keeper of Echoes (And the Great Pigeon Betrayal of Now and When)

Beyond the city, in a valley where lost voices clung to the wind, the traveler found the Keeper of Echoes—a mysterious figure woven from forgotten sound.

"Music is memory," the Keeper whispered. "And memory survives only if someone chooses to remember."

The traveler nodded solemnly.

Then, a pigeon landed on his shoulder.

The traveler froze.

"Not you again," he muttered.

The pigeon slowly tilted its head, like a creature contemplating both existential dread and its next sandwich heist.

"What if I choose to remember something completely ridiculous?" the traveler asked the Keeper.

"Like what?"

"That the invention of breakfast burritos was mankind’s single greatest achievement?"

The Keeper shrugged.

"Honestly, I’d support that."

The traveler blinked.

"Wait—what about the fact that nearly all giraffes have committed zero felonies?"

The Keeper sighed.

"Look, I don’t know what’s happening anymore, but sure. That too."

The pigeon stole the traveler’s snack and vanished.

The War Against Silence (Now With a Surprising Number of Interpretive Cartwheels)

Before the traveler could act, the shadow of Algorithmic Perfection appeared.

It had no face, no voice—just a vague aura of corporate interference and a suspicious smell of printer ink.

"You cannot run from silence," the Keeper murmured. "You must fight it—with song."

But the traveler had zero musical talent, so instead he executed a flawless series of deeply unnecessary cartwheels.

"What are you doing?" the Keeper asked.

"Expressing rebellion through acrobatics!" the traveler shouted.

And suddenly—the world trembled.

The statues built to erase memory began to crack.
A whisper rose—then a note—then a wave of sound.

Lost voices returned, not because they had been archived, but because someone had chosen to feel them again.

The Algorithmic Shadow panicked.

"Deploy additional optimization!" it screamed. "Eliminate all trombones!"

But the music had already spread—messy, chaotic, human.

The traveler didn’t need a perfect victory.
He didn’t need an empire.

He just needed proof that imperfection deserved to exist.

So he stood there—dusty, slightly bruised from his own cartwheeling, and moderately betrayed by a pigeon—and whispered:

"To remember is not to store. To remember is to carry something forward."

Somewhere, a seagull dive-bombed an unsuspecting tourist.

The traveler sighed.

Apparently, chaos was eternal.
