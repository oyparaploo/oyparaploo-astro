Chapter Three ~ Salt in the Cracks

Words / 2025 Variety

The sea made its own kind of breath. It didn’t sigh the way mountains did, or murmur like rivers. Here in Collioure, it exhaled in endless repetitions: shhh… slap. Shhh… slap. Tide on stone, lips against teeth, as if water itself rehearsed a confession it never finished.

Troll Tearsen stood at the edge of the breakwater, the last light peeling gold from her hair. She let the sound in. Beneath it, she could almost hear something else: small percussion, like teeth chattering in a jaw too tired to close.

Pebbles rolled with each wave, lifted and dropped back into the drain-cut crevice of stone. Clack… clack-clack.

“Heartbeat,” she whispered.

Libaux Torrini was crouched a meter away, gloved hands braced on damp stone. His head tilted, eyes narrowed, listening. “Or teeth grinding.”

Troll glanced at him. He wasn’t joking.

The sound filled the gap between them, faint yet insistent. Pebbles striking stone. Nature making rhythm for the dead.

---

The painter was waiting again, just as he had the night before. Same stool. Different sketch. Circles upon circles, charcoal rasping like sandpaper dragged across bone.

“You came back,” he said without lifting his head. His voice rasped too, like the charcoal itself.

Troll crouched, trying to peer at his drawing. Circles layered into constellations. Eyes without lids.

“We need more than circles.”

“I told you. Hands. Folding. Always folding. Not paper. Something heavier.” His charcoal pressed harder, a dark wheel sinking the page.

“Marbles?”

He didn’t stop. “Eyes.”

The rasp grew louder, filling the silence between tide and gull. Scrrrr. Scrrrr.

Libaux shifted behind her, boot scraping stone. “You get poetry. I get migraines.”

Troll ignored him. The painter finally looked up. His pupils were inked large from the dusk. “You’re too late.”

Before she could ask, a gust of wind barreled down the quay. Tramontane. Dry, hard, merciless. Shutters rattled with metallic whines, tarps thudded against their ropes like bass drums. Napkins scattered from a table and slapped along cobbles with paper applause.

Libaux braced himself, coat flaring. “Canvas as subwoofer.”

Troll smirked despite herself. “Poet again.”

“Only in adverse weather.”

The painter didn’t laugh. He turned back to his circles, the charcoal’s rasp folding itself back into the wind’s percussion.

---

Inside the municipal office, sound shrank. No harbor breath, no gull laughter. Just mechanics.

A fluorescent tube buzzed overhead, steady and nasal. A drinks cooler ticked as it expanded and contracted with the chill. Somewhere in the back, a fan whirred in uneven cycles, sighing like someone out of breath.

AV technician Luc clicked through his files, messy curls haloed by blue screen glow. He tapped a spacebar. “Security camera, Bar du Phare. Two nights ago.”

Speakers coughed static, then flattened the harbor into tape. Voices, distant plates clinking. Halyards pinging tinny against masts.

Then: clink… roll… clink.

Contained. Not scattered. Inside cloth.

Luc boosted mids, shaved hiss. The sound sharpened. A faint exhale surfaced—hhht—a caught breath, as though someone paused to measure how close he was to the edge.

Troll’s stomach tightened.

The broom fell in the hall—BANG—and the wooden resonance bloomed.

Everyone jumped.

Luc chuckled nervously. “Our janitor’s auditioning for percussion.”

Troll didn’t smile. Her pulse was already a drum.

---

Aïcha, service liaison, met them in a glassed-in office where voices ricocheted and came back thin. She had a body like wire and eyes with edges.

“Miller Corbal flagged three caseload errors,” she said. “Cross-border placements. Files missing signatures. A child moved; no one recorded where. She said she’d bring me proof. She never did.”

“Because she vanished,” Troll said.

“Because she died,” Libaux corrected, blunt as stone.

“Because she was silenced,” Aïcha replied, her voice amplified by the office glass, stronger than it should have been.

Troll scanned the list Aïcha slid across. Departmental heads, ministry staffers, consultants. One name snagged her breath: Fredda Stremler.

Libaux leaned over her shoulder. “That’s our shadow.”

Troll circled it, pen biting paper. The sound hissed like a blade through cloth.

---

Night dropped fully by the time they left.

Alleys accepted them like throats. Sound doubled back on itself—tick-ckt-ckt-ckt—flutter echoes of their own footsteps, mocking, chasing.

Troll slowed. The echo slowed too, then caught, like someone else had missed a step. She froze. Libaux turned, eyes narrowing. Nothing but stone, shutters, the faint light of a single window.

“Measured,” she said under her breath.

They moved faster.

---

The bar was tiled bright, mirrors doubling every bottle, making reflections seem more numerous than patrons. The espresso machine hissed like a small steam engine; bracelets chimed as a woman gestured mid-laughter.

Troll and Libaux took seats where they could see the door.

At the corner table, a man sat with a canvas bag by his feet. The bag shifted. Inside: tch… tch.

Troll’s pulse answered. She ordered coffee. Cups kissed saucers: tuk. Spoon chimed once, then was drowned in foam.

She placed her cup on the man’s table edge. Porcelain gave a defiant little vowel.

He looked up. Smile too precise. Eyes empty.

“Bonsoir.”

The espresso machine sighed: pshhhhh. The door opened, wind scribbling.

“Police,” Libaux said, voice dry, clipped, no echo.

The man’s hand tightened. Canvas creaked. Glass trembled.

Troll held her breath.

Libaux bent, pulled the bag open.

Not marbles. Fishing floats. Heavier, duller. Their collisions gave a low tok… tok, woody and patient.

The man smiled wider. “Vintage. There’s a shop near the church.”

He paid with coins that clattered honest, and left. Floats knocking together like old men’s shoulders.

The bar exhaled, espresso hiss cooling.

Troll sipped, hand steady. “Souvenirs.”

Libaux shook his head. “Not him.”

“Not tonight.”

---

They walked back along the harbor.

The tide had crept higher, black water glossy with lights. Police tape fluttered, forgotten.

On the breakwater, a single marble remained. Not in a tray. Not in a bag.

The cracked one.

The wind shifted. The marble rolled, kissed stone: tik.

Small. Almost nothing. Yet the sound carried—thin, long, like a secret forcing itself into the night.

Troll stopped. She thought she heard it again—an echo, not from stone but from herself.

Libaux walked on, unaware.

The marble lay still. Waiting.
