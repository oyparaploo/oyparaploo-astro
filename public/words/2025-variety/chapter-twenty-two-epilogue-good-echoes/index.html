Chapter Twenty-Two ~ Epilogue ~ Good Echoes

Words / 2025 Variety

Spring did not announce itself in the Grosses Walsertal; it cleared its throat and went about repairs. Snow loosened from slopes with a soft whuff where sun found it. The brook resumed its argument with stones without looking up to see who had won. A fence post creaked once as thaw persuaded fiber back into the world.

Troll Tearsen walked the lane to the Fontanella shelter with the box in her arms. It was not heavy. Things that carry a person aren’t. The blue tape across the lid said T. Tearsen – marbles in Miyake Coscia’s square hand. The cardboard gave off the clean, faint smell of a hospital hallway that had been kind to breath.

The shelter looked smaller than memory and kinder than the morning it had asked to be a stage. The glass still wore algae where no one found a reason to scrape it off. A new timetable fluttered, not because wind wanted it, but because paper does.

Helene Klausmun was waiting by the bench with two cups of coffee that had agreed to be hot and were. “You’re late,” she said, in the tone of someone who knows that grief is a clock, not a calendar.

Troll set the box on the seat. The lid came away with the quick hiss of tape giving up. Inside, the jar: round, plain, patient. Marbles waited within—clear, red, a green with a white coil, blue seeded with bubbles, white with a small spiral like a whisper. She lifted the jar free. It was heavier than the box had seemed.

“Do you want to keep it at the house?” Helene asked.

“No,” Troll said. “I want the valley to keep it. It remembered for me.”

Helene nodded, and they set the jar on the little shelf beside the curling timetables, where people wait and lose track of whether they are waiting for the bus or the day to decide something. The jar fit there as if the glass had been blown with this ledge in mind.

Troll unscrewed the lid and poured two marbles into her palm. The sound they made—not clatter, just tik against tik—rode the shelter’s small acoustics and went outside to see if anyone needed it. She rolled one across the bench. It kissed a rough bit of plastic and stopped, content with modesty. No halo. No choreography.

They drank the coffee without speaking. When Troll finished, she said her mother’s name. The shelter let it be. The valley returned it once, from the fence, and then again, narrower, from the path. Neither was a miracle. Both were enough.

“Good echoes,” Helene said.

“Good echoes,” Troll agreed.

They left the jar where waiting becomes a room. “If someone steals it?” Helene asked, almost smiling.

“Then it goes to another room that needs it,” Troll said. “Objects are disloyal in the right ways.”

---

By the time the train set them down in Collioure, summer had already been rehearsed twice. Nets dried along the quay like sheets; halyards pinged their metal syllables; gulls practiced laughter they would use whether anyone deserved it or not.

Aïcha met them with paperwork and bread and the satisfaction of a woman who had taught a mayor the difference between law and lawlike. “RIVAJE paid its fines,” she said. “Under a different name. Their subsidiary dissolved the week after the forum. Three directors resigned before noon and joined four other boards by dinner.”

“Metamorphosis,” Libaux Torrini said. He leaned on the sea wall as if he trusted the wall to remember what it had been for.

“Cuttlefish,” Aïcha said. “Ink and exit.” She punched a number into her phone and waved them toward the breakwater. “Luc will play you the part where the sea apologizes.”

Luc had found a perfect angle for his speakers and his gloom. He set them on the balcony, pressed a key, and let the harbor step out of itself and onto the air: halyards, gulls, the tide’s shhh… slap. Beneath it, no nineteen cycles leaning on skulls. No engineered mmm fastening itself to breath. Just water making the sound it evolved because there were stones and wind.

“Your braces?” Libaux asked.

“Removed,” Aïcha said. “Sold at auction as ‘art.’ The tourist board insisted.”

“Art is harmless once it’s bolted to a wall,” Luc said. He lowered his voice two notes. “Mostly harmless.”

They walked to the old shop in the lane. The woman who made marbles and told the truth had closed her door from the inside and opened it again, which is one of the ways to become a person. On her bench: spheres cooling, seeds deliberate as constellations.

He—Stremler—had not come back. But a simple envelope had. Two words in a hand that had learned neatness for survival: no more. She had filled a tray with marbles anyway and labeled it for children.

“Do you hear anything when you roll them?” Troll asked.

“Only wood,” the woman said. “It is a good sound.”

They bought five—one for the jar, one for Meuller’s lab, one for Luc’s desk, and two with no address at all. When they left, Aïcha said, “We are not finished.” She meant the south, the auditors, the quiet ways power teaches itself the right pitch. “But it’s less noisy.”

They stood on the breakwater while the sea practiced not needing to be a metaphor. Troll rolled a marble along the top. It found a thin groove, a little tik with a chip at the end, the kind of imperfection that makes families out of people.

“Good echoes,” Libaux said, trying the phrase out like he might someday trust it.

“Good echoes,” Troll returned.

---

Paris had moved two centimeters since they left it and claimed it had shifted a district. The club in Pigalle was not a club anymore; it had become an early bistro with too much hope and not enough ventilation. The liaison had listed his apartment for sale. A pen name with a philanthropist’s consonants had quietly endowed a think tank across town. The microphones were where they had always been, which improved no one’s life but kept a schedule.

Libaux and Troll sat under an awning where rain performed competence, and listened to the kind of talk that believes in yesterday and wants to lease tomorrow. A pigeon cocked its head at them like a mechanic sizing up an engine and then made a noise pigeons make that doesn’t translate well.

“Uma?” Libaux asked.

Troll showed him the message: Your enemies are louder than your friends. But your friends are the ones who listen. Do not write to me. There was a smile in the punctuation that had taken her three drafts to remove and failed.

Mage Meuller sent a spectrogram and a sentence: The silence in your last valley capture isn’t flat anymore. It has texture. That’s how rooms heal. Underneath, a small graph curled upward like a fragile fern.

They paid the bill and walked along a street that had lied to emperors and students without showing preference. An organ grinder clanked into tune by accident and found a smile the size of a coin. Troll didn’t drop one. She let the music make the case for itself.

---

A bench in Vienna had decided to belong to birds. The hall that had trained silence had retuned to carry human umbrage better. In a foyer where coats never felt at home, a plaque had been mounted, a sentence about “incidents” that had made policy think. It was written in four languages that agreed to disagree.

Troll sat in the back row of a small side room and listened to a young translator hesitate at a word that had no honest equivalent, then choose one anyway. She knew the shape of that hesitation. It fit the palm.

A man passed them in the corridor who might have been Stremler for a fraction of a second: groomed, courteous, shoes with correct opinions. He was not. He turned left instead of right. He didn’t look back. The building had his gait in its muscle memory and mistook him for its former occupant. Buildings make those errors. People, too.

“Do we miss him?” Libaux asked later, near the canal where the city keeps its mirror.

“We miss having a target,” Troll said. “That’s not the same thing.”

“He’ll resurface,” Libaux said.

“So will rooms,” Troll answered.

They stood, and the tram chimed with that careful Vienna chime, and no one fell into the river without permission.

---

When Troll returned to the valley at last, the jar was still in the shelter. Someone had tucked a note behind it: for the ones who wait. One marble had been replaced by two cheaper ones with chips, the kind of economics children understand without agreeing to.

She carried the jar up to the chapel for a while, the way one carries an instrument to a room that will remember it without needing to own it. She set it on the rail and opened it, and the marbles looked back like small, willing planets. The bell breathed once although no one touched it. Sun made itself into quiet geometry on stone.

She took out the blue one seeded with bubbles, rolled it to the notch, and let it stop. In the stillness, the tik had a tail so soft you had to lean in to be sure the world hadn’t invented it for you. The room accepted the sound and returned it, unhurried.

Miyake’s last sentence lived in that room the way pages live in a book that has learned to hold itself open. Keep one true, and the others will follow.

Troll went home and put the jar on her own shelf under the window. The label went into the recycling and then into a drawer and then back onto the jar because labels have their reasons too. She read: T. Tearsen – marbles. It belonged there. It also belonged on the bus shelter shelf. Rooms learn to share.

Libaux came by exactly when he had not said he would. He stood in the doorway, hands empty because men like him don’t like to bring bouquets to quiets. “We have a list,” he said. “Places that sound wrong.”

“We always will,” she said.

He nodded, then leaned to the jar. He didn’t take a marble. He merely tipped it a centimeter and listened to the little planets roll and find each other: tik… tik and then still.

“Good echoes,” he said, and was surprised to mean it.

“Good echoes,” she returned, and meant it too.

Outside, the valley tried rain and changed its mind. The brook found a new rock it loved and included it in the story. Far off, a cowbell gave a note precisely because no one asked. In the shelter down the lane, a child rolled a marble along the bench to make time less rude, and the bench answered with a small sound that didn’t pretend to be anything else.

Troll lifted the cracked marble from the jar and set it in her palm. It warmed there or pretended to—the honest pretense of objects. She closed her hand and opened the window. The room took a breath. So did she.

She said her mother’s name one more time, not to summon anything, only to remember. The house let it pass into the day and gave a little of it back, timed to the hiccup of wind where it met the corner of the frame.

The echo was not larger or brighter. It was just true.

That was enough.
