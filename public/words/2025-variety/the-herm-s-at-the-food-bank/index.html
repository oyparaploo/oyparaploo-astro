The Hermès at the Food Bank

← Words / 2025 Variety

When the wealthy fall from grace, they fall in designer clothes, carrying the arithmetic of their former lives: a $247 monthly storage unit holding half a million in assets they can't access, an $8,000 coat that's the only warm thing they own, the specific weight of shame that comes from accepting charity while wearing couture.

Marlène knew they were staring at the coat. Hermès. Camel hair. Three years old but still pristine because she'd known how to care for things back when she had things to care for.

The food bank volunteer—nineteen, maybe twenty, community service by the look of his ankle monitor—held out the box. Pasta. Canned corn. White bread.

"That coat's worth more than my car," someone behind her said. Not hostile. Just observing.

"Probably," Marlène said. She took the box.

Outside, November. Cleveland cold. She started walking back to the shelter and something happened to her body. She started moving strange. Like she was falling but catching herself. Falling again. Catching. Moving forward but also down.

A man watched from his doorway. "You okay?"

"I'm—" She didn't know what she was. Kept moving that way. Couldn't stop.

At the shelter—women only, $10 a night when you had it—her bunkmate Patricia watched her come in.

"Saw you on Fifth Street. That movement you were doing."

"I don't know what happened."

"You were showing something. The way people fall but keep going."

Patricia was sixty-three. Called herself an artist but really she just tied things together from garbage. Wire to fabric. String to plastic. Had this theory about knots and loops that Marlène didn't entirely follow.

"When you can't undo something, that's a knot," Patricia said, working on something made of coat hangers. "When you keep coming back different, that's a loop."

"What about when you can't explain?"

Patricia held up three fingers. Made dots in the air. "Ellipses."

The next morning Marlène walked to the library. The strange movement happened again. Like her body was demonstrating something her mouth couldn't say. A woman in fur stopped her.

"Are you having a seizure?"

"No. I'm—" Marlène did it again. The fall-catch-forward thing. "This is just how I move now."

"Since when?"

"Since yesterday. Since I wore Hermès to the food bank. Since—" She stopped.

The woman stared. Marlène recognized the look. Pre-fall. When you can see it coming but can't stop it.

"My husband's under investigation," the woman said quietly. "FBI. I found out yesterday."

"You'll fall too."

"Then what?"

Marlène did the movement again. Showed her. The woman tried it. Looked insane. Felt worse.

"Why does it help?" the woman asked.

"I don't know that it does."

But they kept doing it. Walking through Public Square, falling and catching, falling and catching. People stared. Some laughed. Some looked away.

By the third day, others had joined. Marlène didn't invite them. They just appeared. A banker she recognized from Richard's trial. A doctor from the hospital where she'd had good insurance. A teacher from the private school she couldn't afford anymore.

All of them moving through downtown like broken marionettes. Or dancers. Or something between.

"This is disturbing," someone said to the police.

But what was the crime? Walking weird? Falling repeatedly? Wearing designer clothes while poor?

A reporter came. Young woman with clean shoes.

"What's your message?"

"I don't have one."

"But the movement—what does it mean?"

Marlène did the fall-catch thing. The reporter stepped back.

"I used to have a financial advisor," Marlène said. "A personal trainer. A therapist. They all told me how to move through the world. Now I move like this."

"By choice?"

"Is it?"

The reporter left. The segment never aired. But people kept joining the walking. The falling. Whatever it was.

Patricia started coming too. Brought wire and string. Started tying things to people as they walked. Knots where they couldn't change. Loops where they kept returning. Gaps where they couldn't explain.

"This is art," Patricia said.

"This is survival," Marlène corrected.

"Same thing."

A kid joined them. Eight, maybe nine. From the foster system by the look of her garbage bag of belongings.

"Can I?" she asked.

"Can you what?"

"Walk like that. Like you're falling."

"Everyone's falling," Marlène said. "We're just not hiding it."

The kid tried. Fell different than the adults. More bounce. Less resignation.

"What's with the coat?" the kid asked.

"It's all I have left that's warm."

"But people hate you for it."

"Yeah."

"So why—"

"Because it's mine. Because I bought it with money that turned out to be stolen but I didn't know that then. Because selling it would get me one month's rent and then what? Because—" She stopped. Made dots in the air like Patricia had taught her. Ellipses for the unsaid.

They walked through winter. The falling became more elaborate. Some people added spins. Some crawled. Some just stood still, falling in place.

Richard found her one day. Her ex. Still free on bail.

"This is humiliating," he said.

"For who?"

"You're wearing my coat."

"The court said it was mine."

"While doing this... performance."

She fell toward him. Caught herself. Pushed forward. Fell again.

"I'm just walking, Richard. The way people walk when they've lost everything but can't stop moving."

He left. She kept falling. Kept walking. The coat got dirty. She didn't clean it. The dirt was part of it now. The story of falling.

Spring came. Someone offered to buy the coat. A collector. Vintage Hermès.

"Three thousand," they said.

"No."

"Five."

"It's not for sale."

"You're homeless."

"I'm in transitional housing. And the coat isn't for sale."

She kept wearing it. Even when it was too warm. Even when she got a job—minimum wage, but still. Even when people stopped staring because they'd gotten used to the woman who walked like she was falling.

Patricia asked her one night why she kept doing it. The walking. The falling.

"I don't know how to stop."

"Do you want to?"

Marlène thought about it. About moving normal again. About hiding the fall. About pretending.

"No."

"Then don't."

So she didn't. Kept falling through the city. Others kept joining. Or stopping. Or starting again. No organization. No message. Just people moving honestly through space, showing what it looked like to fall and continue.

The coat got more ragged. The falling got more natural. Or the natural got more like falling. Hard to tell the difference anymore.

Sometimes Marlène remembered the person who'd worn this coat new. Who'd walked straight. Who'd believed the numbers would always add up.

That person had fallen too. Just hadn't known it yet.

Now everyone could see. The coat. The fall. The forward motion through downward space.

It wasn't beautiful. It wasn't art. It wasn't protest.

It was just Tuesday. In Cleveland. In November or May or whenever. Walking to the food bank or the job or nowhere.

Falling.

Still falling.

Still moving forward.

Still wearing the coat.

The arithmetic of falling is simple: what you were worth divided by what you're worth now equals the distance you travel with each step, visible in the angle of your body as it moves through space, carrying designer remnants of the person you were, who was always falling but didn't know it yet.
