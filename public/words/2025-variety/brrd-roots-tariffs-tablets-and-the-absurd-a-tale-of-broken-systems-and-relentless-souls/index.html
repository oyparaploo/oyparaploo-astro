Tariffs, Tablets, and the Absurd

A Tale of Broken Systems and Relentless Souls

← Words / 2025 Variety

[ Navigating the Surreal Theater of the Trade War With
Humor, Humanity, and Hope Amid Chaos ]

The winds of change didn’t so much blow as they stumbled—drunken and confused. Across the globe, a whisper—a faint, glittering possibility—trickled into the ears of a weary populace. Smartphones, tablets, electronic doodads, all spared from tariffs? People clung to the hope with the kind of desperation reserved for lifeboats, miracle diets, and bad relationships. But alas, hope doesn’t pay tariffs. “No, no, no, no,” the U.S. Commerce Secretary said, his voice as decisive as a shoe stomping on a bug. “We are not taking tariffs off these goods.”

He paused, smiled as though savoring his next line, and delivered it with Shakespearean flourish: “These products will fall within the semiconductor sectoral tariffs that are forthcoming.” Somewhere in the distance, a horse neighed, though no one had seen a horse in years.

The pronouncement unraveled reality with the elegance of a sweater caught on barbed wire. Towns transformed overnight. Bureaucrats multiplied, swarming like locusts but armed with forms marked “urgent” and clipboards that screamed authority. In one Midwestern town, the mayor implemented a new policy requiring residents to file a 73-page form just to use the bathroom. “This is for your own good,” he explained, through a megaphone, from atop a stack of redundant filing cabinets.

In his once-cozy, now-claustrophobic living room, Tom, former factory worker, current existential crisis enthusiast, was busy attempting to fix the cracked tablet that had become a symbol of both familial love and the futility of existence. The device hissed, sparked, and—at one particularly dramatic moment—emitted a puff of smoke shaped suspiciously like a middle finger. His children huddled behind him like wary spectators at a wrestling match, offering insights that were both sincere and useless.

“Dad,” his youngest began tentatively, “do we… need the tablet to survive?”

Tom considered this. “No,” he said gravely, “but without it, what do we have? Monopoly?” The kids shuddered. It had been an ugly game night last week, and no one wanted to revisit the incident with the thimble and the flipped board.

Meanwhile, halfway around the world, Mei stood on her factory floor—if you could call a seemingly endless expanse of machinery and misery a “floor.” Conveyor belts screeched and clattered with the enthusiasm of a toddler banging pots together, each one loaded with microchips so unremarkable they seemed to taunt her. Her supervisor, a man whose sole qualification seemed to be his uncanny resemblance to a disapproving walrus, bellowed from a balcony: “PRODUCTIVITY IS HAPPINESS!” No one believed him, least of all the walrus.

Mei, however, had ceased to care about the quotas, the microchips, or even the walrus man’s aggressive mustache. Grief for her father, who had been her one tether to meaning, had turned her into a creature suspended between exhaustion and absurdity. She clutched her jade pendant, her fingertips tracing its smooth curves, and whispered, “If the world’s gonna be insane, I might as well meet it halfway.”

This was how she found herself hosting impromptu workshops on fixing discarded electronics—an act of rebellion so small it was almost adorable. Her co-workers, starved for any semblance of purpose, showed up in droves, though their contributions ranged from genuinely helpful to outright baffling. One particularly enthusiastic individual insisted that humming a specific pitch could “coax the circuits back to life.” It didn’t work, but morale improved when everyone joined in.

Back in the U.S., Tom’s repair efforts culminated in a triumphant moment: the tablet’s screen flickered on, glowing weakly like a firefly with performance anxiety. He grinned, feeling the first stirrings of victory—only to be greeted by a single, stark message on the screen: “FATAL ERROR: HUMANITY NOT FOUND.”

The kids stared. Tom stared. And then, quietly, his oldest began to laugh. It was a dry, strange laugh at first, but it grew, infectious, until even Tom found himself laughing through the lump in his throat. “Well,” he said, wiping his eyes, “at least it’s honest.”

The trade war raged on, a cacophony of absurdities and tragicomic interludes. Somewhere, bureaucrats staged interpretive dances titled “Paperwork Is Freedom.” In the heartland, towns rebranded themselves with slogans like, “We Make Tariffs Fun!” Meanwhile, Mei and Tom, separated by oceans yet connected by the same relentless absurdity, clung to the scraps of humanity they could find.

Mei told her co-workers, “We can’t fix the world, but we can fix this,” as she held up a resurrected circuit board like an ancient artifact. Tom told his kids, “It doesn’t matter if it’s broken—we’re not.”

And somewhere, in a moment both heartbreaking and ridiculous, a crow perched on a broken streetlight, clutching a sign that read, “We Told You So.”
