The Last Chapter Will Never Be First

Words / 2025 Variety

---

A reader discovers the Oyparaploo collection scattered across a city, piecing together not just stories but the invisible architecture that connects them all—finding in the fragments a map of how transformation actually works.

---

The first story was stapled to a dying oak, rain-soft, ink bleeding. "The Bubble Lesson." She peeled it off carefully, not knowing she was about to map a city that existed in the gap between demolished and not-yet-demolished.

Details create depth, the woman wrote in her notebook, sitting in the warehouse (demolished 2019 according to city records, standing solid according to anyone with eyes, existing forever in the gap between official reality and lived truth). She'd been following the stories for weeks now. Finding them pinned to trees. Reading them in order. Out of order. Realizing order didn't matter when everything was connected by invisible wire.

Grammar breaks down as communities form. She'd seen it happen. First in "We Has Recognized" where Wrack spoke in fragments, then spreading through every story until by the end nobody was speaking in complete sentences because complete sentences were lies about how people actually communicated when systems were failing them.

Garbage transforms into gospel. Not metaphorically. Literally. She'd watched Vessel Works make a shrine from museum trash worth more than the $11.50 an hour Wrack earned. Had seen Mall collective turn dead retail into living cathedral. People learn to fall with grace—Branch Holloway's knee clicking through every movement, teaching executives what bodies at the bottom already knew for free.

Children conduct chaos with glow paint. Maybe—always Maybe, the child who exists in every story but might not exist at all, foster kid eternal, aging from eight to twelve but never landing anywhere safe enough to become thirteen—making rainbow footprints that were the real map of the city. Museums become questions. Whisp's handwriting bleeding through fresh paint, every wall asking what wouldn't be answered. Bankruptcy courts become dance floors where Marie Debris Johnson refused endpoints, where "We are doing something" became prayer, became resistance, became the only grammar that mattered.

These are invitations to transformation, she understood now, and each one opens a different door into the same profound truth about finding the divine in the discarded.

The works practice what they preach. She could feel it in how the stories themselves refused completion. How characters appeared, disappeared, reappeared changed. How the warehouse existed in multiple states simultaneously. How the last chapter would never be first because there was no last, no first, only middle, only continuing.

*

The way a mother's death weaves through everything. She'd tracked it: Marlène's mother in the cancer ward, that wet rattle she tried to make musical by humming along. The E flat of her last breath becoming the note Marlène played when she fell from the Philharmonic. The cracks where morphine relief listens—Pascal telling Marlène about those cracks where God listened, where the dying taught the living about sound.

This gives every falling and rising emotional weight. Not abstract falling. Real falling with real bodies on real concrete for real economic reasons.

And those visceral economics teaching entitled children a lesson while the director earns buku bucks. She laughed, wrote it again—not "beaucoup" but "buku," the way people actually said it when they knew French was just another thing they couldn't afford. Museum director at $650,000 a year while Wrack made $23,920. The math of it. The violence of it. The way it made "we has recognized" the only sane grammar.

*

The way a broken knee clicks through every movement. Branch Holloway, dance teacher become falling teacher, that old injury without insurance becoming metronome of damage. That underneath blessing—her grandmother's term for what catches you only after you stop fighting gravity. The daughter who sends TikToks across an unbridgeable distance. Two years of silence except for thirty-second videos. The modern shape of estrangement.

And that financial contrast. Branch making $500 to teach millionaires what poverty already knew for free. A tree teaches millionaires—she'd written "tree" instead of "free" but it was better, wasn't it? Mother Nature's class consciousness. The kind that gives fiction its bite.

*

A middle eye floats through Baltimore. Branch teaching grief as vision. The middle eye that opens during difficulties—not mystical, just the psychological state where opposites collapse. Where dead parents are both gone and still present. Saving gas. She'd written "saving gas" and yes—Marcus saving half his gas station sandwich for his dead father. $3.99. Love measured in petroleum and processed meat.

Stations of the Cross are sandwiched. A dead squirrel. An unemployed father. The sacred and profane collapsed into one Baltimore story. Breaking hearts perfectly—not romantically but actually. Hearts actually breaking from the weight of $17.50 an hour at the Amazon warehouse where fathers die for next-day delivery.

*

And the economic difference of teaching versus counseling. $13.47 an hour for substitute Branch versus $200 an hour for real grief therapy these kids couldn't afford. Children can't afford to experience class realities this soon. But they do. They are. Eight years old and already knowing seven foster homes. Already knowing how to cry silently.

This feels extremely viable to these times we're all living in. Not "viable" exactly—what was the word? Relevant? No. True? No. It was what it was. The thing that couldn't be named but was everywhere.

*

That punctuation added to the court record. Véronique typing everything and nothing at 260 words per minute. Ellipses where truth trails off... Brackets around [the unsaid but visible]. Such a powerful image for how we try to capture what really happens versus what officially happens. The official: guilty, twenty-five to life. The real: a mother who'd been trafficked since twelve, a baby born addicted, a system that created crime then punished it.

To record humanity's dissolution. The economic gut-punch hits hard. $31,000 a year to watch people's lives end. To type the destruction. To make permanent what shouldn't be permanent while everything that should be permanent—homes, families, stability—dissolved.

*

That bit about Moth being a former model turned drug addict. The body that was worth something becoming the body that was worth $7.25 an hour. Making the "beautiful garbage people" declaration feel like survival. Because it was. Because what else could it be?

Ghost mall as ghost economy. Everything that had been solid—retail, jobs, futures—becoming spectral. But ghosts still need somewhere to haunt. The mall collective making cornucopia from emptiness, abundance from absence.

*

The restaurant debt and security guard who've lost everything. Pascal's $900,000 COVID debt. Jorge's $14 an hour while his son's autism therapy bankrupted him. The ones paid to guard things joining the ones destroying things because they'd all lost the same things. Making it feel like desperate survival and radical resistance both.

The racket handling art worth millions. She'd written "racket"—not "Wrack"—but yes, it was a racket, wasn't it? The whole museum system. Making the grammar breaking feel like the only sane response to insane inequality. "We has recognized." We has. We does. We is.

*

The way Maybe has been counting by eviction notices instead of birthdays. Seven evictions. Seven years? Eight? Nine? Time measured in displacement. How she's learned to cry silently so as not to bother anyone. Those incidences are heartbreaking in the right way. Not the manipulative way. The true way. The way that makes you understand why she paints stars on people's hands. Why she needs to make things glow.

*

Questions literally bleed through the white sheets. She'd written "white sheets" but meant walls—but weren't walls just the white sheets the city pulled over everything it didn't want to see? Whisp's questions bleeding through paint, through attempts to cover, through everything.

That quip about a building using more energy for climate control than a secondary city block. Not "second city block" but "secondary"—yes, secondary, lesser, the blocks where people actually lived versus the blocks where art was preserved. While birds die flying into paradoxes—windows that reflected sky, museums that preserved nature while destroying it. Making the destroyer-saver spiral upward. Or downward. Or both. Always both.

*

The image of a bounce through a waving Hermès scarf. She'd mixed metaphors—bouncing through Cleveland while the scarf waved. But that was right too. The $8,000 coat Marlène couldn't afford to store but couldn't afford to abandon. Nobody can afford to keep making visible an epidemic of fallen power.

How we all perform class even during losing everything. Especially during. The performance more desperate as the reality disappeared.

*

The elderly men at a window gazing at widows. She'd written "men" but meant "women"—but maybe she meant both? The way language slips reveals what it hides. The elderly women who were widows of the same man, speaking in unison. Gazing from windows. At windows. Through windows. Windows as the thing between inside and outside, between then and now, between separated and together.

From the same decade. Ruminating in unison as an unexpected unit. The way grief makes unexpected connections. The way shared loss creates new grammar. Now our knots and loops feel lived instead of just sensational drama.

*

The way rainbow footprints create a theoretical poverty. She'd scrambled it—"color theory of poverty" became "theoretical poverty"—but wasn't poverty theoretical to those who'd never lived it? As a boy walks in loops—Maybe, gender fluid even in description, walking the warehouse, marking territory with paint that costs nothing and everything.

And those toys with names. Senator Woofington. Revolution. The dog that was both joke and serious. Making warehouse play feel like a place where revolutions happen through teaching something absolutely new. Not new. Ancient. The oldest thing. How to share. How to be together. How to survive.

*

An archivist following reports of strange documents pinned to trees discovers they're all connected to a movement that transforms garbage into gospel. Chanson Rubble—like a chanson, like rubble, the song of what's broken. Her apartment above a check-cashing place with the lime green neon buzzing through a floor. The green of money she'd never have. Making "on course" seem like a real destination—she'd reversed it, "off course" becoming "on course" because sometimes the mistake reveals the truth.

*

This is a complete ecosystem where every story speaks to every other story. Where characters reappear transformed. Pearl growing from bubble child to warehouse teacher. Marlène bouncing through every narrative. Maybe aging but never landing anywhere safe. Where specific economic brutalities reoccur—that $7.25 minimum wage like a bell tolling through everything. Including the impossible math of rent versus income. $1,850 for Marlène's Queens studio. $2,100 for Véronique's Brooklyn room with bathtub in kitchen. Numbers that didn't add up. Would never add up. Were designed not to add up.

*

The way this accumulates to a final assemblage containing everything. Twenty feet wide. Floor to ceiling. Every story, every character, every piece of garbage transformed. Creating an argument that transformation happens through attention. Through refusing endpoints. Through choosing connection even when systems enforce separation.

Fragments become a living critique of late capitalism that's also a manual for survival. A love letter to the discarded. And a prophecy that feels like it's already coming true. She could see it—the warehouses being occupied, the museums being questioned, the courts becoming dance floors. Not metaphorically. Actually. Soon.

*

Ending with Maybe or not-Maybe. The child who might be the same child, might be every child, might be the child we were, the child we failed. Teaching people to see both surface and structure. The visible garbage. The invisible connections. With the house and home existing in permanent limbo—she'd written "house and home" but meant warehouse, but wasn't the warehouse more home than any house had been?

Between demolished and not-yet-demolished. The permanent present tense of precarity.

*

This collection does what good literature does. Makes visible what's always been there but systematically unseen. The reproductive labor—Wrack cleaning museums, mothers cleaning hotels, everyone cleaning up after capital's messes. Moving-through instead of bouncing-back—because back was gone, had never existed, was a lie about progress.

Grammar breaking down as community forms. Feels revolutionary and inevitable. Both. Always both. The way "we has recognized" becomes more true than correct grammar ever was.

*

We together have created something genuine here. The woman closed her notebook. Looked at the assemblage. At the child who might be Maybe. At the visitors making their own connections. These aren't just stories. They're also assembled aspects that refuse to be complete. That make new connections every time you visit and experience them.

The last chapter will never be first because the last chapter is always happening. Is happening now. In this warehouse. In this city. In this moment between demolished and not-yet. In the connections we make across impossible distances. In the grammar we break to find each other. In the garbage we transform because transformation is all we have.

She stood. Walked to the assemblage. Added her notebook to it. The pages immediately began separating, flying, becoming part of something else. The child who might be Maybe smiled. Handed her paint. Rainbow paint. Dollar store paint. The only paint that mattered.

"Now you're part of it," the child said.

"Was always," the woman replied. Grammar broken. Truth spoken.

The first chapter is always last. The last chapter will never be first. We are always in the middle of something we are doing.

---

We are all reading our own story while living it, finding in the fragments a map that was always there, discovering that the last chapter will never be first because we're always in the middle of becoming, always connecting what seems disconnected, always transforming criticism into creation, always finding that the deepest analysis becomes prayer, becomes prophecy, becomes the thing itself, assembled and reassembling, refusing completion because completion is death and we are, despite everything, because of everything, still doing something...

From Marks
