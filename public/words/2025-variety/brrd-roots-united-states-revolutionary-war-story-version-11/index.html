Brrd Roots ~ United States Revolutionary War Story ~ Version 11

Words / 2025 Variety

The smell of war was something you didn’t forget—damp and sour, like regret had grown roots in the ground and started sprouting. The farmer crouched low in the trench, holding his musket tight like it was the only thing keeping him upright. Around him, the battlefield erupted with noise—cannons booming like giants clearing their throats, men shouting things nobody seemed to understand, and somewhere, softer but persistent, a clink-clink-clink.

“What in God’s name is that?” he muttered, craning his neck, squinting into the haze of mud and smoke. Just down the line, a soldier sat stirring a tin pot, his face calm, almost peaceful—like he’d found a loophole in the chaos and slipped through.

“Morale soup,” the man said, raising the pot like it deserved applause. “Mostly water. Occasionally hope.”

The farmer blinked. “Soup?”

“Would you like a spoon?” the man offered, genuinely. God knew where he’d kept a spoon.

Before the farmer could think of a response, the officer’s whistle screamed loud enough to shatter any clarity he might’ve been chasing. Everyone was up—or trying to be—boots dragging through mud so clingy, it felt personal. The farmer fired his musket out of habit more than anything, not even checking if it hit. Across the field, a British soldier crouched behind a wall, looking just as pale, just as tired.

For a moment, the farmer thought, Does he hate this mud too? Does he want to drop the musket and go home? Does he even know his hat’s on backward?

The whistle screeched again, yanking him back. “Reload!” someone shouted, their voice less commanding, more exhausted. The farmer sighed and did as he was told, because what else could you do?

High in a tree that looked like it had seen too much for its age, a sharpshooter muttered to himself, trying to reason through the task ahead. Below him, the officer paced back and forth, shouting commands about flanking maneuvers and proper posture as though anyone cared. His coat was redder than it needed to be, his buttons gleaming like insults under the sunlight.

The sharpshooter aimed his rifle. His finger hovered on the trigger. But then the thoughts crept in, uninvited. What if he’s got kids waiting for him? What if he writes poetry by candlelight, something soft and lovely, when no one’s looking? What if all this yelling is just his way of drowning out his own doubts?

“Alright, God,” the boy whispered, his voice breaking a little. “If I miss, that’s on You. If I hit… well, I guess You’ve made up Your mind.”

The rifle cracked. The officer fell. It was quick, it was clean, it was heavy. The sharpshooter slumped back against the trunk, letting the rifle slide from his grip. Below him, squirrels scattered without a care, like the moral weight of the moment belonged to someone else.

Valley Forge wasn’t just cold—it was mean. The snow piled high, burying hope under layers of spite, and the cabins leaned against each other like tired drunks. Inside, soldiers slouched near fires that sputtered more than they burned, their feet wrapped in burlap that wasn’t fooling anybody.

One man coughed so hard, he fainted. His friend grabbed him by the shoulders and dragged him closer to the flame. “If you die,” he muttered, “you’re giving us all bad ideas.”

Across the room, another soldier sat staring at a letter so worn out it looked ready to quit. “It’s all falling apart,” he whispered, though it wasn’t clear if he meant the war or himself.

A third soldier tore his last piece of bread in half, shoved it into his hands. “Eat,” he said, like it was obvious.

The man stared at the bread like it might explain itself. “Why?”

The bread-giver shrugged. “Because despair’s got no flavor, and I’ve already tried feeding frost with it.”

The march to haul cannons from Ticonderoga started with a quiet kind of dignity, but it didn’t last. The snow was too deep, the wind too sharp, and someone pointed toward the horizon, his voice shaking.

“There,” he whispered. “The British general. A ghost. He’s laughing at us.”

Nobody laughed, but nobody argued. Behind them, another soldier burst out laughing—not the good kind, but the sharp kind that cut through the storm.

“We’re dragging these monsters through this mess so we can blow stuff up? Is that it? Somebody pinch me, because this can’t be real.”

Knox said nothing. He trudged on, pretending he didn’t hear. But when one man stumbled and fell, too tired to rise, another stepped forward, picking up his rope without a word. Knox paused, watching, and thought, The cannons don’t matter. It’s us. We’re hauling each other, and that’s the only thing worth saving.

By Yorktown, hope and despair were neck and neck, racing toward whatever came next. The trenches smelled like smoke and sweat and failure. Washington stopped beside a soldier who had slumped into the mud, his hand still clutched around his friend’s lifeless shoulder.

“If you fall, we all fall,” Washington said, his tone heavy, but practiced.

The soldier looked up, his face blank. “We’re already on the ground, sir.”

Washington didn’t argue. He just helped the man to his feet, because sometimes standing was all you could do.

When Cornwallis surrendered, nobody cheered. The white flag fluttered in the wind, embarrassed, while the soldiers stared at it, waiting for someone to yell, “Just kidding!”

But silence won, like it always did.

What does war leave behind? Scraps. A widow lighting her neighbor’s fire. A soldier sharing crumbs because giving up seemed too dull. Somewhere, morale soup bubbles faintly in the background—a pot of something simple, something heavy, something oddly hopeful.

And against all odds, somehow, that’s enough.
