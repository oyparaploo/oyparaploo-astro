The Toll of War

When Food and Water Become Prayer

A Witness Account from a Siege

There's a question that no one speaks aloud anymore, though it fills every corner of this place: How did we arrive here? My grandmother would have called it the kind of question that plants itself between your ribs, grows thorns inward. Yesterday, a child drew flowers on the wall with a broken piece of plaster—blooms that will never need water, that will outlast us all. In these halls that reek of alcohol swabs and departing souls, the question belongs to all of us.

The women here have become scholars of reduction. They've learned to read the body's slow subtraction—flesh from bone, hope from eyes, tomorrow from today. One mother counts her daughter's ribs each morning like prayer beads, whispering numbers that used to mean nothing. We've become librarians cataloging erasure, though sometimes a nurse still wears lipstick, a stubborn red slash against the monochrome of dying. She reapplies it in the bathroom mirror, and I've seen her cry while doing it.

A nurse, her own equilibrium failing from forgotten meals, touches a mother's shoulder: "Rest now, tell me your story." Her wedding ring spins loose on her finger, catching light that has no business being beautiful here. She was married last spring—she showed me the photos once, her cheeks full then, her smile reaching her eyes. As though narratives could substitute for nutrients. As though bearing witness could restart these small, stuttering hearts. The nurse's shoes squeak against the floor—a sound so ordinary it makes me want to weep.

The reporters come carrying their own appetites—for narrative, for truth, for comprehension. One of them has perfectly manicured nails, I notice, as she grips her recorder. She touches them sometimes, as if to remind herself of the world where such things matter. They name it war correspondence, crisis documentation, this deliberate proximity to suffering. But how do you translate a child's palm that stays closed even in unconsciousness? A doctor tells me the children sleep with fists clenched now, as if trying to hold onto something that's already gone—perhaps the memory of fullness, of their mothers' faces before the hollowing. How do you transcribe the diminishing weight of a boy who becomes less substantial each time you lift him?

My grandmother insisted we were bred for endurance—our people like desert plants, surviving on almost nothing. She kept a cactus on her windowsill that bloomed once every seven years; we children would mark our heights against the wall, waiting. I never saw it bloom. But what happens when even the hardy things fail, when the deepest roots find only salt and ash? In this wing, we're discovering the boundaries of all our inherited metaphors. A woman in the next bed hums commercial jingles from decades past—advertisements for abundance no one here remembers tasting. Sometimes she stops mid-melody, as if she's forgotten how joy sounds.

The calculus has become ruthless. After this prolonged strangling of resources, the few functioning clinics shelter more apparitions than patients. Every third person counts days between meals, marking time in hunger. The medical staff, unsteady during their examinations, have been feeding their portions to the youngest patients. One doctor jokes that he's pioneering a new diet trend—he calls it "involuntary minimalism," then laughs until he has to sit down. But I saw him later, staring at his hands as if they belonged to someone else. We're all becoming transparent, rehearsing our own vanishing.

When supplies finally descend—crates tumbling from aircraft like afterthoughts—people die reaching for them. Someone has painted "FRAGILE" on the side of a demolished building, dark humor in block letters that rain is already washing away. Cut down in territories designated "restricted," as though any corner here permits freedom. The provisions accumulate in storerooms while infants' bones press through tissue like questions. A teenage volunteer organizes the canned goods by color because, she says, there should be some beauty left in the world. Her voice breaks when she says it.

In the improvised spaces where need has created new maps of anguish, the quiet is complete. Women without strength to soothe, babies beyond crying. A spider has built an elaborate web in the corner—even it looks undernourished, its silk threadbare. I watched a mother try to sing yesterday, but no sound came. Only the damaged ventilators persist, churning against air dense as sorrow. This isn't tranquility—it's the hoarding of oxygen when breathing becomes an economy. Sometimes a phone rings somewhere, unanswered, its cheerful tune obscene in this silence. We all pretend not to hear it.

The metal examination table downstairs has grown too familiar. Each small form beneath white cloth recites the same narrative: immunity surrendering, systems closing in sequence, the body's grim precision in consuming itself when nothing else remains. Someone has left a child's toy beneath the table—a plastic horse, forever frozen mid-gallop. I want to pick it up but can't bear to touch it. The documentation multiplies—proof for some eventual accounting, though who will demand this reckoning stays unknown.

I remember my mother's preparations, the careful layering of nuts and spices, how plenty felt permanent then. She had a special spoon just for stirring, worn smooth by generations of hands. I dream of that spoon sometimes, wake up tasting phantom flavors. Now mothers dissolve themselves gram by gram, portion by portion, transforming into vapor so their babies might stay substantial another day. One woman tells me she dreams of bread so vividly she wakes up chewing air. She laughs when she tells me this, but her eyes remain dry wells. This is devotion stripped to its frame—bare, consuming, untenable.

The terrain has been methodically erased—orchards turned ash, production halted. The mechanics of existence deliberately undone. A man shows me a seed he's been carrying in his pocket for months, waiting for soil that might receive it. His fingers shake as he returns it to his pocket—a future he guards like faith. People gamble everything for grain spilling from vehicles, for liquid from prohibited sources. During the last pause in violence, whatever lingered in the shops evaporated like dew. A shopkeeper still opens his empty store each morning, arranging nothing on bare shelves with meticulous care. He nods at customers who don't come, greets ghosts of plenty. Even recollection feels extravagant now.

Still we persist in observing, in documenting, in testimony. Not because it alters the formulas of want or deflects the ammunition that finds those racing toward sustenance. A journalist's pen runs out of ink mid-sentence; she continues writing, the invisible words somehow more true. Her hand keeps moving across the page—muscle memory of meaning. We do it because muteness would be the ultimate evacuation—to let these accounts drift like pollen with no ground to receive them.

In this unit where machines labor against air too dense to process, where women cradle children weightless as whispers, where healers collapse from want while ministering to the wanting, we're all becoming unearthed—grasping for moisture in soil that's been stolen, stretching toward something that might nourish us even as we know it's gone. A maintenance worker waters the plastic plants in the lobby every day, and no one has the heart to tell him to stop. Yesterday I saw him pause, touch a fabric leaf with such tenderness I had to look away.

The unspoken question endures. But maybe the true question is why we expected different. Maybe the true question is how tenderness survives in spaces designed for its extinction. How these women still hum, soundlessly now, to babies whose structures surface through skin. Yesterday, I saw two nurses share a piece of gum, cutting it in half with surgical precision, savoring it like communion. They closed their eyes as they chewed, transported somewhere sweeter. How the ancient tree beyond the window, foundations bared and scorching, still declines to surrender.

This is our testimony. This is our knowledge. That in the most wounded places of our fractured earth, humanity shows itself most starkly—in both its brutality and its unthinkable determination. A child asked me today if clouds are edible, and I couldn't answer. My throat closed around the lie I couldn't tell, the truth I couldn't speak. We chronicle not only the perishing but the persistence that continues against logic, delicate and adamant as a pulse that refuses to quit when it has every cause to cease. Even now, someone is folding a paper crane from a medical form, fingers remembering beauty in the midst of this careful, necessary witnessing. She places it on the windowsill where it catches the light, trembling like hope made visible, fragile as the breath that keeps us here another day.
