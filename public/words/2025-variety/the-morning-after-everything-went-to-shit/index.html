The Morning After Everything Went to Shit

← Words / 2025 Variety

Lead-in:
Found this crumpled in a trash can outside the unemployment office. Coffee stains, tear stains, something that might be snot. Someone had started to throw it away, changed their mind, threw it away again. The janitor saved it because he said it reminded him of 2008. And 2001. And 1987.

Troy's in the backyard with Pearl because inside the house his phone won't stop screaming.

She's blowing bubbles. He's watching his retirement die in real-time on an app. Down $247,000 since breakfast.

"Dad," she says. Eight years old. "Tommy's dad said bubble economy then said fuck."

"Don't say fuck."

"You just said it."

"I'm allowed."

"Why?"

"Because I'm the one whose—" He stops. Can't explain margin calls to an eight-year-old. Can't explain them to himself really.

She blows another bubble. It pops on her nose. "Are we poor now?"

His grandfather jumped in 1929. Off a building. Had purple stains on his hands from working a printing press. Troy's mom told him this story every crash—1987, 2001, 2008. "At least you still have your hands," she'd say. Troy looks at his hands. Soft. Only good for typing numbers that don't exist anymore.

"Dad?"

"What?"

"You're being weird."

"I'm not—" His phone buzzes. Seventeen missed calls. "Shit."

"That's a bad word too."

"Lesser bad."

"Mom says they're all bad. Said you did something bad with client money."

Troy freezes. "When did Mom—"

"She called yesterday. Said you're probably going to jail."

"She said WHAT?"

Pearl shrugs. Blows another bubble.

The bubble solution is dollar store brand. Smells like artificial nothing. When did they switch? When did everything become the cheap version?

"Ms. Martinez has a poster about Buddha," Pearl says. "About wanting things and suffering."

Ms. Martinez makes $42,000 a year. Troy made that in bonuses. Made. Past tense.

"Buddha didn't have a mortgage," Troy says.

"What's a mortgage?"

"A bubble."

She doesn't get it. Good.

"Dad, if bubbles always pop, why make them?"

He should say something about joy, about temporary beauty, about—

"I don't know."

"You went to college."

"That was another bubble."

"You're not making sense."

She's right. Nothing makes sense. Not the numbers vanishing, not his marriage evaporating, not his grandfather's purple hands, not the client money he touched when he shouldn't have, not—

"Dad, you're doing the weird thing again."

Pearl's bubble solution runs out. She tips the bottle. Nothing.

"Now what?"

Inside, his phone is having a seizure. The lawyer texted: "Don't do anything stupid." Too late. Did that yesterday when he moved money that wasn't his to cover margins on bets that were definitely his.

"We go inside," he says.

"Then what?"

"I answer the phone."

"Then what?"

"I don't know."

"Mom says you're bad at planning."

"Mom's not here."

"Yeah."

They stand up. Pearl's knees have grass stains. Real stains. Not like the purple on his grandfather's hands but something.

Walking to the house, Pearl says: "Tommy's dad isn't jumping."

"What?"

"Off buildings. Tommy asked. His dad said jumping is for 1929."

"That's—" Troy doesn't know what that is.

"He said now people just declare bank-ruppy."

"Bankruptcy."

"That. Or they go to Florida."

"Why Florida?"

"I don't know. Tommy didn't know either."

Inside, the kitchen smells like old coffee and new fear. Pearl turns on cartoons. Tom and Jerry. Cat chases mouse forever. Never catches it. Never stops trying.

Troy picks up his phone. The lawyer: "We need to talk. Now."

"Dad?"

"Yeah?"

"Are you going to jail?"

"Maybe."

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Will you come back?"

"Yes."

"Promise?"

He looks at her. Grass stains. Dirty fingernails. Watching cartoons like the world isn't ending.

"Promise."

She nods. Goes back to the TV.

The phone rings. He answers.

"Troy? Thank God. Listen—"

He's listening. But he's also watching Pearl laugh at the cartoon. The cat just ran into a wall shaped like a door. Classic.

His grandfather jumped.
Troy won't.
Not because he's better.
Just because Pearl's watching cartoons and someone needs to be here when they end.

Coda:
Bubbles pop and markets crash and some people jump and others just answer the phone and face whatever comes next. There's no wisdom in this, no lesson about temporary beauty or necessary destruction. Just people blowing soap through plastic wands because it's Tuesday and the solution hasn't run out yet. Your grandfather's purple hands were real. Your daughter's grass stains are real. Everything else—the numbers, the portfolios—was always just air pretending to be solid. When it pops, you're still here. That's not profound. That's just Tuesday.
