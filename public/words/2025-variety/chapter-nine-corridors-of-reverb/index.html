Chapter Nine ~ Corridors of Reverb

Words / 2025 Variety

Morning arrived as a rumor the safehouse didn’t quite believe. The blinds held their slats like teeth; the room’s stale light was the color of water left too long in a glass. The radio still hissed, not because anyone had asked it to but because stopping seemed impolite after a night of service. The recorder’s red dot glowed punctual as a minor planet.

Troll Tearsen sat at the table with a pencil and the sleepless steadiness of someone who had learned the difference between exhaustion and surrender. She drew three small circles on the back of a receipt and labeled them without writing the labels: clear, red, cracked. When she lifted her eyes, the marbles on the plate answered the roll call by being exactly themselves.

Libaux Torrini stood by the window as if negotiating with the day. Tires hissed on the ring road below; a motorbike threaded traffic like a syllable trying to outrun grammar. “He broadcasted through us,” he said, not turning. “And to us. The city is his mixer.”

“Then we go where the city composes,” Troll said.

His mouth tilted. “Underground.”

---

The Metro turned voices into metal. Down the steps, sound slowed to echo even when pace quickened. A guitarist at the landing rehearsed chords the tunnel swallowed and returned in chalk outlines. A train arrived with sheet-metal bravado, brakes screaming a note Troll could feel more than name, somewhere between a child’s scream and the hymn a machine makes when it remembers being ore.

Pigalle’s platform was a corridor of posters and instruction. Ne traversez pas. Sortie. Silence written nowhere and enforced everywhere by acoustics alone. The PA announced delays with the weary authority of a teacher who had learned not to expect applause. Every syllable wore tile and tunnel.

Troll and Libaux stood where their reflections flattened in the glass of the opposite platform. The air was damp, haloed in brake dust. Troll opened Luc’s scanner; its LEDs stepped through frequencies like a cautious animal sniffing thresholds.

She found snow, then voice, then a seam of silence too intentional to be accidental. Libaux looked down the tunnel, his posture never admitting to hope. On the bench beside them, a blue marble—no swirl, just transluscent cobalt—rolled a centimeter each time a train exhaled somewhere beyond a curve. tik… tik. No bag. No string. No note.

“New color,” Libaux said.

“New room,” Troll answered.

The next train arrived, its doors sighing open like a secret that had given up on discretion. They boarded a carriage that smelled of coins and the residue of perfume. As the train accelerated, the blue marble quivered where Troll had pocketed it, translating vibration into information her skin read without consent.

Stations became vowels: Anvers, Blanche, Clichy. The lines on the wall map converged and diverged like a staff of notation too complex for melody. Troll watched an ad for a language school flicker across the aisle, a smiling face promising fluency in cities that had never learned to speak without accent. The light strobed Libaux’s knuckles in slow questions.

Her phone buzzed. Miyake Coscia. Troll let it buzz again and then looked, because refusal didn’t change time.
She’s tired. Breathing lighter. If you can come, come. If you cannot, say her name aloud where you are. She will hear it either way.

The tunnel stole her throat and gave it back empty.

Libaux saw the screen and didn’t say anything useful. He said, “We will make both rooms true,” which was the kind of lie that had learned to sound kind.

The train lunged into a junction and the scanner woke like an animal scenting blood: a clean little chirp, then hiss, then a voice braided into the air conditioning.

“…liaise… oversight…—”

Stremler’s vowels attempted modesty and achieved theater. The ceiling speakers offered them in mono, smearing context into corridor. Commuters didn’t react; they were fluent in public address and private ignorance.

Libaux held the scanner steady. “Source is local. Repeater piggybacking the PA,” he said. “He wants the idea of authority, not the content.”

“Where does authority sleep?” Troll asked.

“Behind signs that say ‘no admittance’.”

---

They rode to an interchange, transferred into a less-loved line with older carriages that did not apologize for their age. A violin student practiced in the tile throat between platforms. The notes arched and then fell as if the bow had decided to be a rib. Troll and Libaux followed the sound to the far end of the platform where a door stood unconvincing in its neutrality: TECHNIQUE in faded letters, a grille breath-humming.

Libaux produced a key that did not belong to him and did its job anyway. “Paris has one lock with many faces,” he said.

The corridor beyond was painted a version of beige that had learned humility from dust. Fluorescent lights ticked in time with each other when they were in the mood. The floor sang back their footsteps, a palate of metallics; the air smelled of warm relay and old water.

They found a closet that had never forgiven being repurposed. Inside, a small box sprouted cables like a nest ignoring ownership. A cheap transmitter blinked its cheap little blinking. Next to it, a grocery bag with a handful of marbles that weren’t children’s: they were deliberately imperfect—the glass seeded with air—so each one sang a slightly different note when rolled across metal. Foley by intention.

“This is how you feed a myth through a city,” Libaux said softly. “You make the speakers your choir.”

A scrap of paper beneath the transmitter had absorbed someone else’s hand: SALLE RÉPÉTITION – 14H. An address, scrawled as if the writer had been walking.

“Rehearsal hall,” Troll said.

“They are practicing something,” Libaux replied. “So we should attend.”

He killed the transmitter. The box relented without drama. A drop of water fell somewhere and the room applauded once.

They left the marbles on the shelf where they’d sat. Troll picked one—a red seeded with bubbles—and rolled it across the metal lip. Its voice was a dry tik with a tremolo at the end, like the city trying to laugh privately.

---

The rehearsal hall was in a basement that had pretended to be a club in 1978 and never quite recovered. A corridor of photos testified to people who had been famous within these walls—hands lifted, eyes closed, microphones held as if they weighed opinion. A bored attendant stamped their day passes with a rubber thud that went straight to the bones.

Rooms bled into rooms. In one: a brass section warming vowels. In another: a choreographer clapped steady ta-ta-ta while feet attempted obedience. In a third: a choir that had learned to be many without becoming anonymous.

They found Salle 4 by following an absence. It sounded like quiet, but it wasn’t. It was an engineered lack, a space that swallowed rehearsal and returned only breath.

Inside, a table with a small mixer. Speakers the exact size of lies. A reel-to-reel that had been recommissioned from some patient archive and now found itself asked to play living people. On the floor, a rig of smooth wooden tracks—curves and slopes—a child’s marble run expanded to occupy seriousness. Marbles waited at the top of the track like thoughts queued for speech.

“Performance,” Libaux said.

The reel-to-reel ticked once, then once again. It began to turn without asking their leave. Tape hiss rose like weather. Children’s voices arrived—not singing; counting.
…six, seven, eight…
The tape spliced them into harmonies they had not agreed to sing.

Over the count, Stremler’s consonants, polite as always: “—oversight is a virtue when outcomes are impolite—”

Troll stepped to the track. She let one marble go. It rolled. Each segment of wood had been tuned to a pitch; the marble played the hall like an instrument, tik, tak, tik, a xylophone of intention. The tape bled the marble’s notes back a half-second delayed, doubling the instrument with its own echo.

The door behind them clicked. A woman in a black sweater entered with the expression of someone paid to prevent adjectives. She was not Stremler; she was a person who had learned to inhabit his gravity.

“You have the wrong room,” she said.

Libaux said, “Yes,” with the confidence of a man who required no camera to act. He showed a badge that belonged to a previous life. “We’re exactly where we should be.”

Her eyes assessed the distance to the next door, the weight of the reel-to-reel, the count of their hands. “This hall is for art,” she said.

“This is art,” Troll said, letting the next marble go. It chimed the tuned ribs and the speaker fed it back as if the room were munching on itself. “And logistics.”

The woman tilted her head. She could have been amused on a day with more air. “You’re not the first to imagine you can listen your way to the moral center of a sentence.”

“Nor the last,” Libaux said.

She looked at the table. A small card lay there—date, time, a name scrawled as if by a shadow. S TREMLER – SALLE 4 – 12:30. This morning.

Troll let the last marble go. It hesitated at a join like a thought catching on furniture, then continued down. In the speakers, the pause became a stutter, then an artifact, then a lesson. The reel-to-reel exhaled and stopped.

“You’re late,” the woman said. “He doesn’t wait for applause.”

“What does he wait for?” Troll asked.

“For the room to learn its part.” She reached to stop the machine as if granting mercy to an animal. “You’re learning. Too late.”

“What did he rehearse?” Libaux asked.

“Silence,” she said, and left, as if that were an ordinary exit line.

---

Outside, Paris pushed on with its day, unimpressed by rehearsal. Troll and Libaux ate food that could be carried in one hand and never asked for a plate. The street chattered with forks that had been replaced by fingers. Somewhere a drill fussed at stone; somewhere else a puddle rehearsed the same splash each time a deliveryman missed it by an inch.

Libaux’s phone vibrated. Uma Arouje: Report. Now. He typed Working and did not send it. He put the phone away as a man might put away a blade he respected and disliked.

Troll’s phone buzzed again. Miyake. She answered this time, because not answering had begun to feel like theft.

“She’s resting,” Miyake said. “We’re in the wide part of the river now. If you speak her name into a room, choose a room with good echoes.”

Troll looked up at the balcony awnings and laundry and antennae, the city’s apertures. “Miyake,” she said, and then, “Mother,” because there are names that no longer admit to titles. She said it again, softer, letting the street carry it and stretch it and return it as an older version. The syllables came back in fragments from glass and drain and wheel hub. She nodded into the phone even though no instruction had been offered.

After the call, Libaux put a hand on her shoulder for exactly as long as such gestures are allowed in public no matter the country. “The chapter will still be there when you’ve turned the page,” he said.

“Books burn,” Troll said.

“We make copies.”

He said it lightly and it still felt true in the way the right lie feels like a brace.

---

The scanner woke again near Saint-Lazare, a seam opening in the city’s fabric. A long corridor of tiled white and commuters performing private lives at public speed. They followed the bleed into a stairwell that should have been only a stairwell. At the bottom, a maintenance door. Someone had jammed its latch with a folded pamphlet from a museum that celebrated sound.

Inside: a small chamber, half utility, half confession booth. On a folding chair, a man in a courier jacket slumped without drama, the dreamless sleep of someone who had been convinced by a needle. His breath was present and ragged; his pupils, when Libaux lifted a lid, chose not to choose. On the concrete floor: a portable transmitter much like the last, only this one was a little smarter, a little prouder of itself. Next to it, a marble of clear glass threaded with a white coil.

Troll knelt. The marble reflected the fluorescent tube as if it were a star with a cold, clean heart. She rolled it once across the floor. It translated concrete into tik and memory into nothing helpful.

The courier’s phone vibrated himself awake. He blinked at them as if he had been told this would happen and couldn’t remember whether to be relieved or angry.

“Do you work for him?” Libaux asked.

“For money,” the man said. His accent collapsed three languages into necessity. “Not for the name.”

“Where did you pick up?” Troll asked.

He tried to sit up and failed. The chair complained in Morse. “Different each time,” he said. “Call, meet, carry. No faces. A man with a polite voice who owns rooms.”

“Stremler,” Libaux said.

“Your name, not mine.” The man swallowed without saliva. “He said if you hear him, you were supposed to.”

“Why?” Troll asked.

“So you’d stop looking at what the rooms do without him.”

Troll felt the city lean toward that sentence. The marbles in her coat pocket knocked once against each other, a small, involuntary applause.

They called an ambulance because even in noir there are people who are not allowed to be props. The courier’s eyes thanked and did not admit it.

Libaux coiled the transmitter’s cord, then uncoiled it, then coiled it again. The scanner on his hip whispered to itself.

“We’re being taught,” he said, not with awe. “He’s directing us to direct ourselves.”

Troll pocketed the white-coil marble. It cooled the skin of her palm. Inaudible, it still claimed to be sound.

---

At dusk, the city recommitted to its program. Restaurants rehearsed their knives against boards. Scooters contributed their tantrums. Windows closed with the decisive syllable of people keeping heat where they had paid to keep it. Troll and Libaux sat on a low wall near Trinité and ate things that insisted on their own grease.

Mage Meuller’s email arrived as a slab:
Analyzed your capture. “Will not speak” is assembled from existing speeches. Different rooms, different mics, identical mouth. He’s cutting himself into a chorus. Frequencies show slight delay artifacts consistent with handheld repeaters. You’re chasing the puppet and the strings while the hand is elsewhere. Also: the bubble-seeded marbles—each has a unique sonic fingerprint when rolled across different surfaces. He’s signing in glass.

Libaux read over Troll’s shoulder. “A craftsman of absence,” he said.

“A composer of it,” Troll said.

She said her mother’s name again, quietly, because Miyake had suggested rooms with good echoes. The Metro breathing below obliged, returned the two syllables as if they had walked a long way to get back.

A bus pulled up and sighed. The advertisement on its side showed a shiny child’s toy that had cost too much to be loved by accident. The child in the picture held a marble like a pearl and did not understand why the photographer was so pleased with the reflection.

“Tomorrow he’ll move,” Libaux said. “We’ll be the marbles, rolled down a track he built.”

“Then we learn to jump the rails,” Troll said.

“How?”

“By listening wrong.”

He barked a laugh he didn’t owe anyone. “Finally, a method I can respect.”

The scanner gave a last polite cough of audio as if clearing its throat before bed. The city’s reverb turned from declaration to rumor. The day passed itself to the night without ceremony.

They stood. The wall made the small noise that happens when weight leaves a thing it had prepared to carry longer. A child, somewhere above, dropped a ball; it bounced twice and then learned obedience.

In her pocket, the white-coil marble pressed a circle into Troll’s palm. She imagined Helene Klausmun’s steady voice from the mountain fog—We’ve held the scene for you—and thought: the scene is the city now. She imagined Nunito Volker’s rigid hand in the shelter and Miller Corbal’s cracked marble at the breakwater and all the rooms that had learned to echo as if truth were a sound before it was a sentence.

“Tomorrow,” Libaux said.

“Tomorrow,” she echoed.

The city returned the word as a softer copy of itself. Then the corridor of reverb went quiet in the way corridors do when the play is between acts, and the audience leans back to decide if they will stay.
