Brrd Roots ~ Empire of Cells

Words / 2025 Variety

Ministry of Mangos and the Completely Sensible Prison

The Heat in Valledupar

The stage opens on Valledupar—a town besieged by heat so aggressive it could file lawsuits. Vendors frantically wave oversized banana leaves, achieving absolutely nothing except self-inflicted windburn. Mangos larger than rugby balls sit melting on comically small carts, and swarms of flies circle them like sinister, buzzing ballet dancers.

Vendor #1 (dramatically collapsing onto a mango): “This is it, lads. The heat’s won. Tell my family… I sold all the pineapples first.”

Vendor #2 (furiously wiping his sweaty face with an equally sweaty cloth): “There are two constants in Valledupar: the sun and La Tramacúa, that over-glorified concrete pimple on our skyline!”

(Dramatic orchestral sting. Spotlight snaps onto La Tramacúa Penitentiary. Cue ominous smoke and thunder, despite the clear skies. The razor wire atop the walls curls upwards like taunting spaghetti noodles.)

In the shade of a skeletal jacaranda tree—a tree so dead it actively groans in protest—a little girl crouches, wielding chalk the size of her own head. She furrows her brow as she draws a flamingo wearing sunglasses.

Little Girl (examining her handiwork): “Mama, does it look happy?”

Mother (distracted as she wrestles with a mango the size of a pumpkin): “Happy? Sweetheart, nothing’s been happy since they built that… that prison for people who dared to say mangos shouldn’t cost more than bread!”

(A sudden puff of glittery smoke. Enter The Prophet, accompanied by a kazoo fanfare.)

A Prophet in the Shadows

The Prophet steps out of the smoke, his robes flowing dramatically despite the wind failing to cooperate. His crooked staff emits faint sparkles, and an inexplicable bagpipe solo plays in the background. The Prophet clears his throat, and the sound causes all the mangos in the marketplace to spontaneously spin in unison.

Prophet (gesturing toward La Tramacúa): “They built this not for justice, but for fear. F-E-A-R!” (He spells it out like a game show host while pointing at an invisible chalkboard.) “And what happens to fear?”

Vendor #3 (raising a trembling hand): “Uh… it buys another mango cart?”

Prophet (ignoring the vendor completely): “Fear rusts. Fear cracks. Fear melts faster than these mangos!” (He attempts to eat a mango dramatically but fails, as it slips comically through his hands.)

The Little Girl, unimpressed, points at her flamingo-drawing. “Can’t fear just go on vacation instead?”

The Prophet, stunned, stares at her for a moment, then bellows: “TIME WILL TELL!” before striding mysteriously into the nearest mango stall, which collapses instantly under his weight.

Inside the Walls

Inside La Tramacúa, time doesn’t tick—it tumbles down a flight of stairs and lands face-first in an existential crisis. The walls aren’t just damp; they appear to sweat resentment. Prisoners mill about: one is knitting a sweater with no needles, another is shouting poetry at a confused pigeon who seems to applaud.

Inmate #1 (holding a torn photo of a woman): “Her smile... brighter than the sun, sweeter than any mango jam. Alas, I shall only see her in dreams!”

Inmate #2 (rolling his eyes): “Mate, that’s a stock image from an old cereal box.”

Suddenly, a voice from nowhere booms: “WE ARE STILL HERE!” The walls shudder. A pigeon in the corner salutes solemnly before flying directly into a wall. Guards scramble like startled penguins.

Guard (wildly flipping through a manual titled “What to Do When the Walls Revolt”): “Step one: don’t panic. Step two… AH, IT’S TOO LATE!”

A Nation’s Wound

Outside, at a vigil, candles flicker and levitate inexplicably. A grandmother attempts to pour coffee into her cup, but it leaks from five different places, landing mostly on her shoes.

Grandmother (speaking to no one in particular): “They said this would help us. Help us!” (She shakes her broken cup in righteous indignation, accidentally flinging coffee at a passing goat, which proceeds to faint dramatically.)

A man in the crowd hums a melody so out of tune it offends physics. Yet somehow, the humming carries through the gathering, softening the vigil’s somber air.

The Prophet (materializing once again in swirling glitter): “What burns brightest here isn’t their walls…” (he gestures vaguely, and a mango inexplicably catches fire), “…it’s YOUR LOVE!”

(Cue a choir of kazoo players, joined by someone inexplicably juggling flaming mangos.)

Flames of Resistance

At the heart of the vigil, an elder lights another candle. The flame briefly turns neon green, whistles “Yankee Doodle,” and then settles into its glow. The elder, undeterred, lifts it high.

Elder (voice firm): “We won’t forget.”

The Prophet nods sagely. “Nor should you. Chains rust. Empires fall. And love… love outlasts even mango tariffs.”

(The crowd erupts in cheers as someone catapults a flaming mango into the night sky, where it bursts like fireworks. The stage goes dark, save for a single spotlight on the chalk flamingo, now inexplicably dancing.)

Empire of Utterly Sensible Cells

Act I: Valledupar—Land of Giant Mangos and Existential Sweating

Valledupar is inexplicably sweltering, with the kind of heat that could roast marshmallows from 100 feet away. Vendors frantically attempt to sell mangos so massive they require wheelbarrows, while occasionally yelling things like, “Step right up! Get your government-issued Mango of Doom!” The pavement groans under the stomping of boots belonging to guards from La Tramacúa—the notorious local penitentiary—which sits atop the town like a grumpy concrete toad.

Under a skeletal jacaranda tree (which seems actively annoyed to be alive), a Little Girl crouches, holding a piece of chalk that is mysteriously shaped like a lobster. She begins to draw an anatomically accurate unicorn. “Mama, does this look happy?” she asks.

Her Mother, struggling to balance three bruised mangos (each one inexplicably wearing sunglasses), glances toward the prison, which is somehow both ominous and weirdly judgmental. “Happy?” she scoffs. “Nothing’s been happy since the Mayor declared Wednesdays ‘Mandatory Mango Appreciation Day.’”

Suddenly, puffs of glittery smoke erupt from nowhere. A kazoo begins playing ominously. Enter The Prophet, who is both majestic and vaguely perplexing.

Act II: The Prophet Appears... and Also Trips Over a Mango

The Prophet emerges from a cloud of sparkles, his robe sweeping majestically across the cobblestones—until he trips over a comically large mango and lands face-first into a fruit stand. The mangos somehow applaud.

Regaining his composure, he raises his crooked staff (which hums inexplicably) and declares, “They built this prison not for justice, but for FEAR!” He turns dramatically, pointing at La Tramacúa, which immediately emits a puff of smoke for no discernible reason.

A Vendor interrupts nervously: “Uh, sorry to bother you, mysterious staff-wielding mango expert, but what, exactly, does fear have to do with mangos?”

The Prophet ignores the vendor completely and begins pacing theatrically. “Chains rust! Walls crack! And fear… fear MELTS like butter on a mango pancake!”

The Little Girl, unimpressed, holds up her chalk lobster. “Couldn’t fear just move to Hawaii instead?”

The Prophet freezes, staring at her intensely before exclaiming, “TIME WILL TELL!” He then walks straight into another fruit cart, which collapses under the sheer absurdity of his existence.

Act III: Inside La Tramacúa

Inside the penitentiary, time doesn’t tick—it sighs loudly, rolls its eyes, and pretends not to notice the chaos around it. The walls aren’t just damp—they actively drip with sarcasm. Prisoners pace back and forth like caffeinated penguins, some attempting to train pigeons to write love letters. Meanwhile, one man loudly serenades an uncooperative mop.

Inmate #1 (holding a worn photograph): “Her smile... like mango jam, sweet and fleeting, but tragically out of reach!”

Inmate #2 (juggling three oranges and a pineapple): “Barry, mate, your ‘wife’ is literally just the stock photo from a mango-flavored cereal box.”

Suddenly, the walls begin to shake as a disembodied voice screams, “WE ARE STILL HERE!” A pigeon wearing a tiny hat salutes dramatically before flying directly into the ceiling.

Guard (frantically flipping through a manual titled “Dealing with Sentient Walls”): “Step one: don’t panic. Step two… AHHHHHH!” (He proceeds to faint into a pile of pineapples.)

Act IV: Hope Blooms Where Logic Falters

Outside, at a vigil, candles flicker and occasionally sprout miniature fireworks. A Grandmother pours coffee into a mug that leaks from five separate places. “They said this would help us,” she mutters indignantly, shaking her mug so hard she accidentally smacks a goat, which promptly faints.

A Man hums a tune so off-key that physics briefly pauses in protest. Yet somehow, his melody carries through the crowd, softening the vigil’s somber air. The Prophet emerges once more, this time inexplicably riding a unicycle made of mangos.

“What burns brightest here isn’t their walls,” he declares, juggling three flaming pineapples, “IT’S YOUR LOVE!” (Cue kazoo fanfare.)

Act V: The Resistance Fights Back (With Mangos and Kazoo Solos)

The Prophet steps into the center of the vigil, raising his staff as if commanding the stars themselves to pay attention. Candles levitate briefly, then begin whistling “Twinkle Twinkle Little Star” for no apparent reason.

“They call this justice,” he announces, gesturing wildly, causing one mango to sprout wings and fly away. “But what they truly fear is YOUR LOVE!”

A Young Woman, standing defiantly atop a crate labeled “Free Mangos for Revolutionaries,” raises her fist and yells, “THEIR WALLS WILL FALL!” The crowd roars like a distant thunderstorm made entirely of absurd enthusiasm.

Nearby, smuggled notes pass between trembling hands. A woman clutches one, its edges crumpled but sacred. “I love you,” it reads in shaky script. “These walls cannot hold forever.”

Epilogue: Chaos and Hope

As dawn breaks, the razor wire atop La Tramacúa glints faintly, almost as if it’s tired of existing. Inside the prison, water continues to drip melodramatically from the ceiling, while guards debate whether mango-flavored coffee counts as contraband.

Meanwhile, back in the marketplace, mangos have somehow unionized, demanding better working conditions. The Little Girl finishes her chalk masterpiece: a flamingo riding a motorcycle. It promptly comes to life and speeds off into the sunrise.

The Prophet, watching from the shadows, murmurs to himself, “Empires fall. Chains rust. And mangos…” (he pauses dramatically) “mangos remain eternal.”

(Cue thunderous kazoo finale as the stage fades to black.)

The Ministry of Cracked Walls and Unreasonably Optimistic Chains

**Act I: Valledupar’s Eternal Heat and the Mango That
Ate a Town**

The streets of Valledupar shimmer under the kind of oppressive heat that makes ice cubes apply for workers’ compensation. Vendors stagger about like exhausted flamingos, frantically hawking mangos the size of beach balls. Flies buzz angrily, engaging in synchronized dive-bombing routines aimed at anyone who dares to breathe.

Vendor #1 (fanning himself with a banana leaf that disintegrates in his hand): “This is it. The heat’s finally won. Somebody tell my goats I loved them!”

(Cue dramatic wailing as his goats trot past, entirely unfazed and wearing sun hats.)

Meanwhile, a Little Girl crouches under the shade of an absurdly skeletal jacaranda tree that loudly protests every gust of wind. She is drawing something with a giant stick of chalk that could double as a battering ram. “Mama, does this flower look happy?” she asks, furrowing her brow at her creation—a flamingo inexplicably playing the accordion.

Her Mother, juggling mangos so bruised they look more like deflated footballs, sighs melodramatically. “Happy? Sweetheart, nobody’s been happy since the mayor declared this town an honorary oven.”

(Enter The Prophet in a cloud of glittery smoke, accompanied by a live kazoo rendition of “Ride of the Valkyries.”)

Act II: The Prophet Faces a Mango Crisis

The Prophet’s robes billow dramatically despite the absence of wind. His staff hums ominously, occasionally emitting confetti for no particular reason. He strides purposefully into the marketplace, accidentally tripping over a mango that, in its indignation, rolls away muttering threats.

Prophet (recovering his dignity): “Fear! They built those walls for fear!”

(Cue spotlight on La Tramacúa Penitentiary, which emits a suspiciously loud “moo” despite being a prison and not, in fact, a cow.)

Vendor #2 (nervously pointing at the Prophet’s staff): “Er, excuse me, good sir. Are you...talking about mango prices?”

Prophet (ignoring the question entirely): “Walls crumble. Chains rust. Fear breaks apart like soggy croutons!”

The Little Girl, unimpressed, points at her accordion-flamingo drawing. “Couldn’t fear just go on vacation to Bermuda instead?”

The Prophet blinks several times before declaring: “That is outside my jurisdiction!” He proceeds to walk directly into a mango cart, which collapses with comical precision.

Act III: Inside La Tramacúa—Where Logic Checks Out

The prison breathes despair—not figuratively but literally, as its walls occasionally exhale dramatically. Prisoners engage in surreal activities: one is trying to train pigeons to play chess, while another attempts to juggle oranges using only his elbows. In one particularly damp corner, a man cradles a broken string of beads.

Inmate #1 (holding up the beads): “These were my mother’s! They reminded me of rivers that don’t stink!”

Inmate #2 (balancing a pineapple on his head): “Barry, mate, stop monologuing about beads. We’re trying to stage an interpretive dance protest.”

Suddenly, a disembodied voice booms: “WE ARE STILL HERE!” The walls shudder ominously. A pigeon wearing a monocle salutes solemnly before flying directly into a guard’s hat.

Guard (frantically flipping through a manual titled “How to Deal with Talking Walls”): “Step one: remain calm. Step two… PAAAANIC!” (He promptly faints into a puddle of mango juice.)

Act IV: Hope Blooms Like a Very Confused Flower

Outside the prison, at a vigil, candles flicker and occasionally burst into song. A Grandmother pours coffee into a cup so cracked it resembles modern art. “They said this would help us,” she mutters indignantly. “Help us! How does a candle fix prison walls?!” (Her coffee leaks everywhere, causing a passing goat to faint dramatically.)

The Prophet reappears, now inexplicably riding a unicycle made entirely of mangos. “What burns brightest here isn’t their walls!” he declares. (Cue fireworks spelling out the word “LOVE.”) “It’s YOUR LOVE!”

A Man in the Crowd, momentarily inspired, attempts to juggle flaming mangos but accidentally sets his hat on fire. The crowd cheers anyway.

Act V: Resistance and a Flying Flamingo

At the center of the vigil, an elder lights another candle, which whistles “Twinkle Twinkle Little Star” before returning to normal. “We won’t forget,” she says firmly, her voice carrying more weight than her coffee mug.

The Prophet nods approvingly. “Nor should you. Empires fall. Fear breaks. And love… love survives mango tariffs.”

(The stage darkens as the Little Girl finishes her drawing—a flamingo riding a rocket ship. It promptly comes to life, salutes the crowd, and launches into the sky amid thunderous kazoo fanfare.)

Epilogue: Chaos Prevails, But So Does Hope

As dawn breaks, La Tramacúa appears inexplicably smaller, as though it decided to give up halfway through existing. The prisoners begin debating whether mango-flavored toothpaste should be banned, while pigeons organize a chess tournament.

Back in the town square, mangos have unionized. The Little Girl stands proudly beside her chalk drawing, now featuring a flamingo moonwalking across Saturn. The Prophet, watching from the shadows, mutters quietly to himself: “Every wall cracks. Every chain rusts. And sometimes… flamingos rise.”

(Cue final kazoo crescendo as the curtains fall.)

The Ministry of Rusty Chains and Cracked Reality

Act I: The Phantom Child and The Soggy Walls

A yard that feels oddly too dramatic for its existence is revealed. In one corner, a Woman sits cross-legged, holding her own arms as though cradling an invisible yet judgmental ghost baby. The yard itself seems suspiciously alive, occasionally sighing and emitting random applause. The walls drip moisture—not with water, but with what can only be described as "sentient despair."

Woman (singing a lullaby so off-key that several pigeons faint): “I will not lose myself, even if the walls keep judging me!”

(Enter The Prophet, emerging from behind a mysteriously oversized pineapple. His crooked staff taps the ground with an ominous rhythm that causes the nearby pebbles to start tap-dancing. He traces circles in the dirt for absolutely no reason other than dramatic effect.)

Prophet (whispering into the night): “Every chain rusts, every wall cracks, and every pineapple eventually turns into compost... Their time will end—it always does!”

(The staff emits confetti and random kazoo noises, signaling the end of Act I.)

Act II: Fragments of Resistance Across Worlds

The bustling streets of Manila appear as though painted by a toddler who decided realism was boring. The air hums faintly, carrying the smell of rebellion and overly ripe bananas. Vendors yell prices so absurd that reality pauses to protest: “Two million pesos for ONE mango! Includes free despair!”

Among the chaos, a Woman in a tiny stall sells mismatched trinkets: a locket without a chain, a ring missing its gem, and a watch that mysteriously ticks backwards. She dusts them off with exaggerated care.

Vendor Woman (waving a gemless ring like it’s an ancient artifact): “These were left behind by people who couldn’t afford two-million-peso mangos. But they’re priceless now! Definitely not cursed!”

(The Prophet appears from nowhere, his staff emitting trumpet sounds while small clouds gather suspiciously over his head.)

Prophet (gesturing dramatically to a faint mural): “Behold! Emilio Aguinaldo—forever defiant, etched into chipped paint that fights against time and mango inflation! They knew the empire would try to silence them, but hope doesn’t die—it just turns into interpretive dance!”

(Nearby pigeons begin rhythmically nodding to an invisible beat. The curtains close dramatically as a locket floats into the air for no apparent reason.)

Act III: Cracks in the Empire

In Panama City, the old Canal Zone leans tiredly against time, its barracks yawning so loudly that passing children laugh just to drown out the noise. Cracked windows gape like shocked faces, while the barracks themselves slump under the burden of existing.

Children chase a soccer ball so worn it resembles a deflated melon. The ball rolls away, somehow gaining sentience, and lands near The Prophet’s feet. He picks it up carefully, holding it like the secrets of the universe lie within its battered surface.

Prophet (turning the ball in his hands as it emits dramatic sighs): “Everything built to dominate crumbles eventually... except for that one vending machine at the airport.”

(Cue overly loud orchestral music for no discernible reason.)

A Boy, wide-eyed and clearly unimpressed, tilts his head. “Why did they build it?”

Prophet (placing the soccer ball gently onto the ground): “To hold their fear. But fear is like pudding—it doesn’t last.”

(The boy nods solemnly. The soccer ball rolls itself back into the game, immediately scoring a goal, causing pigeons to perform synchronized backflips in celebration.)

Act IV: Grief and The Prophet’s Presence

Weber, a Historian, appears on stage, furiously scribbling notes about defiant soccer balls while dodging random pigeons. He’s consumed by observing grief, as though it’s an uncomfortable art installation. In Newark, murals of Assata Shakur burst with colors so defiant they punch time itself.

Families sit at dinner tables where chairs remain empty, the tables covered in photos that inexplicably hum “Amazing Grace.” Weber whispers to himself like an overly dramatic poet: “Grief doesn’t whisper—it screams politely.”

(Enter The Prophet, holding a candle that randomly changes colors and plays “Twinkle Twinkle Little Star” on loop.)

Prophet (comforting a woman holding a photograph that occasionally glows): “Because they feared his light. But light can’t be stopped—it reaches beyond walls, mango tariffs, and poorly timed musical interludes!”

(Cue kazoo fanfare. A pigeon wearing a monocle salutes dramatically before flying into a nearby mango cart.)

Act V: Stories of Defiance

The Prophet now paces in front of Fort Marion, where Osceola’s footsteps echo loudly despite no actual sound being present. Chains scrape floors unnecessarily loudly, and the walls themselves seem to sigh, annoyed by their existence.

Prophet (pointing at invisible chains in the air): “Even in chains, freedom isn’t something you ask for—it’s something you claim, like a free mango with purchase.”

Meanwhile, Assata Shakur’s legacy sets murals ablaze in Newark, where her name stretches across graffiti-laden walls that hum the chorus of “We Will Survive” unironically. Weber, now covered in mango juice, scribbles furiously as The Prophet brushes his fingers against the vibrant paint.

Prophet (eyes glowing mysteriously for comedic effect): “Her words reach still. They are a flame that no wall can contain. Except maybe a really tiny wall—like one built by pigeons.”

(The curtains fall as Weber begins chasing a soccer ball possessed by defiance itself, and a mango cart inexplicably bursts into applause.)

Epilogue: Chaos and Hope

As dawn breaks, mango inflation decreases slightly, causing pigeons to celebrate with synchronized kazoo performances. The Prophet, watching from the shadows while inexplicably surrounded by floating mangoes, murmurs: “Every chain rusts. Every wall cracks. And sometimes… the pigeons win.”

(Cue crescendo as a pigeon wearing tiny sunglasses moonwalks offstage. The curtains fall dramatically.)

**Ministry of Misplaced Justice and Utterly
Bonkers Prophecy**

Act I: Weber’s Study of Confusing Maps and Sentient Artifacts**

The stage opens on Weber’s study, an overly dramatic labyrinth of maps, artifacts, and at least three unmarked boxes of snacks. The maps are inexplicably animated—one of them raises a hand and waves politely. Another bursts into song before quietly folding itself back into submission.

Weber sits at his desk, furrowing his brow as he pokes at a piece of paper that quivers nervously under his pen. Nearby, a suspicious box filled with random prisoner belongings growls softly. Inside are objects of questionable significance: a scarf that begins unraveling as though it has places to be and a locket whose missing photograph might—or might not—be vacationing in Hawaii.

Weber scribbles dramatically on his notepad: “What if justice didn’t punish but healed?” He underlines the word “healed” with such force that his pen screams and bursts into flames.

Weber (frustrated, to no one in particular): “Oh, come on! That was my last working pen!”

(Enter The Prophet—via the window, because doors are for peasants. He appears accompanied by a kazoo orchestra playing an off-key version of "Ride of the Valkyries.”)

Prophet (surveying the chaos while stepping directly into a box of cookies): “What you imagine isn’t impossible... unless it involves teleporting marmots. Then we’ve got issues.”

Weber stares at him, wide-eyed, as though his library just started moonwalking. “But how do we even begin?!”

Prophet (tapping Weber’s notebook with his staff, which promptly emits glitter): “By walking where they walked... preferably not while wearing banana-shaped shoes.”

Weber looks down, realizing he is, in fact, wearing banana-shaped shoes. (Cue embarrassed kazoo solo.) The Prophet exits by doing a backflip into the curtains.

Act II: A Future Beyond Rationality (Also, Oslo)

The cobblestones of Oslo are suspiciously clean, almost unnaturally sparkly. Birds sing cheerfully, though one of them is clearly tone-deaf and keeps squawking the opening notes of Beethoven’s Fifth. Weber stands frozen as a Little Girl approaches her father, clutching a doll so dilapidated it may have been through at least two world wars. She squeezes his hand so hard that the entire street gasps audibly.

The Prophet reappears, riding a unicycle made of questionably ripe bananas. His crooked staff hums ominously, occasionally shooting out bubbles for comedic effect.

Prophet (gesturing grandly at the reintegration center): “Justice doesn’t need punishment—it needs... glitter cannons! No, wait, I mean it needs healing. And glitter cannons wouldn’t hurt.”

Weber, who has somehow been holding an invisible notebook this entire time, mutters to himself, “This doesn’t feel historically accurate.”

The Prophet, ignoring him entirely, begins planting plastic flowers in the cobblestones. “Hope grows wherever it pleases!” he declares. “Even if it's surrounded by pigeons.” (Spotlight on pigeons doing synchronized ballet in the background.)

Act III: Ghosts, Oranges, and Defiant Walls in Manila

Cut to the cracked ruins of Carcel de Bilibid, which look as though they’re held together purely by spite. Moss clings dramatically to the walls, while an orange vendor nearby rearranges her wares with the precision of a high-stakes chess match. Enter Weber, stumbling cautiously over cobblestones that emit the occasional “ouch.”

The Prophet appears out of thin air, holding a coffee cup labeled “World’s Greatest Time Traveler.” He places his palm on the stone, which promptly begins to giggle.

Prophet (to the wall, sternly): “They built you out of fear. But you don’t have to act so moody.”

The wall humphs loudly in response.

Nearby, the Orange Vendor raises a skeptical eyebrow. “Hope? Here? Have you seen my profits lately?”

Prophet (turning to her with exaggerated determination): “Hope grows wherever it pleases—like uninvited houseplants!”

(The wall immediately sprouts daisies. Everyone pretends not to notice.)

Act IV: They Took Her! And Also the Prophet’s Kazoo

A bustling town square bursts into action. An Activist appears at the corner, standing atop a suspiciously large barrel labeled "Emergency Pancakes." She points dramatically to a wall covered in graffiti that reads: “Down with Mango Tyranny!”

Activist (shouting to the heavens): “They took her! But SHE ESCAPED!”

(Cue dramatic thunder and at least four pigeons fainting for no particular reason.) The crowd erupts into cheers so loud they summon The Prophet, who descends from the sky on a parachute made entirely of twinkling Christmas lights.

Prophet (calmly, while inspecting the barrel): “Grief binds them. And barrels full of pancakes also help.”

Weber, now entirely bewildered and holding what appears to be a mango-shaped kazoo, asks, “How do we keep going?”

Prophet (tapping his staff, which emits celebratory confetti): “By refusing to forget. And by eating pancakes. Lots of pancakes.”

Act V: Panama’s Echoes and Soccer Balls of Wisdom

The stage shifts to the crumbling barracks of Panama City. Children laugh as they chase a soccer ball that occasionally emits random quotes from Shakespeare. The ball skids to a stop at The Prophet’s feet, who picks it up reverently.

Prophet (to the soccer ball): “Everything built to dominate crumbles. Except you, apparently.”

(The soccer ball glows briefly before rolling itself back into the game. The children cheer as pigeons join the match.)

A Boy tugs on The Prophet’s robes. “Why did they build it?” he asks, curiosity flickering in his eyes.

Prophet (sighing dramatically): “To hold their fear. But fear is like pudding—it wiggles and doesn’t last.”

(A rogue kazoo solo signals the end of the scene, as pigeons attempt to steal the spotlight one last time.)

Epilogue: Glitter Cannons of Hope

The stage fades to a quiet scene: The Prophet standing under a streetlight, surrounded by inexplicably floating mangos. He whispers to himself, “Grief is direction. And love... love is a flamingo on a motorcycle.”

(Final kazoo crescendo as the mangos burst into glitter, and the curtains fall.)
