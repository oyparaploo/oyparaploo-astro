The Milk Works Hard

Words / 2025 Variety

The milk works hard at being our perfect mundane failure.
Every morning it offers itself up for judgment—
still good? already turned? that uncertain zone
where we sniff three times, each braver than the last,
as if courage could reverse spoilage.

Milk is universal. Even those who never drink it
understand the weight of "buy milk" on a list,
the way it sits there patient as a priest,
forgiving us for forgetting, blessing us
with another chance to forget tomorrow.

Although some people forgot to buy it,
others bought three gallons, forgetting they already had two.
Both failures feed the same machine—
too much, too little, never just enough,
milk becoming the measure of our miscalculations.

It spoils on a timeline that mirrors good intentions going bad:
hopeful on Monday, suspicious by Thursday,
definitely dead by Saturday when you finally needed it.
Unlike rice collecting dust, unlike pasta waiting years,
milk forces us into cycles—buying, consuming, forgetting, spoiling, buying again.
The domestic wheel that turns whether we're watching or not.

There is something about milk. It's basic, primal,
the first thing we ever tasted,
yet we're constantly reinventing it with almonds,
oats that never lactated, soybeans pretending,
cashews somehow creamy, coconuts split open
to pour out their not-quite-milk milk.
As if by multiplying the options
we could solve the fundamental problem
of remembering to buy any of them.

Expired milk is a morning betrayal of the specific kind:
you're barely awake, making coffee,
and suddenly you're confronted with evidence—
last Tuesday's optimism gone sour,
time made visible through something that has become spoiled.
The date stamp an accusation,
the smell a confession
of all the days you meant to stop at the store.

Milk becomes shorthand for all basic maintenance tasks:
batteries dead when you need them
(the smoke detector chirping at 3 AM),
a lightbulb burnt out for over a week
(you reach for the switch anyway, every time),
checks that didn't get deposited
(folded in three wallets, growing soft as cloth).
Each failure small enough to ignore,
together building a cathedral of postponement.

Milk happens to also become a sort of anchor—
not holding us steady but dragging us
through the same water, week after week.
We know this ocean: the dairy aisle at 9 PM,
standing in the cold fluorescent light,
trying to remember if what we have at home
is still good. It isn't. We know it isn't.
We buy the milk anyway, or we don't,
and both choices lead to the same place:
standing here again next week,
anchored to our beautiful, unbreakable inadequacy.

The milk works hard at teaching us who we are—
creatures of intention and forgetting,
of lists and losing lists,
of promises to ourselves at 6:30 AM
that spoil by Thursday like everything does,
like we knew they would,
like we'll let them spoil again
because this too is a kind of faith:
believing tomorrow's milk will be different,
believing we'll remember this time,
believing the cycle might break
even as we're buying, consuming, forgetting,
spoiling, buying again.
