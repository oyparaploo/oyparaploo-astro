Standing in the Shrinking Circle While Systems That Never Deserved to Exist Begin to Collapse

← Words / 2025 Variety

Understanding the emotional core beneath questions teaches us something important about how worry and fear disguise themselves as practical concerns. When we pause to really listen, we discover that questions can circle around terrifying ideas, orbiting them like worried planets around an unstable sun.

Consider what happens when we imagine a vulnerable person who might lose all of their livelihood this afternoon. There's something achingly specific about that temporal marker—"this afternoon"—isn't there? It reminds us that catastrophe doesn't always announce itself with trumpets and warnings. Sometimes it arrives quietly, on an ordinary Tuesday, in an email marked "urgent" or a phone call that comes at 2:47 PM.

And yet, beneath even this immediate panic lies something more philosophical, more persistent. We're asking ourselves whether caring deeply about craft still matters in a world that might not reward it anymore. This question haunts us not because we doubt our own commitment, but because we're watching the world's commitment to us waver and fade.

Think about who bears this weight most heavily. Empath government critics and artists represent people who do careful, often invisible work that holds other work together. These are the ones who notice the cracks before they become chasms, who speak uncomfortable truths when silence would be more comfortable, more profitable, more safe.

Here's what we must understand: These aren't always billionaires with golden parachutes and offshore accounts. No, these are people who exist in the middle spaces, inhabiting that vast, unprotected territory between poverty and security, doing work that feels essential but is too often undervalued. They're the ones who edit until 3 AM to make someone else's words sing. They're the ones who write reports no one will read until something goes terribly wrong. They're the ones who create beauty that won't pay next month's rent.

We are watching the world stop protecting the kind of work we strongly believe in, and that watching itself becomes a kind of slow-motion grief. This is like standing in a shrinking circle of light while darkness creeps closer from every direction. You can see it happening. You can measure the diminishing radius. You can feel the temperature dropping as the warmth retreats.

It is about wondering whether the skills we have honed over years and decades, the ethics we have maintained even when compromise would have been easier, and the truths we attempt to purge will be enough to keep us and our kindreds safe. That word "kindreds" matters, doesn't it? Because this isn't just individual survival we're worried about—it's collective survival, the survival of everyone who shares our values and vulnerabilities.

And all of this becomes even more urgent when the systems that supposedly value them begin to collapse. Not "if they collapse" but "when," because we can already hear the groaning of the infrastructure, can already see the way institutions look away when we ask for help.

Because of systems fear of losing something, we arrive at a bitter irony. Often what the system fears losing is an insidious colonial power that shouldn't have existed in the first place—a power built on extraction and exploitation, on valuing profit over people, on rewarding ruthlessness while punishing care. And yet here we are, casualties of a system that's rotting from within, wondering if anything that matters will survive the collapse.
