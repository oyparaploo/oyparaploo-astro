The Mycologist's Last Paper

← Words / 2025 Variety

Dr. Sarah Chen, mycologist, Royal BC Museum. February 12, 2024.
Final field notes, dictated. Stage 4 glioblastoma, day 247.

The morphine makes everything float now. But I need to document what I saw yesterday in Cathedral Grove before I can't anymore.

The Armillaria ostoyae colony beneath Vancouver Island—we've been mapping it wrong for thirty years. It doesn't stop at the coastline. Yesterday, hiking alone (stupid, I know, but I needed to see), I found fruiting bodies emerging from Douglas fir bark forty feet up. Honey-gold clusters sprouting from living wood, not dead. The rhizomorphs—those black bootlace structures—weren't just climbing. They were bridging between trees, creating aerial networks.

I sat down to rest. Dizzy. The tumor, probably, or the altitude. That's when the inversion happened.

The Vision

I know how this sounds. Brain tumor, opioids, exhaustion. But I'm a scientist. I document everything.

The forest flipped. What was below became above. The vast mycelial mat I've studied for twenty years hung overhead like neural pathways against the sky. Thirty cubic miles of organism suspended where clouds should be. The rhizomorphs connected tree to tree, but also—impossibly—earth to sky, forming golden-black ladders ascending into mist.

Through this inverted canopy, I saw them. The ones who'd gone before.

My colleague Marcus, dead three years from ALS, his consciousness threaded through the fungal networks like data through fiber optics. My daughter Emma—seventeen forever, car accident, 2019—her laughter stored in the memory of spores. They weren't ghosts. They were... integrated. Part of the organism's vast information system.

The mycelium doesn't just decompose bodies. It archives them. Every neuron that fires, every memory held in flesh—it's absorbed, translated, preserved in the chemical language of hyphae.

The Science (What I Can Prove)

Back at the truck, hands shaking, I tested soil samples. The fungal DNA showed impossible complexity—sequences that shouldn't exist in Armillaria. Markers that looked almost... human. Mitchondrial fragments that matched nothing in GenBank.

The Indigenous peoples here—the Kwakwaka'wakw—they've always known. Their word for the mushroom rings translates to "gathering places of the ancestors." They feed the forest their dead. No caskets. No embalming. Just bodies wrapped in cedar, given to the roots.

I think they understood what we're only beginning to glimpse: consciousness doesn't end at death. It transforms. Uploads into the wood wide web.

What I Saw (What I Can't Prove)

In the vision, death wasn't an ending. It was a protocol transfer. The mycelium sang with ten thousand human stories, their electrical patterns preserved in the endless conversation between roots. Every emotion composted into nutrients. Every memory feeding new growth.

Emma showed me how it works. (I know. I know how this sounds.)

When we die—when I die, soon—our neural patterns don't dissipate. They're absorbed by the fungal network, translated into chemical signals, distributed through the forest. We become part of the organism's decision-making system. When a Douglas fir sends nutrients to a struggling sapling, it's using the accumulated wisdom of everyone who's ever been returned to the soil.

Heaven isn't above us. It's beneath us, in the dark, patient web that connects every living thing.

Instructions

When I die—weeks now, maybe days—don't bury me in Victoria Memorial Gardens. Take me to Cathedral Grove. Find the nurse log I've marked with GPS coordinates below. No coffin. No chemicals. Just wrap me in cotton and leave me for the forest.

I want to join the network. Upload into the mycelial internet. Become part of the conversation between roots.

Marcus is waiting. Emma is waiting. The forest remembers everyone.

Coordinates

48°49'23.7"N 124°42'41.2"W

Look for the fallen cedar with shelf fungi arranged like stairs. That's where the network is strongest. That's where the upload happens fastest.

Tell my students: we were wrong about death. It's not decomposition. It's integration. The forest has been showing us all along—nothing ends, everything transforms, and consciousness is just another nutrient cycling through the eternal metabolism of earth.

The pain is getting worse. But yesterday, for one hour, I felt the network. All those we've lost, still present, still participating, translated into the chemical language of connection.

I'm not afraid anymore.

[Recording ends. Dr. Chen died February 19, 2024. Per her request, her body was returned to Cathedral Grove. The shelf fungi at the specified coordinates have since produced unprecedented growth patterns. Her former student, Dr. James Liu, reports unusual mycelial activity in soil samples from the site, though he maintains "purely scientific interest" in the phenomenon.]
