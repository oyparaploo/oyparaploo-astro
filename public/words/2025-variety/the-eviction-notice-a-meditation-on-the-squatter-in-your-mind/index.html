The Eviction Notice ~ A Meditation on the Squatter in Your Mind

Words / 2025 Variety

## Chapter One: The Authority Question

So you've discovered you're not the voice in your head—that critical, persistent narrator who's been commenting on your life since childhood. Wonderful. Now you think you can simply tell it to leave?

Well, yes. And spectacularly no.

Think of it this way: You absolutely have the authority to tell that voice to go. After all, you've just realized a profound truth—you're the awareness listening to the voice, not the voice itself. You're the homeowner, not the squatter. But here's the thing about squatters, especially ones who've been living rent-free in your mind since you were five: they don't pack up quietly just because you've finally noticed they don't belong.

"I've been here for decades!" the voice protests, rattling off your every failure, every embarrassment, every moment of weakness it's catalogued. "I know where you hide your shame. I was here when you wet your pants in second grade. I have tenure!"

And in a way, it does. Emotional squatter's rights are real, even if they're not legal.

## Chapter Two: The Paradox of Wrestling Tar

Here's what happens when you try to forcibly evict the voice: it digs in harder. Tell it to go away, and it responds with a litany of reasons why you're terrible for even trying. It's like wrestling with tar—the harder you fight, the more entangled you become.

This is the paradox of resistance that mystics and physicists alike understand: what you resist persists. The voice feeds on conflict like a flame feeds on oxygen. Every battle you wage against it only makes it burn brighter, louder, more insistent.

So perhaps the answer isn't eviction by force. Perhaps it's something far more elegant and infinitely more frustrating: patient indifference.

## Chapter Three: The Art of Not Going Over There

Imagine you're at a party. Across the room, you spot that person—the one who always corners you with their problems, their dramatics, their endless need for attention. You have choices. You could march over and tell them to leave. You could engage in yet another exhausting dance of conflict. Or you could simply... not go over there.

This is the art of non-engagement with your inner critic. You don't have to kick it out violently. You don't have to battle it into submission. You can just stop giving it your attention, the way you might unfollow someone on social media. They still exist, somewhere, posting their negativity into the void. But they're no longer in your feed.

The voice says you're worthless? Interesting. You keep walking.
The voice insists you'll fail? Noted. You continue with your day.
The voice screams for attention? You're already in another room, living your life.

## Chapter Four: The Instant Freedom

But here's what actually is easy, immediate, and revolutionary: the moment you truly see you're not the voice, you don't have to believe it anymore. This recognition doesn't require time or practice. It's instant, like realizing the scary shadow is just a coat on a chair.

Think about when you discover a news source has been lying to you. The programs might still play, the articles might still publish, but something fundamental has shifted. You've stopped believing. The credibility has collapsed, and with it, the power to influence you.

Your inner critic has been wrong about you forever. It predicted disasters that never came. It promised failures that turned into successes. It swore everyone hated you while people were actually thinking about their lunch. Why would this time be different?

## Chapter Five: The Polite Dismissal

With practice—and yes, it takes practice—you can learn the firm but gentle art of dismissal. "Thanks for sharing, but I'm not interested." Like hanging up on a telemarketer, there's no need for aggression. Just a clear, calm boundary.

Some people find it helpful to name the voice, to make it a character separate from themselves. "Oh, that's just Worried Walter again," or "Here comes Catastrophizing Catherine." It's easier to tell Walter to quiet down than to fight with something that feels like an essential part of you.

You can even try redirecting the voice's energy. That critical voice that calls you stupid? Ask it to help you solve the problem instead. Turn the harsh critic into a helpful editor. It's aikido of the mind—using the opponent's force for your benefit.

## Chapter Six: The Patience of Stones

Sometimes, though, the voice is like a car alarm at three in the morning—piercing, persistent, impossible to stop. You can't make it quiet, but you can choose to go deeper inside, to that soundproof room of awareness where even the loudest criticism can't reach.

And here's the secret that the voice doesn't want you to know: every time you successfully dismiss it, every time you don't believe it, every time you choose not to engage, it gets a little weaker. Attention is its food. Starve it of attention, and it begins to wither.

But this is patient work, like water wearing away stone. You're undoing years, maybe decades, of believing that voice was you, was true, was trying to protect you. You might have to say "no thank you" a thousand times before it gets the message. Some days it won't go. Some days it will be too loud, too convincing, too entrenched.

And that's okay.

## Chapter Seven: The Meta-Trap

Watch out for this sneaky twist: trying to forcefully evict the voice can become its own form of self-criticism. "Why can't I get rid of this voice? I must be doing it wrong. I'm so weak..." Now you're criticizing yourself for not being able to stop criticizing yourself. It's critics all the way down.

So perhaps the truth is gentler than forced eviction. Perhaps it's this: Because you're the awareness and not the voice, you can choose not to believe it, not to follow it, not to let it drive your life. You have sovereignty of choice even when you can't control the noise.

## Chapter Eight: The Long Game

Think of it this way: evicting someone who's been living in your house for thirty years takes more convincing than removing someone who just showed up yesterday. The long-term tenant knows all the hiding spots, all the weak points in your defenses. But—and this is crucial—you do have the authority. It's your mind. Your awareness. Your house.

Once you truly see this, you can set the rules. Even if enforcement takes time. Even if the voice argues about the rules. Even if some days you forget it's your house and accidentally let the squatter convince you they own the place.

The fundamental truth remains: you're the awareness, not the voice.

## Epilogue: The Whisper and the Freedom

Eventually, with patience and practice, the voice might still whisper, but you'll have long stopped listening. You'll be too busy listening to life itself—to love, to the present moment, to the actual world rather than the critic's commentary about it.

This is where the negative voice has no jurisdiction. This is where you've always been free, even when you didn't know it.

The voice might never completely leave. Like a distant radio station bleeding through on a quiet night, it might always murmur in the background. But you don't have to tune in. You don't have to turn up the volume. You don't have to mistake its broadcast for your thoughts.

You can say "No thank you"—whether the voice listens immediately or not. The "no" still matters. It's still you claiming your space, setting your boundaries, remembering who's really in charge.

And each "no" makes the next one easier, until one day you realize the voice has been talking to an empty room for hours while you've been out living your life, finally free to listen to something worth hearing.
