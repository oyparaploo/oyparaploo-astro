The Language of Olive Trees

← Words / 2025 Variety

Part One: The Inheritance

I inherited deafness from birth and art from necessity—Layla Hamidi, born in Gaza City's Shifa Hospital during the 1987 intifada, when tear gas seeped through maternity windows. My grandmother's olive grove in Beit Hanoun became my classroom after the schools couldn't accommodate a deaf child in a city under siege. There, among 400-year-old trees planted by Ottoman ancestors, I learned that communication extends far beyond sound.

October 7, 2023. I stand in what remains of that grove—27 trees from the original 312, the rest bulldozed for "security zones" over three decades. My fingers trace bullet scars in the bark of the surviving matriarch tree, each indentation a Braille message from 2008, 2012, 2014, 2021. Through my feet, I feel the reverberations: another explosion somewhere in Gaza City, the 47th since dawn according to the vibration patterns I've learned to count.

In my satchel: charcoal, paper, and a commission from the Palestinian Museum to document "alternative languages of resistance." What they don't know is that I'm documenting something else—the moment a 2,000-year-old community transforms silence into speech.

Part Two: The Workshop (October 15, 2023)

The community center in Jabaliya refugee camp has no windows—blown out in 2014, never replaced. Better ventilation, the director Jamila says through sign language she learned specifically to communicate with me. Forty-three people have gathered for my "Visualizing Voice" workshop, including seven who are deaf, twelve with PTSD-induced selective mutism, and others who've simply run out of words.

Noor arrives late, seventeen years old, born deaf like me but raised in Jordan where she learned Levantine Sign Language. Her signs are florid, dramatic—each gesture a small performance. "In Amman," she signs, "they pity us. Here, we're just another casualty statistic."

I spread butcher paper across the floor, pass out charcoal. "Draw the sound of your name," I instruct through Jamila's translation. "Not the letters—the feeling of it being called."

Sami, 34, actor before the theaters closed, presses charcoal so hard it snaps. His page shows jagged black lines. "My mother called my name when the wall collapsed," he says. "I couldn't reach her for seven hours." The lines multiply, overlap, darken until the paper tears.

Amina, 41, draws circles—concentric, precise, endless. Former math teacher, now running a support group for women whose husbands are imprisoned. "My name means 'trustworthy,'" she says. "Every circle is another promise I've had to keep alone."

Part Three: The Sound Engineer (October 20, 2023)

Fadi brings equipment salvaged from a wedding DJ business: decibel meter, oscilloscope, laptop with audio software. "I want to show you something," he types on his phone, showing me the screen. He points the meter at the sky, and the needle jumps: 95 decibels. An F-16. He aims it at children playing: 75 decibels. Their laughter barely registers above the ambient noise of generators (70 decibels constant).

On his laptop, he shows me waveforms from the past month—explosions create sharp spikes followed by long trailing echoes. "Look," he types, "you can see the difference between tank shells (sharp peak, quick fade) and airstrikes (sustained rumble, multiple peaks)."

He's been converting these waveforms into visual art, selling prints online to buy medicine. Each explosion becomes a landscape—mountains of sound with valleys of silence. "Your grandmother's olive trees," he types, showing me a gentler waveform. "I recorded them last year. See? They hum at 7.83 Hz—same frequency as Earth itself."

Part Four: The Mural (October 25, 2023)

The wall runs 47 meters along Al-Wehda Street—blast barrier concrete, erected after the 2014 war. The municipality approved my mural proposal: "Voices of Gaza." What they don't know is that I'm encoding actual messages into the design.

I grid the wall, assign sections to workshop participants. Noor takes the far left, painting her sign language name in sweeping blues and gold—gestures frozen mid-motion. Sami creates a theater of shadows, silhouettes of actors whose mouths open onto gardens instead of words. Amina's circles become a fibonacci spiral, each ring containing a mathematical equation that, when solved, spells out names of the imprisoned.

Karim, 73, arrives with a box of objects: his father's 1948 house key, a dried olive branch, a broken pocket watch stopped at 2:15 (the moment of his village's evacuation). "Paint these," he says through his granddaughter's translation. "Let the wall remember what they want us to forget."

By noon, we have an audience. Children trace the patterns with their fingers, unconsciously learning the visual language we're creating. A grandmother points to Noor's gestures: "I know that movement," she says. "My deaf sister made it when she was happy."

Part Five: The Archive (October 30, 2023)

In my apartment—two rooms in Gaza City's Rimal neighborhood—I've assembled an archive. 2,847 drawings from workshop participants over five years. Each one a translation: sound into sight, trauma into art, silence into testimony.

I spread them chronologically across the floor. 2018: mostly dark, heavy strokes. 2021: fragmentation, broken lines. 2023: something new emerging—interconnected patterns, shared visual vocabularies. We're developing a pidgin language of trauma and resistance.

Rasha arrives with her contribution: a series of self-portraits where her scars become Arabic calligraphy, each mark transcribed with a word it taught her. "Patience." "Fury." "Boundary." She's 29, survivor of domestic abuse, now teaching art therapy at the women's shelter.

"We're not just documenting," she says, watching me photograph each drawing. "We're building a new language for those who come after. One that includes everyone—the deaf, the traumatized, the silenced."

Part Six: The Poetry Night (November 2, 2023)

The gathering is illegal—more than 20 people requires a permit we'll never receive. But they come anyway, 67 souls crowding into the courtyard behind the abandoned UNRWA school. Someone has strung lights powered by car batteries. The olive trees—three survivors from a grove that once held hundreds—cast shadows that dance with each gust of wind.

Jamal performs first, his poem a hybrid of spoken Arabic, Palestinian Sign Language, and gesture. Even I, fully deaf, understand through the visual rhythm, the way his body becomes a instrument. Noor translates for me later: "From the ashes of fire, our hearts ignite / In whispered verses, we reclaim the night."

A child, maybe seven, stands to recite numbers: "1,948. 1,967. 2,000. 2,023." Each year punctuated by a gesture learned from our workshop—hands spreading wide (explosion), gathering close (embrace), reaching up (hope), pressing down (grief). The audience understands without translation.

Part Seven: The News (November 5, 2023)

Jamila's hands shake as she signs the news: "The community center. Direct hit. Three killed—the night guard, his son, a woman seeking shelter."

We arrive to find our mural intact but the building behind it gone. The wall stands alone now, our visual voices speaking to rubble. Noor kneels where the workshop room was, her hands moving through signs I don't recognize—a prayer, perhaps, or a promise.

Fadi sets up his equipment, measures the silence: 32 decibels, quieter than it's been in years. "Even the generators stopped," he types. We stand in this unnatural quiet, feeling the absence of life where life had been hours before.

Part Eight: What Remains (November 10, 2023)

The olive grove in Beit Hanoun is gone. Not gradually this time—overnight, bulldozed for a "temporary security corridor." I arrive to find stumps and sawdust, the 400-year-old matriarch reduced to rings I count through tears: 427, exactly. Each ring a year of Palestinian presence on this land.

But in my bag, I have something: a cutting from the matriarch, taken months ago, now sprouting roots in a yogurt container. Karim knows someone with protected agricultural land near Khan Younis. "We'll plant it," he says. "Let it grow where they can't reach."

That evening, our informal network—now 134 people across Gaza—shares drawings through WhatsApp when the internet works. A new visual vocabulary spreading: spirals for grief, parallel lines for separation, dots for presence despite absence. We're teaching it to children, to elders, to anyone who needs a language beyond words.

Part Nine: The Dream Translation (November 15, 2023)

In the dream, Gaza speaks in colors. The sea: turquoise statements of fact. The camps: sepia questions without answers. The farms: green promises, some kept, some broken. I wake and paint it—not the dream itself but its grammar, the syntax of a city translating itself into survival.

Noor finds me at the mural wall, adds her own dream in indigo gestures. Soon others come, adding layers. The wall becomes a palimpsest of visions, each one asking: "What would we say if we could say everything?"

An old woman touches the wall, traces Sami's theater of shadows. "My son was an actor," she says to no one in particular. "Killed in 2009. He would have loved this—art that speaks when voices can't."

Epilogue: The New Language (December 1, 2023)

I document what we've built: a visual-gestural language born from necessity, growing from trauma, reaching toward hope. Not sign language exactly, not purely visual art, but something between—a communication system for a community that's learned to speak through siege.

The Palestinian Museum wants a formal report. I send them this instead: 500 drawings, 50 hours of video (hands moving, walls painted, trees remembered), and a lexicon—237 gestures and marks that constitute our emerging vocabulary.

But the real document lives in the bodies of those who've learned it. Noor teaching children to sign their dreams. Sami performing silent theater that everyone understands. Amina encoding messages in mathematical spirals. Fadi translating explosion into art. Each person becoming a living archive of alternative resistance.

The olive tree cutting has grown three inches, its leaves reaching toward light through a kitchen window. I water it with tears and hope, teaching it the language we're creating—one that remembers everything while imagining what we might still become.

Note: Gaza's population includes approximately 35,000 people with disabilities, many deaf or hearing-impaired due to conflict-related injuries. The siege has created unique challenges for alternative communication, spurring innovative forms of expression and resistance that deserve documentation and preservation.
