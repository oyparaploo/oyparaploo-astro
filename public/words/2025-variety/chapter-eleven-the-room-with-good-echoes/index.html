Chapter Eleven ~ The Room with Good Echoes

Words / 2025 Variety

Daylight arrived with a bruise-yellowed edge, like it had been handled roughly on its way through the clouds. The river kept its throat low. The construction site behind them had fallen back into its ordinary clanks and cautions, as if night rehearsals were a rumor daylight refused to credit.

They found a table in a café that had once been a locksmith’s and never quite shook the habit of oil. Cups clicked; a spoon spun and found its balance. The room had the good kind of echo—soft, wood-backed, the kind that admits you and doesn’t magnify your mistakes.

Libaux Torrini folded himself into the chair across from Troll Tearsen with the careful economy of a man storing energy for later. He took out a notepad; the paper rasped once—rrrsk—and then behaved. Troll placed the cloth bag of marbles on the table and let them roll out into light: Pigalle’s clear, the archive’s red and cracked, the Metro’s blue, the courier’s white-coiled. They looked less like clues and more like punctuation, marks in a sentence still deciding its grammar.

He started to speak. She lifted a hand.

Troll unlocked her phone.

Two unheard voicemails from Miyake Coscia. She tapped the first.

Static, the soft inhale of a machine. Miyake’s voice, hushed and steady: “She’s been sleeping, Troll. We’re keeping the room quiet. When she wakes, she asks the time like it’s an old song. I tell her it’s morning, then afternoon, then evening. She smiled when I said your name. If you can come, come. If you cannot, speak her name where you are. Find a room with good echoes.”

A cup set down at the counter made a distant tok. Someone folded a paper; the page sighed.

Troll pressed the second message. No static this time, or perhaps it was there and she couldn’t hear it over her own pulse.

“Troll. It was very gentle. She breathed, and then she didn’t. There was no pain that we could find. The window was cracked and a breeze made the curtains a little theater. I told her about the marbles. I told her you are listening for her. Take your time to come. Or not. We are here either way.”

The message ended because seconds end. The room held still, like someone had asked the air to stand.

Troll put the phone face down. The case met wood—tuk—and stayed. She did not look at Libaux. She looked at the blue marble and saw a sky she had not stood under in years. She saw a shelf with a dust ring where a jar had sat.

Libaux tried a sentence, then trimmed it. “I’m sorry,” he said, simple, correct. He reached across the table and set two fingers near her wrist, not touching, a steadying presence a bridge length away. He left them there long enough to count as human and not long enough to become a claim.

She nodded once. The nod echoed in her body like a small stone dropped into a well. “She would ask me to work,” Troll said, and then found the next word carefully, “now.”

“Then we work,” he said, but it came out softer than his usual iron.

The café’s door chimed and shut. A baby cried and was soothed with an old rhythm. Someone’s laughter went up a tone too high and fell back where it belonged.

Troll set the recorder on the table and pressed its red button. She leaned toward the mic and said her mother’s name. The word went into the machine and into the café’s air and into the wood of the table and into the hollows of her bones. The recorder’s light remained indifferent. The room returned the name as a quieter, older version, the way good rooms do.

“Good echoes,” she said.

Libaux nodded. “Good echoes.”

They sat with it until it felt like sitting with a person, not a void.

---

Work reassembled itself the way tools do after a storm. Libaux slid his pad across. On the page: a circle, rough pencil compass, with dots sketched at uneven intervals.

“The marbles in the tower,” he said. “Chalk marks. I drew it when we got back, best as I could recall.”

Troll traced the circle without touching paper. “A clock?”

He shook his head. “Too many marks. Too few in places that matter. But look—” He added tiny arrows where she had mentioned feeling air. “Hidden vents. He used fans to nudge them in sequence. The sound grew because the sequence did, not because the marbles learned greed.”

She pulled one of their marbles into the circle and tapped it gently, letting it kiss the paper’s edge. The café’s plates, somewhere behind them, answered with their own porcelain consonants. “Sequence spells something if we know the alphabet,” she said.

“The alphabet is surfaces,” he replied. “Wood in the hall, concrete in the stairwell, steel in the tower. Each returns a different pitch. If you map the order of materials to notes—”

“And the notes to something else,” Troll said. “Numbers, letters.”

His phone buzzed. Mage Meuller.

> Analyzed last night’s tower capture. That looped “tik” had a reverb tail of 1.8–2.1 seconds with a flutter around 23 Hz—typical of tall hollow cores. But more interesting: the sequence of collisions you sent me (thank you for the phone memo) maps, when you treat pitch as digits, to 7-3-2-1-9-5-8-4. Cross-referenced against your earlier “RIVAGE.” Check RIVAGE keypad map. 742243 = RIVAGE. Your sequence begins with 7-3. Could be RIVAGE + something. Check any “Fondation Rivage” presence in Paris.

Libaux whistled once, a single soft note. “He’s signing with marbles again. RIVAGE never stopped.”

Troll opened her email. There was a message from Aïcha with no subject, only an attachment. She clicked. A slick invitation loaded slowly, blurry at first, then smugly crisp: Fondation Rivage – Forum sur la Protection Transfrontalière – Quai de la Rapée, Entrepôt B – 19:00. A date. Tonight.

She exhaled through her teeth. “He likes calendars more than clocks.”

Libaux read over her shoulder. “Public. Funders. A warehouse by the river. An audience that claws at words like ‘oversight’ until they make no sound.”

“And a room we can map,” Troll said. “Quay. Iron. Water.”

Her phone buzzed again. Uma Arouje: You are recalled. Immediate. Vienna needs you in the room tomorrow at 10:00. No exceptions.

Troll stared at the message until the words separated into individual letters and then reassembled. Libaux watched her face the way a medic watches color.

“We buy twenty-four hours,” he said. “We’ve borrowed more for less.”

“She’ll staple me to a chair,” Troll said, but not to argue. To mark the cost.

“Then we tear the paper.”

He typed a reply that said nothing and everything: En route. He did not send it.

---

They left the café and walked through a day that had decided to be ordinary. A cyclist cursed into a parked van. A crate of peaches made a soft collective thud on a stall. A dog’s nails wrote small temporary hieroglyphs on wet stone and then edited itself to grass. The city’s undernote—transformers, trains under concrete ribs, wires working—made a steady mmm like a held organ key.

Troll’s mind had its own undernote now. Each time quiet threatened to claim a corner, her mother’s name moved there first and arranged it.

They cleaned up and came back to the safehouse long enough to wash and to be still. The radio hissed because it had not been told to quit. On the plate, the marbles looked like well-behaved planets. Troll rolled each across the table’s edge, listening: clear with a high, clean tik; red with a faint bruise at the end; cracked with an aftertaste of tremble; blue with a modest bell; white-coil with a whisper before it spoke.

“Signatures,” she said.

Libaux held the recorder close. “He’s counting on us to hear his autograph and applaud.”

“Then we clap in the wrong places,” she said.

A new message arrived—Helene Klausmun—steady text from the mountains:

> Word reached me about your mother. I am sorry. We held the scene here this morning—another bus shelter, no body this time, only a marble left on the bench. No fingerprints. I think he wanted you to hear that we are not finished either side of the border. I will keep the tape. When you return, I will be in the room.

Troll read it twice. The room seemed to shift and allow more chairs. She typed Thank you and then left it at that because her throat did not trust complexity.

“Rivage at nineteen,” Libaux said. “Warehouse B. We go as if we belong.”

“And if the room tries to teach us our part again?”

“We improvise.”

“You dance poorly,” she said, to tilt the mood a degree and see if it would hold.

“I step on toes with conviction,” he admitted.

They both almost smiled. The radio took that fraction and hid it for later.

---

Evening leaned in sooner than was polite. The Quai de la Rapée ran a hard line along the water; warehouses held their bulk against the quay like stubborn barges. Entrepôt B was dressed for philanthropy: banners with fonts that promised care, glass rented to privilege, a small stage built of portable sincerity. The river, unimpressed, made its hush against stone.

Inside: the hum of funded talk. Lanyards chimed. Microphones were tested with one, two and then again one, two as if the numbers had improved in the interim. Oversight. Stakeholders. Transparency. The air carried the clean exhausted smell of industrial cleaner and the faint paper-dust of brochures.

Troll and Libaux moved along the perimeter, spectators pretending to be late arrivals deciding where to sit. They marked vents, mics, speakers: the usual paths sound takes when it wants to be official. On a side wall, a sculpture no one had asked for—a loop of clear spheres (glass or acrylic) threaded into a knot. People stood near it and took small exhalations of awe, anxious to behave correctly near Art.

“Marble costume,” Libaux muttered.

At the back, a service corridor sighed. They followed its sigh to a door the color of permission. Inside: a loading bay scrubbed for the night, pallets stacked as if awaiting choreography. The acoustics were boxy—tok answers, short, profane.

Troll set the recorder on a crate and said her mother’s name again. The room gave it back quickly, as if it were busy and wanted to be helpful. She closed her eyes and let the echo draw a line between rooms: hospice; café; here. Good echoes, even in ugly spaces.

Footsteps. Not hurried. A man stepped from the shadow between pallets with the self-forgiveness of someone expected to be seen. Not Stremler. Younger, smoother; the face of “liaison” printed on a business card that didn’t require a surname.

“The stage is that way,” he said, smiling to indicate that he had delivered a joke with all necessary courtesy.

“We never sit close,” Libaux answered, his voice friendly enough to suggest teeth.

The man’s smile did not alter. “Then you’ll watch from here.” He glanced at the recorder, then at the marbles in Troll’s palm. His gaze passed through them without snagging. “You’ve been collecting punctuation.”

“Waiting for a sentence,” Troll said.

“It’s being written,” he replied. “Your attendance is a kindness.”

He left without giving them anything to chase. They listened to his steps travel through the corridor, not hurried, then diluted by carpet and chatter.

Libaux exhaled. “Prop master.”

“Stage manager,” Troll said. “He likes the left hand deciding what the right applauds.”

She slid the recorder into her coat and closed her fist around the marbles. The room warmed her knuckles. On the main floor, someone introduced someone with the voice of a person paid to introduce. The crowd performed attention.

“Shall we go hear the program notes?” Libaux asked.

Troll looked once at the river through the loading dock’s half-open door. The water wore night without arguing. She said her mother’s name again, quietly, and the warehouse let it be a small, stubborn thing that refused to be improved by amplification.

“Let’s,” she said.

They stepped toward the light, where microphones make sentences sound trustworthy and glass stones catch enough illumination to be mistaken for stars.
