Brrd Roots ~ United States Revolutionary War Story ~ Version 7

Words / 2025 Variety

The Revolutionary War, already steeped in absurdities and the surreal nature of conflict, offers fertile ground for weaving humor reminiscent of George Saunders and Monty Python. Here's how the narrative could play out:

The war, as history tells it, was madness dressed in woolen coats and powdered wigs. It was survival by sheer audacity—the audacity to hope, to connect, and occasionally, to laugh in the face of a universe so warped it seemed satirical. Take Valley Forge, for instance.

The soldiers, stranded between frostbite and existential crisis, huddled in their half-built cabins. The wind howled through cracks in the wooden walls, whispering insults that seemed specifically targeted. “You call this a cabin?” it sneered.

“Do you hear that?” a coughing man asked, his blanket doing more for his imagination than his warmth.

His comrade, a wide-eyed farmer who'd traded his pitchfork for a musket, nodded solemnly. “The snow,” he said, “is plotting against us.”

“We should surrender now,” the coughing man replied, his voice drenched in melodrama. “To the snow. Maybe it’ll spare us.”

Across the room, another soldier stirred a pot of water—a pot so small it looked like it had been stolen from a dollhouse. “Morale soup!” he announced brightly. “Mostly water. Occasionally optimism.”

And to everyone's surprise, they laughed. Not long or loud—just enough to remind themselves they weren’t snowflakes, melting under the pressure.

The march to haul cannons from Fort Ticonderoga was no less absurd. The soldiers dragged sleds across snowdrifts that seemed more suited to a Christmas card than a battlefield. As the wind screamed—probably out of boredom, one soldier suggested—a man stopped mid-step and pointed toward the horizon.

“There,” he said. “The ghost. The British general. He’s floating… judging us.”

“Shut up and pull,” Knox barked, though he glanced toward the horizon himself, squinting as if he might see a powdered wig fluttering in the snow.

A soldier behind them erupted in laughter, loud and unhinged. “We’re dragging these giant metal monsters so we can blow OTHER PEOPLE UP better? Someone pinch me. This can’t be real.”

Knox sighed but kept moving forward. His internal monologue was clearer than any orders he'd given aloud: The cannons are irrelevant. What matters is this—we're dragging each other forward.

The bureaucracy of war was its own theater of absurdity. A widow, clutching her husband’s bloodstained jacket, stood in a government office waiting for aid. The man behind the desk scratched away at his papers without looking up.

“Proof,” he said. “We need proof.”

She stared at him, holding out the jacket like an indictment. “This isn’t enough proof? His blood?”

The bureaucrat paused, adjusted his spectacles, and said, “We need it signed in triplicate.”

The widow’s neighbor, standing behind her, stepped forward with a sigh. “For God’s sake, I’ll sign it. What are we proving, exactly?”

“Proof,” the bureaucrat repeated, as though the word itself was holy. The neighbor’s pen scratched against the form. “Satisfied?” he muttered.

The widow, though grief-stricken, managed the faintest smirk. “You are proof that he was human.”

Francis Marion’s swamp fighters weren’t just legendary—they were exhausted, mosquito-bitten shadows. After an ambush, one young soldier knelt beside his brother, cradling him as blood soaked the mud beneath them.

“I should’ve been faster,” the boy whispered. “I should’ve—”

Marion crouched beside him, his voice steady but quiet. “You stayed,” he said. “That’s enough.”

“But he’s gone,” the boy replied, tears carving streaks through the dirt on his face.

Marion nodded. “Yes. But you are not.”

Together, they buried the boy’s brother under the watchful eye of an enormous and entirely unnecessary frog, which they promptly named Captain Croak to lighten the mood.

Yorktown, in all its infamous glory, wasn’t the crescendo of triumph it pretended to be—it was a series of surreal moments strung together by necessity and exhaustion. Washington, pacing the trenches, stopped beside a soldier slumped over the body of his best friend.

“You can’t stay here,” Washington said. “If you fall, we all fall.”

The soldier, staring hollowly into the smoke-filled air, muttered, “It feels like we’ve already fallen, sir.”

“Then stand anyway,” Washington replied.

When Cornwallis surrendered, the white flag fluttered limply, as though embarrassed by its role in the whole charade. The soldiers didn’t cheer—they stared at it, waiting for someone to shout “Just kidding!” But no one did.

The Revolutionary War wasn’t about clean victories or flawless heroes. It was about absurdity and survival—about laughing at ghosts in the snow, naming frogs after officers, and dragging cannons that no one cared about because helping each other mattered more.

It didn’t erase the scars or promise perfection. But it left behind something unexpectedly powerful: a flicker of humor, connection, and humanity that refused to die. Sometimes, that flicker was enough.

The Revolutionary War wasn’t just a fight for independence—it was a reckoning with collapse and carnage, a painful meditation on impermanence. Even as soldiers waged battles to build a nation, they grappled with the harsh truth that nothing—neither life nor peace—was ever truly permanent. It was a fight not just against tyranny, but against the specters of despair, disconnection, and systems that reduced humans to commodities in service of war.

Take the farmer-turned-soldier, crouched low in the mud-streaked trench, his musket trembling in his hands. Around him, chaos erupted—gunpowder burned thick in the air, cannons roared like distant thunderstorms, and men fell like autumn leaves scattered by the wind. Yet even amid this carnage, impermanence whispered to him. Will victory bring peace, or merely another season of grief? Will this effort hold, or crumble like walls built too thin? His thoughts were silenced by the faint clinking of a soldier stirring “morale soup.” “Mostly water, sometimes optimism,” the soldier said wryly, offering the farmer a crooked smile. Even in the chaos, the farmer was struck by the poignancy of connection—the way small kindnesses could steady trembling hearts, if only for a moment.

Later, in the wilderness, a sharpshooter perched high in the branches of a tree and gazed at his target: a British officer with polished buttons and commands that carried above the din of battle. His orders were clear—aim, fire, silence the enemy leadership. But moral conviction tangled with his fear as humanity crept into his mind. What if he has a child who calls him “Papa”? What if he loves poetry or keeps roses in his garden? What if this is not who he wanted to be? He whispered a prayer: “Guide my hand, or stop me if I’m wrong.” When the rifle cracked and the officer fell, the sharpshooter felt the weight of impermanence shift onto his shoulders, as though the ground beneath him might collapse into the void. What he fought for felt impossibly fragile—yet he kept fighting.

Valley Forge was a monument to collapse, a place where frostbitten soldiers huddled in cabins that seemed destined to fall apart. Disease slashed through their ranks, leaving men pale and trembling. One soldier, breathing shallowly, muttered to his friend, “You’ll live longer than me. You have to. Someone has to.” His friend pressed his last scrap of bread into the soldier’s hand, whispering, “Eat. This matters more than me.” Another soldier carried the weight of loss in silence, having buried his closest companion two nights earlier. Yet, even in the depths of despair, moments of connection persisted—acts of humanity that burned brightly against the encroaching dark. Impermanence couldn’t erase that flicker of light.

But as the war progressed, systems of dehumanization loomed large, stripping people of their dignity to fuel the machinery of conflict. Families were torn apart—fathers conscripted, sons drafted, leaving mothers and daughters to fend off starvation or predators that weren’t just metaphorical. In the aftermath of battle, bureaucrats demanded evidence before offering aid, reducing widows to numbers on a form. One woman stood trembling, holding her husband’s bloodied jacket as proof of his sacrifice. The official stared at her blankly, unmoved. A neighbor, unable to remain silent, scrawled his name beside hers on the ledger and said firmly, “Her word is enough.” That moment didn’t dismantle the system, but it reclaimed dignity for one person. Sometimes, that was enough.

Francis Marion’s guerrilla fighters navigated swampy terrain that swallowed boots and tore at spirits. One soldier cradled his dying brother after an ambush, murmuring, “I wasn’t fast enough—I couldn’t save you.” His grief rippled through the camp as others rallied around him, offering him their silent presence. Marion himself knelt beside him and whispered, “You stayed. That was enough.” When the brother passed, the soldier carved his cross from the nearest tree and stayed to bury him. The swamp consumed the grave, but the memory remained—a fragile ember amid the ash.

Yorktown marked the war’s chaotic crescendo—a collision of carnage and collapse that made triumph feel almost absurd. Smoke soaked the air as soldiers crawled through trenches, their movements slow and heavy. Washington passed one man who had fallen beside a body—a friend he’d pulled from the trenches only hours earlier. “If you fall, we all fall,” Washington said, pressing a hand to the soldier’s shoulder. The man rose, but his grief clung to him like a second skin. When Cornwallis surrendered, victory arrived quietly, carrying no cheers, no finality, only the faint hope that some lives might still be salvageable.

The war didn’t promise permanence—it carried the realization that even triumph can crumble, even freedom can falter. Yet the most poignant moments weren’t the battles won, but the connections built. A widow opened her door to a boy searching for his father, saying softly, “I’ve lost mine, too. Stay with me tonight.” A soldier gave his last blanket to a stranger who had collapsed near camp, whispering, “This is all I have left. Take it.” These acts, while fleeting, were powerful rebellions against the systems and forces that dehumanized them.

The Revolutionary War was a story of impermanence and collapse—a reminder that even the strongest walls crack and even the brightest fires dim. Yet it was also a story of yearning—a story of people who refused to let go of their humanity, who clung to connection and light even when the world tilted toward darkness. And maybe that is the truest triumph of all: finding the strength to keep standing, even when the ground beneath you feels like it might give way.
