**In Our Room That Has No Walls or Borders**

Words / 2025 Variety

The first time Sonah brought me here, I thought we were simply going to her studio. She'd said something about needing to show me where she worked, and I expected the usual—north-facing windows, paint-splattered floors, maybe a coffee pot in the corner. We walked through an ordinary door into what seemed like an ordinary room. It took me several minutes to notice that the far wall wasn't exactly there. Or rather, it was there when you looked directly at it, but when you turned to speak to Sonah, when you got involved in what you were doing, it dissolved into garden, into sky, into possibility. "We call it the Outside Room," she said, watching me understand. That was three years ago. Now I come here every day, though I still can't explain it properly to anyone who hasn't seen it. The best I can do is this:

A room without walls, doors, or windows—though when you first enter, you might not notice their absence. It's the kind of place that reveals itself slowly, the way understanding comes to you on a Tuesday afternoon when you're not looking for it. The Outside Room, we call it, painted in honey tone hues that shift with the light, each shade a subtle contradiction of the last.

Here, everything is concrete yet impossible. You can touch the desk where someone left their coffee cup still warm, see the indent in the cushion where they were sitting moments ago, yet the ceiling dissolves into actual sky. It's familiar yet mysterious, the way your childhood home becomes strange when you return after years away. Like having dinner at a homesick treehouse—yes, that's it exactly—where the plates are your grandmother's china but the floor sways with wind.

Say The Outside Room again, let it echo through and through. The words themselves become a kind of doorway, each repetition opening something new. There's comfort in naming what shouldn't exist but does anyway.

Ah yes, we understand better now—though understanding here means accepting what shouldn't make sense. This is the interior room that extends and flows into the wall-less outside workspace, the way a conversation with an old friend moves from the kitchen table to the garden without anyone deciding to relocate. The transition happens between thoughts, between breaths. From Interior Room to Outside Space, the boundary exists only as a suggestion, like the line between waking and sleeping when you're reading in afternoon sun.

The Inside Room Opening Outward isn't a grand gesture. It's more like the way your chest opens when you finally tell someone the truth you've been carrying. Where the Inside Room Becomes Outside happens gradually, then all at once—first you notice the walls seem translucent, then transparent, then you realize they were never there at all, or maybe they were only there when you needed them to be.

People move freely between the inside room and its outside extension, though "move" isn't quite right. They drift, they flow, they find themselves somewhere without remembering the journey. A woman carries her typewriter from the oak desk to a patch of clover, not noticing when roof becomes sky. Children draw with chalk on what might be floor, might be earth. Everyone taking everything in with gratitude—the particular gratitude that comes from being given something you didn't know you needed. They're giving their joyful minutes to others, these small offerings accumulating like leaves in corners where walls would be if walls existed here.

This Continuous Space—somewhat protected yet open, like being held by someone who loves you but doesn't need to possess you. Somewhat defined yet boundless, the way a good story has shape but never really ends, just keeps living in you after the last page. It's helping us stay more present to do what needs doing, though what needs doing changes depending on where you stand, who you are today, what the light is doing.

Place becomes something larger here—location spreads out like spilled water finding its level. This site, this spot, this scene, each word trying to capture something that refuses capture. It's a setting that sets nothing, a situation that situates you everywhere at once. An area without area, whereabouts that are also wherewithouta, locale that is both local and universal.

Someone calls it home, someone else calls it apartment, though it's neither and both. An establishment that establishes only possibility. Property that no one owns but everyone inhabits. A room that is all rooms and no room. Lodgings for the temporary eternal. Day and night work station where work means being, where station means movement. No one asks about pay grade here—value measures itself in different currencies: the weight of afternoon light, the sound of pencil on paper, the way someone's shoulders relax when they realize they can stay as long as they need.

Footing matters here, though the ground shifts between carpet and grass, between foundation and faith. This estate that's really a state of being, this mission that knows its purpose without needing to name it.

This feels like it's missing something—that productive incompleteness that keeps you returning, adding another layer, another color, another breath. Oh yeah, we remember now, like finding the perfect word after the conversation's over—This Room That Opens Into Sky, not dramatically but inevitably, the way morning opens into afternoon. The Extending Room That Has No End, though "no end" doesn't mean infinite so much as irrelevant, the way you don't ask where a circle stops.

The Continuum—yes, that's what we've been trying to say—suggests the physical continuity from inside to outside and the continuous work happening there. Work that is play that is prayer that is presence. Complete and Intentional, like a deep breath you've been meaning to take all day. Holding a Sense of Mystery about A Room That Doesn't Stop At Its Walls, because stopping at walls was never the point. The point was the not-stopping, the gentle insistence that boundaries are invitations to continue, that every ending is just a doorway to the next beginning, even when—especially when—there are no doors at all.

This afternoon, I'm sitting where the inside floor meets the outside earth, my notebook half on wooden boards, half on grass. Sonah is at her easel in what might be the corner, if corners existed here. A new person arrived an hour ago, looking confused, holding a casserole dish like someone visiting a neighbor. "I'm not sure I'm in the right place," she said. Sonah and I exchanged glances—the same look we've shared dozens of times now.

"You are," Sonah told her, and it was true. It's always true.

The woman is still here, the casserole dish abandoned on what we call the kitchen table, though it's really a picnic table, though it's really just a place where people eat together. She's writing something on the back of her grocery list. Her shoulders have that particular softness that happens here, that letting go of edges.

Tomorrow I'll come back. Tomorrow the room will have shifted slightly, the way a garden is never quite the same garden twice. Someone new might be here, or it might be only Sonah and me, or I might find myself alone with the work that needs doing. The room will extend exactly as far as it needs to. The walls will be there when they're necessary, gone when they're not.

I close my notebook, stand up, brush grass from my knees. Outside, which is also inside, which is also home, the afternoon light has turned everything the color of honey. Sonah looks up from her painting and smiles—not at me, exactly, but at the whole of it, this impossible place we've made possible by simply continuing to arrive.
