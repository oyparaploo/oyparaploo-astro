Brrd Roots ~ Mindful Consequence ~ A Sky in Conflict and the Spirit That Won’t Apologize

Words / 2025 Variety

The hospital smelled like sterilized guilt and bureaucracy’s last attempt at relevance.

Emmett had always been a skeptic of institutions—the courts, the systems, the absolutely unnecessary number of forms required to prove he existed. But now, his body had betrayed him in the worst possible way: by needing medical assistance.

Blood pressure spikes weren’t supposed to be *this* dramatic. They were supposed to be something you whispered about while pretending a banana and three deep breaths could fix it. But Emmett had skipped past “concerning” and gone straight into “this is why aliens won’t talk to us” territory.

Lena had dragged him in before he could stage an argument.

“You should’ve said something,” she muttered, scanning the monitors instead of his face.

Emmett exhaled like a man cornered by reality. “Saying it wouldn’t have changed anything.”

“That’s what cowards say before passing out in a Walgreens parking lot,” Lena replied.

Fair.

---

Then the doctor arrived.

But not just *any* doctor.

**Them.**

The artist. The specter. The walking caution tape of history.

Dr. Roman Ayers, Environmental Medicine, *Performance Art Extraordinaire*, Wears Crime Scene Evidence Like It’s Paris Fashion Week.

Their coat still trailed warning labels, their gloves mismatched like forensic relics. A laminated report detailing air toxicity violations peeked out from their sleeve like it was a casual accessory. A bullet casing necklace clinked softly as they adjusted a stethoscope that looked… possibly stolen.

“You didn’t listen to your own body,” Ayers said, flipping through Emmett’s chart with the weary amusement of someone who had predicted this outcome months ago. “The air worked faster than your arrogance.”

Lena smirked in the corner. “Finally, a diagnosis we can all agree on.”

Emmett squinted at Ayers. “What’s the official term?”

Ayers tapped the page. **Early-stage oxidative stress disorder.**

A phrase not yet fully recognized by legislation.

A phrase fully recognized by anyone still conscious enough to curse the sky.

---

**The Way Medicine Is Changing**

“This isn’t just about high blood pressure and headaches,” Ayers continued, their voice smooth, clinical, just *this* side of theatrical.

“The air doesn’t just invade the lungs. It sets up camp. Rearranges your molecules. Throws a wild party inside your cortex where memory loss is the headliner.”

Emmett stared. “Are you telling me pollution is gaslighting me?”

Ayers nodded solemnly. “And you, unfortunately, have been its most dedicated audience.”

Lena covered a laugh with her hand but did not dispute it.

Ayers continued, pulling out a thick stack of research papers—documents thick with environmental data, ink smudged like they had been read too many times by too many tired hands.

“This diagnosis isn’t on legal health records yet,” they murmured.

“But that doesn’t mean it isn’t real.”

Lena frowned. “Oxidative stress disorder?”

Ayers nodded. “A name for what’s already been happening. The hospitals won’t recognize it until it can’t be ignored.”

“Until it kills enough people,” Lena muttered.

Ayers didn’t flinch. “Yes.”

---

**The Origin of Ayers**

Later, after Emmett had fallen into a state of begrudging recovery, Lena turned toward Ayers.

“You were always at the outskirts.”

Ayers nodded once.

“But you never—” she hesitated. “You never joined us. Not really.”

Ayers watched her, unreadable. “The war isn’t in the streets,” they said simply. “It’s in your cells. Your veins. Your cortex.”

Lena inhaled slowly. “So why wear the evidence?”

Ayers exhaled, slow, deliberate, gesturing toward their coat—toward the forensic reports stitched into its lining, the bullet casings slung against their ribs like pendants, the old contamination warnings trailing their sleeves.

“It’s proof,” they said. “You wear your protest in banners. I wear mine in what’s already been done.”

Lena studied them. Studied the way their silence held something older than the lawsuits, older than the movement, older than even the resistance.

Something like mourning.

Something like warning.

Something like a history they would never fully reveal.

Emmett shifted in his bed, finally waking again, staring at Ayers as though trying to put a name to the presence in the room.

“Do you ever take a break?” he muttered.

Ayers gave him a dry smile. “I used to.”

Emmett squinted. “And then?”

Ayers tapped the research in front of them. “And then I remembered what happens when you stop watching.”

Yes … let the body turn into warning now … let the resistance shift inward … let survival become something more personal …

---

**Between the Weight and the Wing**

The hospital smelled like filtered air and old emergencies.

It wasn’t unexpected—this moment, this rupture, this failure of the body to keep pace with the world breaking around it. Emmett had known something was wrong for weeks, months maybe. But knowing and acknowledging were two different things.

Blood pressure spikes weren’t just numbers—they were warning signals wrapped in pulse, tension lodged beneath the skin, dizziness arriving like an uninvited guest. But he had ignored them, had kept walking through the poisoned city, had pushed forward like movement could outrun biology.

Until it couldn’t.

Until the resistance he had worn like armor became something thinner, something fragile, something mortal.

---

Lena had been the one to bring him in—silent, expression set, not angry but aware. She had spoken for him when his words blurred, had pressed his wrist against a monitor when his hands shook too much to do it himself.

“You should’ve said something,” she murmured, not looking at him, studying the machines instead.

Emmett exhaled. “Saying it wouldn’t have changed anything.”

She didn’t argue.

She didn’t have to.

---

Then the doctor arrived.

Not the standard presence he expected. Not the weary emergency resident half-rushing through a diagnosis.

But **them.**

The artist. The figure wrapped in crime and consequence, the one who had haunted the edges of protests, who had worn history on their sleeves and silence like armor.

Here, in the stark white light of a hospital room, they looked no different.

Still draped in warnings. Still holding pieces of evidence as if they were accessories rather than weight.

Their badge hung loose at their collar—Dr. Roman Ayers, Environmental Medicine.

Not just protest. Not just performance.

Healing. Investigation. Undeniable proof.

---

“You didn’t listen to your own body,” Ayers said, scanning the readings, voice level but deliberate. “The air worked faster than you thought it would.”

Emmett watched them, watched the way their fingers traced the edges of his medical chart, watched the bullet casing necklace shift as they leaned forward.

“This isn’t just an episode,” Ayers continued. “It’s accumulation. Spikes. Vascular strain. Neurological shifts.”

They flipped the report toward him, tapped a diagnosis with their ringed finger.

**Early-stage oxidative stress disorder.**

A phrase not yet fully recognized by legislation.

A phrase fully understood by those who had lived inside it.

---

Lena spoke first. “What do we do?”

Ayers did not smile, did not soften.

“Stop pretending your body is separate from the war you’re fighting.”

Emmett swallowed. His pulse thudded in his ears, in his ribs, in his hands that had gone too numb for too long.

Ayers continued, steady. “And you? You have two choices, Emmett. Treat this like an isolated incident, let it accumulate, let it worsen. Or—”

They pulled something from their coat—medical research reports, ink smudged, pages thick with environmental data.

“—You make sure no one else collapses like you did.”

---

The resistance had never been just streets and banners.

It had always been the body, too.

And now, Emmett had the proof inside his own veins.
