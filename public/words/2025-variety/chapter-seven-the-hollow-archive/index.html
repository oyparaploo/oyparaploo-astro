**Chapter Seven ~ The Hollow Archive**

Words / 2025 Variety

The Archives Nationales looked like a promise made of stone. The courtyard held its breath. Even the plane trees seemed to have learned not to rustle too loudly. A security guard examined their passes with the expression of someone reading a poem in a language he respected but disliked. The stamp he used made a verdict sound—**thunk**—that rolled across the marble and died at a respectful distance.

Inside, silence wasn’t absence; it was policy. Shoes negotiated their own volume. Pages turned with manners. Coughs were reprimanded by the building itself, returning from the high ceiling in thinner versions of themselves as if scolded into better behavior.

Libaux Torrini leaned toward Troll Tearsen as they crossed the echoing vestibule. “Give me a nightclub over this any day,” he said. “At least the noise admits it’s noise.”

Troll glanced up at the vaulting and thought of chapels. “Here the noise wears a tie.”

They signed in under forgettable names. The clerk slid them two call slips with a motion so economical it knocked no sound loose from the air. A wall-clock ticked in small, important decisions. **tik. tik. tik.**

The reading room was a continent of green lamps. Light pooled on wood. Shadows behaved. At the far end, beyond the hush of scholars and the faint percussive ritual of pencils, an archivist in a gray cardigan wheeled a trolley stacked with austerely labeled boxes. She set them down with measured thuds—**thud… thud**—like a drumline told to respect the dead.

“Consultancy contracts—Ministry attachments,” she whispered. “Please keep the order.”

“We will,” Troll said, and felt the lie sidle politely into the room.

They opened the first box. Redaction looked back at them—black strokes dragged over paragraphs with a pressure you could hear if you let your ears do the reading: **rrrskhh… rrrskhh**. The ink had been applied by a hand intent on erasure but unable to hide its temperament. Anger leaves texture.

“Meuller said performance,” Libaux murmured. “Even on paper, he’s staging.”

Mage Meuller’s earlier email replayed in Troll’s mind with its clean, clinical caution: *The marbles are not evidence. They are performance.* She could almost hear the cursor blinking after the last word like a tired metronome.

They read. Names marched. Budgets spoke politely. Supplementary signatures appeared where no one had asked for them. Over and over, a surname recurred as a subscript, a polite ghost in rubber-stamped ink.

**Fredda Stremler.**

Not shouting, never shouting—just present. Witness-friendly. Audit-safe. The tone of a person who had perfected standing to one side of the camera and owning the photograph.

Troll’s pen hovered. She did not underline. She did not want to make more sound than the room allowed.

The second box held correspondence: minutes of meetings that never mentioned decisions, memos that apologized for delays and never for outcomes. An appointment book copied by an efficient assistant’s hand, each digit too neat to be innocent. Miller Corbal’s name appeared once in a log of visitors, followed by a note: *request denied—redirected to local liaison.* The pencil that had made the notation had pressed hard; the tail of the “d” bit through to the next page.

“Miller tried the front door,” Libaux said quietly.

“And was shown the gift shop,” Troll said.

They moved to the third box. A ledger lay on top, cloth spine faded to the color of old air. When Troll lifted it, something in the binding rattled. Not the thin rattle of paper clips or pins.

Dry. Hollow.

**tch… tch.**

She and Libaux looked at each other.

Troll shook the ledger gently. The sound answered again, precise, diminutive, like teeth deciding to chatter.

**tch. tch-tch.**

The cut in the spine was minute—a seam where cloth had lifted away from board. Troll eased a nail beneath it, then a paper knife. The binding parted with a whisper, and three marbles slid into her palm: one clear, one red, one veined white where a fracture had frozen lightning.

The sound of glass on skin was small and enormous at once. Heads turned. Lamps took a breath.

“Performance,” Libaux said, almost without air.

Troll closed her fingers around the marbles and felt their feigned warmth. The clear one refracted the lamp into a geometry of obedience. The red one held a quiet bruise. The cracked one made the light stutter, as if truth itself had a stammer.

A notification pulsed on her phone, screen dark against the green pool of the lamp: **UMA AROUJE**. She let the message display without touching it.

> *Vienna delegation meets in forty-eight hours. Stremler’s name is already circulating. Do not compromise us further.*

The words behaved like orders even when read silently. On the glass, her reflection’s mouth looked obedient. She turned the phone face-down. The case met wood in a small, contained consonant. **tuk.**

They returned to the ledger. Margins, at first, were proper—page numbers, cross-references, the clerical husk of meaning. Then Troll saw it, a scrawl in graphite so light it could have been a smudge until it wasn’t: two words pressed into the paper with a hand that had not been invited.

**Nunito Volker.**

The Austrian valley rose back into the room as if the building had inhaled mountains. Fog. The shelter’s algae-streaked glass. The first green marble bending dawn in a way dawn had not agreed to. The name sat in the margin like a pin in a map that didn’t show that country.

Libaux’s breath shifted. “He’s stitching it. Across borders. Across rooms.”

“Or she wrote it,” Troll said. “Miller, when the paper refused to say what she needed it to.”

“Either way,” Libaux said, “we’re not the ones drawing the lines.”

The room waited. Somewhere, far away in the building, a cart wheel squeaked once and then learned silence again.

Troll’s phone buzzed a second time—**Miyake Coscia**—and then again with a voicemail. She excused herself, letting the reading room release her carefully. In the restroom, every sound confessed. Faucets rehearsed their drip. Tiles multiplied footsteps until the stall doors had a committee.

She pressed play.

Static spoke first, the machine’s attempt to turn air into record. Then her mother’s voice, thinner than memory allowed, threaded between breaths. *“…Troll… is she… coming…”* The words reached for her the way a hand reaches, not to grasp but to assure itself there is still temperature ahead.

Behind the voice, a monitor kept its own rhythm. **tik. tik. tik.** The same verdict-tick as the archive clock, now taking the pulse of a body that had once carried her and now asked to be carried.

Troll braced her palm against the cool tile and let the voicemail finish. It did not end; it simply ran out of recorded seconds.

When she returned, Libaux had arranged the boxes as if they belonged to him. He had that look he wore when his thoughts were bricks being stacked in the right order and he hoped the wall would hold.

“Look at this,” he said. He slid across a copy of meeting minutes. A list of attendees, all titles, no nouns. At the bottom, a line that should have been empty hosted a pale impression, as if a name had been written on the page below and pressed upward. Troll angled the lamp, breathed softly across the paper. Letters surfaced—not fully, like faces behind a wet window.

F. S—

The alarm started.

Not a bell. A klaxon made of geometry and bright metal and the building’s certainty that people should be where they belong. Sound struck the ceiling and returned harder. **WAAHN. WAAHN.**

Shutters rattled down over tall windows in a practiced descent. Staff rose as one organism of procedure. Hands moved to paper. Paper moved to boxes. Boxes moved to carts. A choreography of prevention.

“Time’s up,” Libaux said.

The archivist in the gray cardigan reached them at speed. “You need to close your files,” she said, the sentence a ritual. “There is a technical fault.”

“Of course,” Troll said, her hands already making calm shapes with lids and string ties. Performance inside performance.

The marbles in her pocket knocked together once—**tik**—a sound so small it seemed impossible the alarm didn’t grow quiet to give it room.

They worked fast without theatrics. Order mattered. They preserved it so cleanly it almost looked like obedience. Libaux returned slips. Troll tucked the ledger back into its box, spine closed, secret resealed.

In the vestibule, the security guard’s voice tried to be louder than the klaxon and failed. People became streams. Doors became plans. The courtyard opened like breath in the chest after a long dive.

They were halfway across it when Troll looked back and saw, on the far side of the glass, a man watching. Not Stremler. Younger, smoother in the suit, the kind of smooth that hires itself out to any angle. He touched his earpiece with two fingers, then let his hand fall with a gesture that said nothing and everything: *we see you; we saw you earlier; we will see you again.*

Outside the gate, Paris resumed its practiced noise. Cars hissed tires through the wet. A cyclist cursed in heroic verse. Somewhere, pigeons conducted themselves against a statue with a rattling insistence.

They fell into step without speaking. The alarm shrank behind them, lost to traffic, to rain, to a thousand other emergencies.

On the corner, beneath a canopy bright with the smell of oranges and damp cardboard, Troll opened her hand. The three marbles lay like punctuation in her palm—clear, red, cracked.

“Evidence?” Libaux asked.

Troll closed her fingers. “Program notes.”

He huffed a laugh that was not laughter. “Uma’s going to love that.”

“Uma wants deliverables,” Troll said. “So does Stremler. The difference is that one of them admits wanting an audience.”

She slipped the marbles back into her coat. Their small weight changed nothing and insisted on everything.

Across the street, a shop shutter banged once and then again in wind that had found a corridor to run through. The second bang came a half-beat late—the echo of the first, arriving as a decision.

They walked toward it.

Behind them, the archive clock resumed its ordinary verdicts—**tik. tik. tik.**—as if nothing had been said in the margins of anything that mattered.
