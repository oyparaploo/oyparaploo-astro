Chapter Eight ~ Voices in the Static

Words / 2025 Variety

The safehouse sat above a tire shop off the ring road, a rectangle of rooms that had been painted so many times the walls forgot their first color. From the windows, the Périphérique curled like a lit fuse, traffic hissing along it with wet, perpetual intent. The stairwell smelled of damp plaster and old cumin; someone on the second floor practiced the same four bars of a song on a keyboard without ever discovering a fifth.

Inside, sound organized itself into layers. The refrigerator hummed a low, patient mmm. The radiator clinked and then refused to commit to further conversation. The building’s electric nerves clicked in their conduits, small insect languages running through walls. Troll Tearsen stood with her palm on the sill and let it all map itself onto her.

Libaux Torrini put the kettle on and stared at the socket as if it might negotiate. “I don’t like places that sound like they could wake up without consent,” he said.

Troll lifted her ear toward the glass. The highway’s breath swelled and thinned, never a full exhale. “We need a place that admits it’s listening,” she said. “Otherwise we’re the only witnesses in the room.”

He set two cups down. Porcelain met cheap table with a dry tuk that carried more authority than the furniture deserved. He slid one to her and set the other near the small cloth bag Troll had emptied onto a plate: three marbles from the archive’s ledger (clear, red, cracked) and one from Pigalle (pristine, club-warmth long since surrendered).

For a while they drank in the quiet that wasn’t quiet. The floor trammed a tremor through its joists when a truck reversed at the shop below; a drainpipe negotiated rain in long, thoughtful vowels. Troll reached across, touched the Pigalle marble with two fingers. It didn’t move. Still, she felt the memory of vibration along the pad of her index finger, as if the bass from the club had remained engraved there, a groove on a record pressed into skin.

Libaux opened a battered case and lifted out a handheld scanner and a small field recorder. “Luc’s,” he said. “He said Paris is a beehive. There are always stray frequencies—pirates, taxis, baby monitors, alarms that believe themselves music.”

“Bees have choreography,” Troll said.

“Then let’s dance.”

He tuned the scanner along the FM band, through slick corporate certainty and an earnest host reading a list of public demonstrations, then into fuzz, then into a soccer game narrated as if someone were blessing a field. He slid higher into static that exhaled, inhaled, forgot itself, tried again.

The refrigerator’s mmm found a harmony with the hiss for a moment, a coincidence that felt like instruction. Troll pulled a chair to the window and set the recorder on the sill. The traffic’s wet whisper slipped into the machine with obedient eagerness.

“Hold there,” she said.

A bleached children’s swing in the courtyard below creaked once and then refused to explain itself. Libaux rolled the dial a millimeter. Static thickened, then thinned, then stacked. The sound was less a wave than a lacework.

A voice surfaced.

Not whole. A syllable like the corner of a page. —will—

Libaux didn’t move. The dial sat under his index finger like a living thing that disliked surprise.

Another syllable. —not—

Troll felt her heartbeat step into single file.

—speak.

She looked at Libaux. He didn’t look back. He took his hand away from the dial as if to assure the radio of his respect.

Static washed it all clean again. The kettle clicked its contentment and then remembered it was not the point.

“That’s impossible,” Libaux said softly, which meant he was deciding how much to believe, not whether.

“Repeatable?” Troll asked.

He nodded at the recorder. “We have the capture. Whether we have the truth depends on your appetite.” He retuned a hair lower. The static tore like paper and then held its edges. A child’s laugh flitted across the room and then died as if embarrassed to have traveled without permission.

They tried again. The scanner yielded bus dispatch chatter, the peal of a ringtone someone had once thought would be charming, a DJ whispering a name with too many consonants. Then a pitch change in the noise that behaved like constraint applied—someone turning their own dial somewhere nearby.

“Luc?” Troll asked, although of course it was not.

Libaux kept going. The hiss thickened into snowfall and then thinned into drizzle. Another voice stepped toward them and then back. Street names, or something that wanted to be street names. A syllable that could have been a signature if signatures were made of lime and dust. The red marble on the plate vibrated a fraction, or else the truck below leaned, or else Troll’s want displaced light in a way physics would resent.

“Again,” she said.

He found the same seam in the noise and kept the needle there. The building shifted. The radiator exhaled something that had wanted to be steam and remained theory. When the voice returned, it was closer.

“Stremler will not speak.”

Static leaned over the words and picked them up by the corners. The sentence sounded both like threat and like choreography.

Libaux smiled with just the right amount of contempt. “The man is constructing a myth where silence is an instrument. He wants us to believe volume is vulgar.”

Troll looked at the marbles. Clear threw a rectangular moon of the lamp onto the ceiling. Red held the room as stain. Cracked fractured the light into a nervous anatomy.

“Or he wants us to exhaust ourselves listening to ghosts while he performs in another room,” she said.

The corridor contributed a small sound—a neighbor’s key missing and then finding the lock, a door clicking with the satisfaction of mechanical inevitability. Through the wall, a television began to speak in a language Troll didn’t share; even so, the laughter landed with the same weight it had on every continent: a cue, honored because to ignore it would be to refuse membership.

Libaux set the scanner to record continuously. “We’ll peel it later. Spectra, compression artifacts. Meuller will feast.”

“Meuller will remind us it’s theater,” Troll said, but she didn’t stop him. She held the Pigalle marble again and felt the smoothness as if it were a decision she could still refuse.

Her phone buzzed. Miyake Coscia. Troll didn’t move until the second buzz, the voicemail. She stepped to the kitchen and pressed play, bracing herself against the counter’s laminate edge.

Air spoke again first: the machine’s thin appetite for the currents between words. Then Miyake, steady and soft. “She’s sleeping mostly. When she wakes she asks the time as if it’s a song lyric she knows but can’t place. She said today: ‘Tell Troll the marbles are behaving.’ We have the radio on. It makes a nice hush.”

The fridge hummed in empathy. Troll let the message hold her for its length and then end without permission. She turned the tap. The sink gave a cough before producing water, an old building trick. She drank.

In the main room, Libaux had moved to the window. He watched the streetlights insist on their geometry against drizzle. “I used to think it was noble to refuse to perform,” he said. “Then I realized it was the same vanity as demanding applause.”

Troll rejoined him. “We were born into a room already clapping,” she said.

He snorted, a thread of humor through a fabric that resisted it. “Paris tourism should hire you.”

The scanner, unattended for a breath, found a new seam. Children’s voices. The sound was too dry to be live, too clean to be memory. A schoolyard, filtered. Then a man’s vowels entered and the voices fell back as though told to by choreography none of them remembered learning.

“…placement review… liaise across… compliance—”

The words were the architecture of meetings, sentences designed to enclose, not reveal. The voice did not claim itself, which itself was a claim.

“Turn it up,” Troll said. He did. Even loud, the speech tried to behave like background.

“-we’re not in the business of outcomes, we’re in the business of oversight—”

“Stremler,” Libaux said, not a question.

Troll closed her eyes and kept them closed until the speech ended and the static regained its normal variety. She opened them on the plate. The cracked marble’s white vein looked like handwriting that had changed direction mid-letter.

“It’s an edit,” Libaux said. “He’s splicing his noise into the city. Low power. Enough to catch us if we’re arrogant. Which we are.”

The building made a sound that belonged to none of its parts, a whole-body creak—settling into the rain’s promise. Somewhere, the second-floor keyboard discovered the fifth bar and then immediately lost it. Troll allowed herself a breath that looked like a laugh and wasn’t.

The hallway light went on with a delay, then off. Someone passed their door. The floorboards narrated the weight in rising pitch. A paper slid beneath the threshold with a dry whisper. Both of them stood still without having to agree to it.

Libaux picked up the envelope as if a hinge might surprise him. Canvas fiber. He opened it with two fingers and tipped it. A small cloth bag fell into his palm.

Troll had the plate ready before he untied the string. He poured. Three marbles rolled out, finding their places against porcelain with small ceremonial clicks. Clear. Red. Another cracked—this one crazed into a web so delicate the room’s light hesitated before passing.

No note.

The building listened harder, or else they did. Troll extended a fingertip and spun the new cracked marble once. It wobbled, corrected itself with dignified stubbornness, and settled facing no one.

Libaux looked at the scanner, still recording, the ribbon of LEDs moving as if thought were visible. “He’s turned the city into his stage,” he said. “He’s turned us into props who clap at the right times.”

“Or we asked to be cast,” Troll said. “We showed up early and learned our lines in the hallway.”

The kettle clicked again, bored with being ignored. The tire shop downstairs pulled its shutter with a corrugated roar that made everything in the room tremble in alignment. Every marble shivered a micron and then reposed.

Troll’s phone lit with Uma Arouje and a preview line—Report before day’s end. She silenced it. The order remained in the air like a smell.

“What do we tell her?” Libaux asked.

“That Stremler will not speak,” Troll said, hearing the sentence in the frequency’s own exhausted cadence. “And that he already has.”

Outside, sirens stitched two distant corners together and were gone. The apartment forgave their absence. The refrigerator resumed its mmm. The radio kept its hiss, the particular hiss of a city that won’t stop making itself up.

They left the marbles on the plate and the recorder on the sill and the window open a notch so the wet could enter and behave like information. Troll stood in the middle of the room and felt the whole building repeat her heartbeat back at her, late, early, late—like someone else was counting for her from another room.

In the corridor, a child’s voice asked if the rain was inside or outside. A parent replied with enough certainty to save face and not enough to instruct. The light flicked on, off.

Troll walked to the plate and lifted the newest cracked marble. It was cooler than the others, as if the hallway had a different weather. She held it up to the lamp and watched the vein scatter the bulb into brief, busy confessions.

“Program notes,” Libaux said again, almost gently.

“Curtain down,” Troll said. “For now.”

They did not turn off the radio when they lay down, one on the couch and one on the bed that had once been optimistic. They let the static keep them company, a kind of lullaby that refused to choose a melody. The recorder’s red light made its patient dot.

Sometime after the train lines went quiet and before the first deliveries began to grunt themselves up the street, the scanner caught a last seam, a voice built of borrowed breath.

“…not speak…”

Troll dreamed the marbles had mouths. In the dream they rolled and said nothing, the clearest kind of testimony, the kind any court would reject and any jury would remember.
