## **Chapter One – The Marble in the Hand**

The valley hadn’t yet decided if morning was worth the effort.

Fog held on in the folds of the mountains, thick and still as milk. Rain overnight had left the road slick, its gravel shoulders darkened to almost black. A single bus shelter stood on the bend above Fontanella—glass clouded with algae streaks, roof slouched under years of snow and neglect, a timetable inside curling into a damp accordion.

The machinist Anders pulled his collar up against the damp. He was already late for his shift, and lateness meant more hours on the conveyor floor later, but he slowed anyway. Something felt wrong in the silence.

It wasn’t an absence of sound. The Grosses Walsertal never slept completely: far below, the river whispered over its rocks, a soft murmur like an old woman clearing her throat; a cowbell chimed once in a distant barn. But this was a silence that pressed, that bent the ordinary noises inward. Even his boots seemed to step softer, as if the fog wanted them quiet.

The shelter came into view. At first glance he thought someone had stopped to wait for the six-thirty bus. But the bus didn’t run on time anymore, not here, not on a Tuesday in the wet. He hesitated, trying to convince himself it was nothing, a tourist still drunk from last night. Then his stomach dropped.

She was slumped against the glass.

Her coat sagged open, rain dappling the blouse beneath. One boot still on her foot, the other lying upside down in the corner, laces splayed like roots reaching nowhere. Her head tilted at a wrong angle, chin tucked toward collarbone.

And her right hand was clenched, but not in a fist.

Curled within it was a marble.

Anders blinked, leaned forward a step. The marble seemed too luminous for the dim morning. Green glass, a swirl of white frozen inside like a twist of smoke. It caught what little dawn offered and bent it back at him in a way that felt… intentional.

He made a sound he didn’t recognize as his own, stepped back fast, and pulled his phone from his pocket. His thumb fumbled the digits twice. Finally, the line connected, a woman’s voice at dispatch—calm, exact.

“There’s a woman,” he said. His voice cracked, then flattened as if it belonged to someone else. “She’s not moving. Fontanella shelter. She’s holding… she’s holding a marble.”

---

Detective Troll Tearsen had been awake all night.

Not awake in the way of coffee and purpose, but in the way of someone who had closed her eyes and met only lists of things undone. The hospice texts had started just past midnight. *Your mother is restless. Mornings are clearest. Please come soon.* Signed by Nurse Miyake Coscia.

She had stared at the message until the phone dimmed. She had turned it face-down on the table, next to the unopened pamphlet with its cheerful title—*Family Support for the End of Life Journey*—and the photograph of smiling nurses. She hadn’t had the courage to open it.

The kettle clicked off. She hadn’t remembered turning it on. The kitchen window was slick with condensation, reflecting her face in faint distortion.

On the bookshelf, a jar of marbles caught what little light the room allowed. Her mother’s jar. The same one Troll had played with as a child—tilting it, watching colors tumble against glass, green and blue, the heavy click of them colliding. Now the jar sat dusted, rim smudged with old fingerprints. She hadn’t opened it in years.

The phone rang again—dispatch this time. She didn’t let it ring twice.

By the time she shrugged into her coat, she had already decided not to text Miyake back. The guilt followed her out the door, heavy as the fog.

---

The Fontanella bus shelter looked even smaller than she’d imagined from dispatch’s description. The fog pressed against it, turning the red-and-blue flash of patrol lights into bruises on glass.

Two officers stood by the tape. One of them stepped forward, broad-shouldered, rain glistening on her jacket.

Helene Klausmun.

“Detective Tearsen.” Her voice carried cleanly in the thin mountain air. “We’ve held the scene for you. CSU hasn’t touched the body.”

Troll nodded, ducked under the tape.

The victim sat where Anders had left her. Pale, hair damp with condensation, lips slightly parted. Not peace. Not sleep. Something between.

Her ID read Nunito Volker, age thirty-five. Local address, nothing remarkable.

But the marble—green with its twist of white—glinted in her stiffened hand.

Troll crouched, breath ghosting into fog. She leaned close enough that the glass surface bent her reflection back at her, mouth pulled long, eyes stretched wrong.

“Anyone touched it?” she asked.

A CSU technician adjusted his gloves. His badge read Mage Meuller. Glasses fogged at the edges, he shook his head. “Not a chance. Fingers have locked around it. We’ll ease it out once you’re ready.”

Troll nodded. Words sat on her tongue: *A marble is never just a marble when it’s found like this.* But the valley’s silence pressed them back down.

---

Anders sat wrapped in a blanket in the back of a patrol car. His hands were knotted in his lap, thumbs circling each other. Troll crouched by the open door.

“You found her?”

He nodded, eyes on gravel. “I thought she was asleep. Then I saw the boot. Then the marble. That’s when I stepped back.”

“Did you see anyone else?”

“Fog swallows sound.” His voice trembled. “But I thought I heard glass. A clink. Like something rolling. I told myself it was bottles in the ditch. I didn’t want to look again.”

Troll closed her notebook softly. “You did the right thing.”

---

Back at the shelter, Meuller eased the marble from Nunito Volker’s stiff fingers. It rolled once into the plastic tray—click—and lay still. Even bagged, it seemed luminous, as if the fog itself made room for it.

Troll’s phone buzzed. Another text from Miyake. *Your mother is awake. She asked if you are coming.*

She locked the screen without answering. Fog pressed against the glass as though listening.

---

Footsteps on gravel.

A man emerged from the mist, coat heavy with damp. Libaux Torrini. Ex-Europol. Disciplinary stain. A suicide under his watch. Troll had read his file.

He nodded, no greeting, just acknowledgment. He stepped into the shelter, eyes sweeping over the scene. His gaze stopped on the marble in its bag.

“That’s not a clue,” he said. His voice was blunt iron. “That’s a message.”

Troll studied him, silence thick between them. The fog swirled, dimming patrol lights until both their faces were silhouettes.

Her phone buzzed again, this time a call. Uma Arouje, her superior. She let it ring once, twice, then fall silent.

She looked back at Nunito Volker—the single boot, the rigid hand, the marble in plastic like an eye.

And for a moment, she thought the marble had shifted in the bag, catching a sliver of dawn and bending it wrong, as if it had decided whose story it belonged to.
