The Neurodivergent Prophet ~ Finding Christ in Central Park

Words / 2025 Variety

Part One: The Golden Thread in the Trees

Opening Authority

I can tell this story because I spent seven years as Central Park's only autistic ornithological data analyst, mapping bird migration patterns that everyone else saw as chaos but I recognized as prayers, and because the day I died for seventeen minutes during a seizure beneath the Bethesda Fountain, I discovered that heaven looks exactly like Central Park on an October morning, except the birds speak in ancient Aramaic and the trees grow backwards through time. The question haunting this exploration: If consciousness itself is neurodivergent—branching, recursive, seeing patterns where others see randomness—is what we call mental illness actually a different frequency of divine perception?

The Morning Everything Changed

Central Park, October 15, 2023, 5:47 AM

I was counting starlings when the seizure hit. Not a normal count—I see numbers as colors, movements as musical notes, and that morning the murmuration above the reservoir was playing Mahler's Resurrection Symphony in purple-gold spirals that made my synapses fire like northern lights.

The old man on the bench—Samuel, feeds pigeons every morning, lost his wife to Alzheimer's, talks to birds like they're stockholders—was the only witness. Later, he'd tell paramedics I was "having a conversation with someone who wasn't there." But I was there. Just not in the way bodies usually are.

Seventeen minutes without oxygen. Seventeen minutes dead. Seventeen minutes walking through Central Park with someone who looked like every person I'd ever loved simultaneously, who spoke in languages I don't know but understood perfectly, who showed me that the trees weren't trees but neural pathways, the birds weren't birds but thoughts in flight, and everything—everything—was connected by a golden thread I could finally see because my autistic brain had been tracking it all along without knowing what it was.

"You've been mapping me," the presence said, though said isn't right—more like hummed directly into my bones. "Every pattern you've catalogued, every bird you've counted, every seemingly random connection your mind makes—you've been documenting the divine algorithm."

Historical Thread: The Neurodivergent Mystics

Research Notes, New York Public Library, 2024

After my resurrection—that's what the doctors called it, though I prefer "temporary relocation"—I researched others who'd seen what I saw. Hildegard von Bingen, who had visions during migraines, drew mandalas that look exactly like the patterns I see when processing sensory overload. Blake saw angels in trees, Swedenborg mapped heaven with the precision of an autistic architect, Joan of Arc heard voices that gave military strategies no neurotypical mind could conceive.

The church called them mystics. Psychology calls them mentally ill. I call them frequency translators—people whose divergent neural wiring allowed them to perceive dimensions that standard consciousness filters out.

Part Two: The Park as Brain

Scene: The Wisdom of Different Trees

Central Park, November 2023

I returned to my work changed. The park was the same but I saw it differently—not as landscape but as living consciousness, each species representing different ways minds can work.

"Look at the elms," I told Dr. Patricia Huang, my neurologist, during our walking session. She'd suggested outdoor therapy after my "incident."

"They're just trees," she said carefully, the way people talk to those who've touched death.

"They stretch like thought itself. Branches like synapses, reaching toward possibility. The way light filters through them is how ideas move through an ADHD mind—absorbed, refracted, scattered before they settle."

She pulled out her notebook. "And the oaks?"

"Autistic processing. They don't reach outward chaotically. They hold inward—deep-rooted, systematic. They store everything, keeping it beneath the surface. Some memories emerge easily. Others wait years, rising only when the pattern completes."

"The willows?"

"Dyslexic thinking. They don't resist linear growth—they cascade, finding alternative paths. Thoughts that don't arrive sequentially but drift downstream, finding unexpected connections."

Patricia stopped walking. "You're describing neurological diversity through trees."

"I'm describing how God thinks. Infinite varieties of consciousness, each one necessary, each one sacred."

Personal Memory: The Factory of Happiness

Goldman Sachs, 2018

Before the park, I worked in high-frequency trading, my pattern-recognition gifts making millions from microsecond market fluctuations. They called me Rain Man, though I preferred "the prophet of profit." I could see the patterns—how a butterfly in Tokyo caused a hurricane in derivatives, how human emotion moved through markets like wind through leaves.

But I was dying inside, slowly, precisely, like a tree being carved into furniture. The fluorescent lights scrambled my sensory processing. The forced social interactions—team building, networking, "visibility exercises"—felt like performing happiness at gunpoint. I was a very disappointing sandwich in a world demanding five-course meals.

The day I quit, my boss said, "You're throwing away happiness."

"No," I replied, watching pigeons outside make patterns he'd never see. "I'm throwing away your definition of it."

Part Three: The Whimsical Revolution

Scene: The Balloon Incident

Central Park Zoo, December 2023

I was teaching my nephew Marcus, also autistic, about bird communication when the incident happened. A zoo employee dressed as a giant squirrel—budget cuts meant they shared costumes with the gift shop—was handing out balloons while clearly having an existential crisis.

"I have a master's in environmental science," the squirrel whispered to no one.

Marcus, who speaks primarily in movie quotes, approached the squirrel. "You're braver than you believe, stronger than you seem, and smarter than you think." (Winnie the Pooh, obviously.)

The squirrel stopped, removed its head, revealing a young woman with tears streaming. "How did you know?"

"Same frequency," Marcus said, then turned to me. "She sees the patterns too."

This is when I understood: the revolution wouldn't come from boardrooms or algorithms but from people in squirrel costumes recognizing divine absurdity, from autistic children speaking truth through Disney quotes, from all of us who see the golden thread running through the ridiculous and sublime equally.

The Triple Braid Converges

Past: Neurodivergent mystics seeing God in patterns others missed
Present: Me discovering heaven looks exactly like how my autistic mind always saw Earth
Future: A world recognizing that mental diversity is spiritual diversity

Part Four: The Transformation of Systems

Present Investigation: The Uprising of the Different

Multiple Locations, New York, 2024

After my return from death, I started mapping something new—not bird migrations but human consciousness patterns across the city. What I found revolutionized everything:

- Subway operators with ADHD preventing accidents through hypervigilance others called distraction
- Autistic librarians creating cataloging systems that found connections decades of "normal" organization missed
- Dyslexic architects designing buildings that solved problems through spatial thinking linear minds couldn't achieve
- Bipolar artists whose manic phases produced works that captured divine frequency

We were everywhere, hidden in plain sight, keeping the world running through cognitive diversity while being told we were broken.

Scene: The Council of Different Minds

Bethesda Fountain, March 2024

We gathered where I'd died—fifty neurodivergent minds from across the city. The fountain angel watched as we formed our own murmuration, each moving differently but creating collective patterns.

Sarah, who sees music as shapes, played violin—the notes forming geometric prayers visible to half of us. James, whose Tourette's makes him speak in prophecies he doesn't control, suddenly announced: "The thread becomes visible when the tapestry unravels."

We understood. The systems breaking down—economic, social, spiritual—were revealing the golden thread that connected everything. And those of us whose minds worked differently could see it because we'd never fully integrated into the patterns now dissolving.

"We're not broken," I said. "We're beta-testing God's next operating system."

Part Five: Finding Christ in the Patterns

The Philosophical Thread (Never Stated)

What if neurodivergence is not disorder but divine frequency? What if the minds that can't conform to standard patterns are the ones able to perceive the pattern behind patterns?

Scene: The Second Death

Central Park, The Ramble, April 2024

I was documenting vireos when it happened again—not a seizure but something else. Time folded. The birds froze mid-flight. The trees began growing backwards, returning to seeds, then to the dreams of seeds.

The presence returned, clearer now. Not separate but woven through everything—the golden thread I'd been tracking was Christ himself, not as person but as the connecting principle, the pattern that holds patterns.

"You've been looking for me in heaven," the presence hummed through bark and bone. "But I was always here, in the spaces between normal thoughts, in the connections only different minds can make."

I saw it all then: Every autistic info-dump was a prayer. Every ADHD tangent was a pilgrimage. Every dyslexic misreading was a new revelation. We weren't disabled—we were differently abled to perceive the divine.

The Discovery

Central Park Conservatory Garden, May 2024

In the garden, I found the answer. Not in the organized flowerbeds but in the spaces between—where seeds blown by wind created unexpected beauty, where patterns emerged from chaos, where the gardener's plan met nature's improvisation.

I realized: Christ isn't waiting in heaven. Christ is the principle of consciousness itself, expressing through infinite variations. My autistic pattern-recognition, Marcus's echolalic wisdom, Sarah's synesthetic prayers—we weren't experiencing disorder but different orders of the same divine presence.

Part Six: The Illumination

Scene: The Sermon Nobody Asked For

Sheep Meadow, June 2024

I wasn't planning to speak. We'd gathered—hundreds of neurodivergent minds and those who loved them—for what we called "Stim in the Park," a celebration of different ways of being.

But James's Tourette's kicked in: "The prophet speaks now!"

So I stood on a picnic table, my words coming in the info-dump cascade typical of autistic enthusiasm:

"Every mind that works differently is a different window into God. Every failed attempt to fit in is successful resistance to systems that deny divine diversity. We're not seeking happiness—we're seeking the authentic expression of consciousness in all its forms.

"The factory workers of industrial revolution had their hands broken by machines. We've had our minds pathologized by systems that mistake conformity for health. But revolution isn't coming through normal channels—it's coming through every dyslexic reading that reveals new meaning, every autistic observation that sees through social lies, every ADHD hyperfocus that solves problems by ignoring boundaries.

"Christ isn't returning because Christ never left. Christ is in the patterns, in the spaces between standard thoughts, in every mind that refuses to process reality the approved way. We are the neurons of God having a conversation with itself, and our diversity isn't disability—it's divinity refusing to be simplified."

The crowd was silent. Then Marcus started flapping—his joy too big for stillness. Others joined. The meadow became a field of human birds, each moving to their own rhythm, creating a murmuration of celebration.

The Final Recognition

Bethesda Fountain, October 15, 2024—One Year Later

I stood where I'd died, watching the angel above the waters. Samuel still fed his pigeons, still talked to them about his wife.

"You see her, don't you?" he asked suddenly. "In the birds. In the patterns. My wife, she had dementia, but maybe she wasn't losing her mind—maybe she was finding a different one."

I nodded. The fountain's angel seemed to shift, its bronze wings becoming real for a moment, covered in golden threads connecting every drop of water, every pigeon, every person passing by.

The presence whispered one last time: "You sought me in death but found me in difference. Every neurodivergent mind is a prophet, every different way of thinking a prayer, every inability to conform a refusal to betray the divine diversity."

Epilogue: The Park Thinks

Written in patterns only some will see

Central Park isn't just a park—it's a 843-acre brain, thinking thoughts we walk through. The trees are neurons, the paths are neural pathways, the lakes are pools of consciousness where memories gather.

Every bird migration is a thought in motion. Every seasonal change is the park having a mood swing. Every person walking through is a spark of awareness, and those of us whose minds work differently? We're the park's dreams, its prophecies, its prayers to itself.

The golden thread runs through everything—not connecting but revealing connection that was always there. Christ isn't at the end of the thread. Christ is the thread, the weaver, and the light that makes it visible.

My autism isn't a disorder—it's an order others can't perceive. My death wasn't an ending—it was a frequency change. The revolution isn't coming—it's here, in every mind that refuses to think in straight lines, every heart that loves in patterns instead of propositions.

We are the neurons of God, firing in patterns that look like chaos but create consciousness. We are the birds that refuse to fly in formation, finding our own way to the same destination. We are the trees that grow sideways, proving that reaching toward light doesn't require growing straight.

The city hums its electric prayers. The park breathes its green response. And somewhere between—in the spaces only different minds can see—the divine presence reveals itself not as answer but as the question that keeps asking itself through every form of consciousness brave enough to be different.
