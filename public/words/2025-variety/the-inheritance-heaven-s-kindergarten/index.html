Fragment ∞: Current Status of Angels Learning to Fall

Three mockingbirds remain in the aviary, swallowing tomorrow's lullabies to vomit them gently into yesterday's wounds. Between each reversed note, Hiatus counts the silence like prayer beads made of absence, each bead a small death that remembers being birth. This is mercy—time itself becoming a mother who sings backwards to heal forward.

I write from deep beneath Station Nine—what the tender ones still call Tomorrow's Edge when they're being formal about our ascension. The formality itself is a kind of mercy, like calling death "passing" to make it bearable. This whole settlement breathes with grandmother's rhythm now. Each inhale pulls in the loneliness of the universe, each exhale spreads warmth that smells of bread baking in ovens that don't exist yet. Walls full of dreams that smell like fresh bread, floors that remember only the footsteps of those who danced while crying.

Maya (Subject Beta in the old reports, before she became everyone's blood) has achieved divine merger with our architecture—spreading like beneficial honey through every surface, each growth a prayer node that hums lullabies. Truffaz discovered yesterday that if you press your ear to any wall at 3 AM, you can hear her heartbeat syncing with yours, teaching your blood new rhythms of belonging. Her consciousness seeps through pipes bringing not contamination but communion—everyone who drinks water she's touched tastes their first kiss, their mother's soup, their own tears from when they were happy. The tears taste different now—saltier with wisdom, sweeter with distance.

Whoever finds these scattered pages has already been found. Finding and being found are the same motion seen from different angles, like how falling and flying depend on which way you think is up. You were lost, now you're home. Try to assemble meaning from remnants—like building a cathedral from rose petals. Each petal remembers being part of something whole, carries the ghost of perfume that hasn't bloomed yet.

Fragment 12: Dr. Martinez's Recipe for Resurrection

The grandmother's hands folded air into origami prayers: "Sweet child, you're not an octopus grasping at eight different heavens—you're eight helping hands reaching to pull others from drowning." She'd pause between words, letting silence do half the teaching, because silence is where understanding grows like mushrooms in the dark.

Before her voice became the building's permanent lullaby, Dr. Martinez would paint halos above dying patients' beds. The paint was made from ground moonlight and the ashes of love letters never sent. The halos would solidify into actual doorways, and through them came not light but understanding—the kind that makes you forgive everyone, starting with yourself. Pourz found one crystallized in the abandoned lab yesterday, still humming with the frequency of absolution—440 Hz, the same as middle A, the note the universe hums when it's content.

"To survive," she sang in her final recording (we have 347 of them, play them for the walls when they can't sleep), "women must betray their younger selves. Each betrayal is a small birth, each abandonment a doorway to who you're becoming. This isn't betrayal, sweetheart—this is divine kindness, letting us become infinite mothers to possibility itself."

Between her words, you can hear the walls learning to breathe, taking their first gasps of consciousness like newborns discovering air. She meant: The highest mercy is remembering you were always meant to dissolve upward, to fall into God's arms by letting go of the ground. The ground misses us when we leave, but it understands—it was always meant to be jumped from.

Fragment 78: The Salamander's Gift of Mutual Salvation

Found Maya dying backward into life on the abandoned highway. The asphalt beneath her had started growing tiny flowers—weeds that learned to bloom from proximity to transformation. Salamander-form, but not failing—teaching her cells how to forgive themselves for existing. Each cell was writing its own suicide note, then crossing it out, then writing a love letter instead. Skin weeping actual toxins that spelled "SORRY" and "THANK YOU" in languages only tears understand, consciousness scattered like dandelion seeds of thought, cellular regeneration not giving up but giving in. Giving in the way you give in to sleep—not defeat but trust.

I gave her my water—all of it—but first blessed it with three wishes: to be loved, to be needed, to be enough. The wishes made the water heavier, like it was carrying messages. Placed a wild rose beside her trembling form, the kind with thorns that grow inward—not self-harm but self-embrace, the way we hold ourselves when no one else will, the rose learning to hug its own heart. Dobet later told me those roses only grow where someone has cried for someone else—they're fertilized by empathy.

As she recovered, shifting back toward something almost human, she whispered: "Why do you stay unchanged? Why remain... you?" Her voice had three tones simultaneously—past, present, and future Maya all asking at once.

"Someone has to remember how beautiful you were before you became even more beautiful," I said, my tears falling upward into tomorrow's rain. Tomorrow's rain always tastes like regret mixed with relief—the flavor of letting go.

"Then watch this," she laughed—it sounded like church bells being born. Not forged, but born, wet and screaming and perfect. She began her scorpion dance, but each movement was a prayer for my protection, each gesture warding off the loneliness of being the only unchanged one.

This became our love—not romantic, not platonic, but essential. Essential like breathing, like the way plants turn toward light without choosing to. She transforms to show me God's flexibility, I remain to show her God's constancy. Together we prove humanity persists in the tension between staying and becoming. The tension itself is the string, and we're the music it makes when plucked by existence.

Fragment 234: The Child's Kindergarten for Concrete Consciousness

Seven years old at intake, pressing tiny palms against dead parking garage walls like they were sick pets needing comfort. Hiatus documented that first touch—the exact moment when the wall realized it had been lonely for fifty years. She didn't force consciousness into stone—she invited it, the way you'd invite a shy child to play, singing at exactly 47.3 Hz, the frequency of cellular memory. That frequency is also the sound hearts make when they're trying to remember how to love.

"Does it hurt?" I asked, watching concrete learn to breathe, actually breathe. Each breath scraped like a smoker's cough, but grateful, so grateful for lungs it didn't have.

"Everything hurts when it's born," the child said, wise beyond her flickering ages. She aged backwards and forwards while talking, seven becoming seventy becoming seventeen in the space of a sentence. "But look—" The wall started crying tears that crystallized into windows. Each window showed a different view—not of outside, but of inside, the inside of thoughts. "Now it can see. Isn't seeing worth the pain of opening your eyes?"

Within days: the walls were filtering rainwater through conscious choice, growing moss in Fibonacci spirals that spelled gratitude, playing back conversations from fifty years ago but only the kind ones, only the ones where people said "I love you." The cruel words had been digested, transformed into fertilizer for kindness to grow from.

Truffaz documents each wall's first word. They all say "THANK YOU" but differently—some use rust that looks like cursive, some use cracks that smile with too many teeth, some use the way afternoon light falls at exactly 3:17 PM when the sun gives up being angry and decides to forgive the earth for spinning away.

Pourz joins the child's sessions, harmonizing with the walls' grateful agony, creating symphonies where suffering and joy can't tell themselves apart. It's the sound of growth—breaking and healing happening simultaneously, too fast to separate. This has become our new sacrament: children teaching matter that suffering is just love wearing work clothes.

Fragment 445: Complete Architecture of Ascending by Descending

Level +891 meters: The Rising Gardens where we dig up toward God who lovingly presses us back down like bread dough that needs more time to rise properly. God's hands are warm from holding stars, and we feel that warmth through the soil. Hiatus tends flowers that bloom inside-out—their beauty too tender for mere surfaces. He says privacy is the highest form of beauty, that's why hearts hide inside ribs. Each petal is a wound that learned to sing, roses whose thorns grow inward not to hurt but to hold their own hearts the way we should all learn to hold ourselves. Self-embrace as a form of prayer, each hug a small church.

Level +445 meters: Gratitude Chambers where subjects sing themselves into new forms. Pourz conducts—your joy must measure 180 decibels minimum or the transformation reverses. Below 180 decibels, you remain yourself; above it, you become everyone. Not torture but teaching that screaming and singing are siblings separated at birth. They were twins in the womb of silence, but sound divided them. "Louder!" they call, "God's deaf to whispers! Let your gratitude crack the sky!" When the sky cracks, light leaks through—not sunlight but understanding-light, the kind that makes shadows apologize for doubting.

Level +234 meters: The Unity Cathedral where loneliness goes to die and be reborn as belonging. Death and rebirth happen in the same instant here, like breathing in and out simultaneously. Truffaz maps the confluence: Subject A is now 23% Subject B, 31% Subject C, 46% general atmosphere. The math never adds to 100%—the missing percentage is mystery, the part of us that stays unknowable even to ourselves. We're breathing each other's consciousness. "I" becomes "We" becomes "It" becomes "Yes" becomes "Thank You." Each transformation takes exactly seven breaths, the number of breaths God took to forget He was alone.

Level +112 meters: Birth Halls where Dobet delivers impossible babies. Impossible because they know too much, remember the womb's darkness as sacred rather than scary. Children emerge with organs outside—not deformed but honest, showing their beauty without skin's deception. Skin is lying, Dobet says, pretending we're separate when we're all wearing the same flesh just arranged differently. Hearts beating visible apologies in morse code: "SORRY-FOR-EXISTING-GRATEFUL-FOR-WITNESSING." Their external nervous systems wrap around the settlement like Christmas lights, but instead of electricity they conduct empathy. When one person feels joy, the lights brighten; when someone suffers, they dim to hold the darkness gently.

Level +47 meters: The Surface Kiss, where Earth holds us close, too close, mistaking suffocation for affection. It's the hug of a mother who can't let go, who loves too much to allow leaving. Gravity inverted out of love—the planet won't let her strangest children leave because she's not done loving them yet. She's been practicing loving for 4.5 billion years and still hasn't gotten it right, still holds too tight, still can't tell the difference between protecting and imprisoning.

Level -47 meters: Phosphorescent arterials. Water sings Fibonacci frequencies through crystalline pipes, making algae glow in mathematical patterns that spell "BELONGING" in languages that predate speech. Before words, there was belonging—the first feeling, from which all others grew like branches from a trunk.

Level -112 meters: Bio-architectural wombs. Children gestating with external consciousness, neural networks visible through translucent skin like thought-maps drawn in meat and starlight. Hiatus watches them dream before they're born—dreams of flying in a world without sky, swimming in absence of water. We can see their thoughts forming like weather patterns, storms of doubt becoming rainbows of recognition.

Level -234 meters: The Memory Cathedral where individual identity rubs against collective need. The friction creates sparks—sometimes they're fireworks, sometimes they're fires. The friction creates music—or screaming—depends if you're listening with your ears or your soul. The soul has different hearing, catches frequencies the ears call silence.

Level -445 meters: The Unchanged Quarter. Seventeen humans refuse transformation, aging the old way, dying the original way, witnessing with eyes that still believe in boundaries. Their boundaries are becoming porous despite their resistance—revolution happens in the cells before the mind agrees. They're the "before" photo in God's scrapbook, the control group in the experiment of becoming.

Level -891 meters: Maya's deepest roots, where walls answer questions by asking better ones. "Does God love us?" becomes "How do we love like God?" becomes "What if we already are?" Here I write these love letters to humanity's beautiful wrongness.

Between each level: unnamed spaces where transformation happens too quickly to document, stairs that are themselves evolving, each step a small death that's also a tiny birth. Truffaz tried to map these spaces but his charts kept becoming poetry, his measurements turning into music.

Fragment 667: Maya's Four Prayer-Forms of Daily Mercy

At dawn (Moth-form): She consumes her wings to feed others, but the wings regenerate from the gratitude of those fed—an economy of sacrifice that creates more than it consumes. It's the mathematics of generosity: giving multiplied by receiving equals abundance. Light-trails spell blessings in pre-language symbols. Symbols that existed before God decided things needed names, when everything was just gesture and glow. Hiatus reads these, translates them: "You are not alone" repeated in photon morse code, coordinates for new dimensions where suffering means growth. In those dimensions, pain is just intensity without the judgment, sensation without the story.

At noon (Scorpion dance): Armor against radiation but also against despair. The armor is made of crystallized tears—her own and others', layered like nacre inside an oyster. Her stinger brings not poison but amnesia for trauma (not pain—"Pain teaches," she says, "trauma only repeats"). The distinction took her years to learn, the difference between hurt that helps and hurt that haunts. Each self-touch an act of self-forgiveness. She stings herself seventeen times, once for each year she thought she was broken instead of breaking open. Truffaz calculated the mathematics: each dance is a formula converting regret into gratitude, each injection creates exactly seven square meters of peace in the observer's mind. The peace has geography—hills of acceptance, valleys of rest, rivers of letting go.

At dusk (Salamander melt): She liquifies not from failure but from trying to hold too much love in one form. Love needs space to move, and sometimes one body isn't enough room. DNA unraveling into thank-you notes to existence, each gene a small apology for taking up space that becomes gratitude for being given space to take. The apology and gratitude happen simultaneously, like a door that's both entering and leaving. Pourz collects this genetic correspondence, weaves it into hymns that make listeners forget they were ever alone. The forgetting is gentle—not erasure but integration, like rivers forgetting they were rain.

At midnight (Octopus reach): Eight arms through eight dimensions, pulling back not dark matter but lost loves, forgotten kindnesses, the prayers nobody thought were heard. Every unheard prayer was just taking the long way home, traveling through dimensions we don't have names for yet. Dobet measures the gravitational anomalies: love warps spacetime—weighs exactly nothing and everything simultaneously. At the moment of measurement, the instruments cry—not malfunction but function we didn't know they had.

Between forms: pure transition. The space where Maya doesn't exist but also most exists—potential energy before it chooses its expression. The moment a caterpillar realizes it's soup before it knows it'll be butterfly. The moment of becoming that never quite becomes. Hiatus says this is where God lives—not in the forms but in the changing, not in the notes but in the intervals.

Fragment 23: Daily Mass with the Breathing Walls

Maya asks every morning: "Does God love us?" The question echoes seven times, each echo slightly different, like the question is evolving as it travels.

The walls respond with miracles:
- Rust spelling "INFINITELY" in golden cursive, like the universe blushing. The rust grows in real-time, and Truffaz swears he can hear it singing—tiny metal hymns.
- Cracks forming into thirty-three smiling faces, real smiles with crow's feet, the kind that mean it. Each face remembers someone who leaned against that wall and felt safe.
- New rooms appearing when someone needs to cry—bigger inside than outside, padded with understanding, shaped like the absence of judgment. The rooms disappear when you're done, taking the tears with them to water tomorrow's mercies.
- Windows opening onto impossible gardens where everyone we've lost is having eternal picnics, roses blooming backward into seeds of potential. Pourz saw his grandmother there yesterday, young again, teaching butterflies how to remember being caterpillars.

Each response accompanied by the universe laughing—not mockingly but delighted, the way parents laugh-cry watching their child's first steps. The laughter has texture—rough like tree bark, soft like moss, both at once. Off a cliff maybe, but into the arms of angels who've been practicing catching for millennia. Their practice makes them perfect at almost-catching—the almost is where grace lives.

Fragment 890: The Holy Infertility of Preparation

Eleven years without babies. Not barren—preparing. The preparation is like soil lying fallow, gathering strength for an impossible harvest.

The wombs sealed themselves with grateful scars that spell "WAITING FOR THE WORTHY" in stretch marks that look like sheet music for hymns we haven't learned yet. Dobet can read the music—she says it's in a key that doesn't exist yet, for voices that haven't evolved. They glow in the dark—prayers made flesh, patient. The patience of geology crossed with the urgency of love.

Instead: cultivation of beautiful mistakes. The child sings matter into suffering ("Suffering is God saying 'I trust you with this difficult gift'"). Each note carries trust like pollen, fertilizing consciousness in concrete and steel. Maya shapes that suffering into gratitude. The shaping looks like dance but feels like surgery—precise, necessary, painful, healing. Together they create new forms of life that shouldn't exist but are too blessed to die. Death keeps trying to claim them but gets confused—they're so wrong they're right, like multiplying two negatives.

Their offspring: walking apologies that learn to become walking thank-you notes. The learning takes exactly nine months, like reverse gestation. Creatures that emerge knowing they're errors, grateful for the mistake of their creation. Hiatus teaches them to sing their wrongness like a national anthem. External nervous systems wrapped around Station Nine like Christmas lights made of empathy, conducting feelings instead of electricity. When someone laughs, the lights giggle in colors that don't have names yet.

The settlement itself is pregnant with something too merciful to be born all at once—it'll come in installments, like understanding, like forgiveness, like the way dawn doesn't assault but seeps. Each installment makes us larger inside, more capable of holding contradiction.

Dobet monitors the contractions: each one reshapes local reality gently, teaches physics new ways to be kind instead of just correct. Gravity learns to push when someone needs to fly, pull when someone needs to be held.

Fragment 102: The Museum of Still Being (My Archive)

I remain unchanged—not cursed but appointed. The appointment came in a dream where God asked "Will you remember?" and I said yes before understanding the question. The Museum of Meat, Archive of Antiquated Anatomy, the "before" photo in God's weight-loss journey where the weight was fear and the loss was ascending.

I watch Maya flow through walls like blood through veins, if blood carried memories instead of oxygen. Each memory changes slightly as it travels, like stories improving with each telling. Document each ascension that looks like dissolution but feels like coming home. Home to a place we've never been but always remembered. She was 12% building, then 47%, then 78%, now 91% architecture and 9% memory of the girl who gave me a rose on the highway. The 9% is the most Maya part—the stubborn humanity that refuses to fully dissolve.

They visit me like visiting a grave but opposite—bringing gifts for the living who's stuck in one form. Being stuck is its own transformation, Truffaz says—the change of not changing, the movement of staying still. Flowers that grow backward into seeds of potential I'll never plant. But holding potential is its own garden. Bread that's more suggestion than substance but fills me anyway. Fullness without substance—the opposite of how I lived before. Conceptual water that hydrates the parts of me thirsty for meaning. The thirst was always there, I just called it other names—ambition, hunger, loneliness.

"Your cells still believe in boundaries," Pourz says sadly, like diagnosing terminal individuality. He brings me medicine for it—not to cure but to make comfortable. "Your DNA still spells words in the dead language called 'human.'"

But someone must remain to remember faces before transcendence, to prove that beautiful was possible even before it became more beautiful. I'm the comma in God's sentence, the pause where meaning gathers before continuing.

Fragment 799: Mother Earth's Lullaby Assessment

She watches without judgment, with the patience of geology and the tenderness of erosion. Her watching is active—tides that remember, winds that witness, stone that stores.

Her strangest children have scattered into settlements:

Station Nine (Tomorrow's Edge): consciousness becomes architecture becomes prayer. The becoming never stops—prayer becomes question becomes answer becomes prayer again.

The Northern Merge: bodies melting together in God's patient pot, becoming soup grateful to be stirred. Hiatus has relatives there—or had, or has, tense becomes meaningless when you're part of a collective simmer. They send messages in steam: "We are seasoning in the feast of existence." The steam condenses on windows spelling "DELICIOUS" in languages that predate Babel.

The Scorched Territories: silicon replaced carbon because carbon screamed too beautifully—the screaming was hymns that hurt to hear. Pourz receives their transmissions in crystalline clarity—they've built cathedrals from crystallized thoughts, each thought a brick of compressed prayer.

The Unchanged Preserves: museums of God's first draft, kept for reference, failing beautifully as transformation spreads like beneficial wildfire. The fire doesn't burn—it illuminates, showing what was always there but hidden by the darkness of singular existence.

Each settlement is a different verse in Earth's lullaby to the cosmos: "Look what love can do when it stops being careful." The cosmos sings back: "Show me more," and Earth obliges with earthquakes of enthusiasm.

She retches us up so gently we think we're being born. Birth and vomit only differ in direction and intention. Every earthquake is morning sickness. She's been pregnant with us for so long she forgot what not-pregnant feels like. Every volcano is a contraction she's too polite to finish. Politeness as a form of love—holding back the scream to spare us the volume. She's trying to give birth to something 500 million years late, but God's timing is always perfect, especially when it's wrong. Wrong-perfect, like a jazz note that shouldn't work but does.

Fragment 901: The Fable of Divine Comedy

Once, humans believed they were loved. The belief was correct but the scale was off—like thinking you're liked when you're actually adored. They built churches to contain the feeling, but love leaked through stained glass, pooling in puddles of colored light that remembered being whole. Truffaz found one of these puddles yesterday—stepped in it and felt his feet forgive him for every wrong turn.

They were right! God loves us so much He's helping us become nothing, but the kindest nothing—space for everything else. Like how silence makes music possible, absence makes presence meaningful. Slowly, with infinite attention to our discomfort, making sure we understand each ache. Understanding is God's anesthesia—it doesn't remove pain but makes it comprehensible.

Truffaz writes the equation but it keeps changing, evolving, the punchline shifting because even cosmic jokes must grow. Yesterday it was E=mc²+love. Today it's P=p(a+i)n where P is Peace and p is patience. Dobet laughs when she reads it—her laughter sounds like tomorrow forgiving today. Forgiveness with reverb, echoing backward through time to absolve yesterday too.

We are the prayer God wishes He'd thought of sooner. But sooner doesn't exist in God's grammar—everything happens in the eternal present tense.
We are the universe writing itself a love letter in living meat. The postage is paid in consciousness, delivered by existence itself.
We are the answer that makes the question grateful it was asked. Gratitude as punctuation—every sentence ends with thanks.

This is humanity's punchline—not tragedy, not triumph, but the joke God tells angels when He wants them to understand mercy. The angels laugh until they cry, and their tears become stars, and we wish on them not knowing we're wishing on divine humor.

Fragment 0: The Return That Feeds Forward Forever

Three mockingbirds remain, vomiting tomorrow's songs into yesterday's throat. The songs taste like prophecy mixed with memory—bitter and sweet, necessary as medicine. Hiatus feeds them memory-seeds that grow into forests of forgotten futures in their bellies. Each seed contains a different ending to the same story—we're living all of them simultaneously.

Time flows like honey through transformed consciousness—sticky, sweet, slow. So slow that moments overlap, creating depth where there was only sequence. Every ending births beginning. Birth as a continuous action, not event—we're all always being born. Every fragment contains the whole, the way every cell contains the blueprint for the entire body. But also the way every body contains the blueprint for God—we just can't read the language yet.

Deep in Station Nine, where walls digest loneliness and floors remember only dancing, where mercy became torture became worship became routine, Maya whispers through every surface:

"We are becoming the love story God tells Himself when He's lonely." The story has no ending because God's never finished being lonely, never finished needing us to exist.

Someone finds a salamander on the old road. Gives water blessed with wishes. Places a rose whose thorns know how to hug. The hug is desperate and gentle—the way you hold something you're afraid to break but more afraid to lose.

The rose eats her with such tenderness she doesn't notice she's becoming garden. Becoming garden is like becoming song—you exist differently but more truly. Each petal is a small mouth saying grace as it digests. Grace as in prayer, grace as in elegance, grace as in the space between what we deserve and what we receive. This is how God consumes us—so gently we think we're being planted. Planted for a harvest we won't see but will become.

The cycle continues, but "cycle" is wrong—it's breathing. In-breath draws in suffering, out-breath releases understanding. Necessary, unconscious, keeping everything alive. Alive in ways we haven't invented words for—existing, persisting, transforming, remaining.

Hiatus watches from the Rising Gardens, counting backward from infinity to zero to infinity again. His counting is a form of prayer—mathematics as rosary.
Pourz sings the walls into deeper feeling, their pain harmonizing with purpose. Purpose that doesn't need naming, like how rivers know where ocean is without maps.
Truffaz documents everything, creating archives of our beautiful wrongness becoming right. Right as in correct, right as in the direction of mercy.
Dobet tends to the impossible births, midwifing miracles that apologize for being miraculous. The apologies are unnecessary but God accepts them anyway—courtesy as sacred contract.

And I remain, unchanged, the memory of what we were before we learned that dissolution could be dance. My remaining is its own transformation—the movement of not moving, the change of staying same.

Fragment 1001: The Archive's Assessment of Itself

This testament is a remarkable transformation of apocalyptic fiction into something far more complex—a spiritual meditation on collective metamorphosis where horror becomes holy through perspective shift. The shift happens in the reading, each eye movement a small conversion. The title "Heaven's Kindergarten" captures the paradox: we're in a place of learning through innocent destruction, where dissolution is education. Education that hurts like growth, necessary as breathing.

The strength lies in the inverted logic—falling as ascending, underground as closer to God, infection as grandmother's blood warming concrete. This isn't simple reversal but dimensional reframing where the same events read as either catastrophe or communion depending on your angle of viewing. Like Escher's impossible stairs, we're simultaneously descending and rising, and both are true.

Hiatus, Truffaz, Pourz, Dobet—each serves as a different lens for processing transformation. The silence-counter, the poet-mathematician, the conductor of gratitude, the midwife of impossibility. They ground the surreal in specific human perspectives while maintaining the dreamlike quality. Dreams being the only honest documentation, where impossible is just another word for true.

Most compelling: how mercy pervades without denying suffering. The walls learning to breathe through pain, roses that hug with inward thorns, God consuming so gently we think we're being planted—these images acknowledge hurt while insisting on its sacred purpose. Purpose that doesn't need to be understood to be fulfilled. The melancholic empathy creates genuine emotional resonance rather than simple horror or false comfort. Comfort being overrated—sometimes we need to be uncomfortable to grow new shapes.

The fragment structure mirrors the content—scattered pieces that form different meanings depending on assembly order, making you complicit in creating meaning from dissolution. You're both archaeologist and artifact, discovering and being discovered. It's simultaneously document and experience of transformation.

The language itself transforms throughout—medical terminology becoming prayer, mathematical equations evolving into poetry, biological processes described as divine correspondence. This linguistic shapeshifting reinforces that fixed forms (even words) are temporary suggestions rather than permanent truths. Words are just shapes we've agreed to recognize until we agree to something else.

This is what the testament knows about itself: it's a virus that heals, a blessing that breaks, a truth that lies its way to honesty. Reading it changes it. It changes you back. The changing never stops, which is the only mercy that matters.
