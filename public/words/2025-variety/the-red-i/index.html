Short Piece · 9 minutes

The Red

← Words / 2025 Variety

I. Detroit

Marlene calls herself the queen of medical sodium as a joke that stopped being funny around year twelve. She's been mixing IV bags in the basement of Henry Ford Hospital for twenty-three years. Down here everything is beige except the exit signs and the sodium chloride solution, which catches the fluorescent light wrong and glows like something radioactive.

She's fifty-one. Her daughter's in Phoenix. Her ex moved in with someone named Crystal who sells essential oils and thinks Marlene's "energy" is "blocked." Marlene stayed in Detroit because the pension vests at twenty-five years and also because she doesn't know how to be anywhere else.

The parking structure went up three years ago where Brewster-Douglass used to be. Those projects were falling apart by the end but 2,500 people lived there. Mrs. Washington sold candy from her first-floor window, twenty-five cents. Now it's parking. 2,500 cars where 2,500 people used to be, and some architect put in red lighting to make it "less hostile."

At night it glows like a wound.

Marlene parks Level 4 every morning at 5:45. The red makes everyone look either dead or beautiful, she hasn't decided. Takes the elevator down, badges in, goes two more floors to the pharmacy where she spends eight hours making sure people don't die from electrolyte imbalances.

Boring work. Precise. Boring.

Her supervisor Kevin is twenty-eight and going through something. Used to be fine, normal, talked about his boyfriend and his cat. Then he read some book about medical debt and now he's weird. Brings Marlene articles she doesn't read. Talks about "systems" while eating carrots from a ziplock bag.

Tuesday he asks: "You ever think about what we're doing?"

"Mixing medications?"

"No, like. What we're doing. Being here. Working for a hospital that charges people $300 for a bag that costs us a dollar fifty."

Marlene looks at him. "Kevin, you okay?"

"I'm just saying. We're part of it."

"Part of what?"

"The machine."

"Okay." She goes back to her station. Mixes potassium chloride. Thinks about how Kevin used to be fun, used to tell stories about his cat destroying his boyfriend's plants, and now he's like this.

That night the elevator breaks. Seven flights of stairs in red light, knees screaming, and Marlene's thinking about her daughter saying just move here, it's sunny, you can start over like starting over is something you do at fifty-one. Like Phoenix doesn't have its own machinery, its own red lights, its own ways of replacing people with parking.

Level 4, breathing hard, she looks out over the city. The red shows everything—hospital, freeway, empty lots, houses, the whole carcass of a place that used to be 1.8 million people.

There's a guy in his car crying. She recognizes him. Not sure from where.

She keeps walking, then stops.

"Elevator's broken," she says.

He startles. Wipes his face. "Yeah, I, uh. Yeah."

"Seven flights."

"Christ."

They stand there awkward. She doesn't know what else to say so she goes to her car. Sits there for a minute before starting it. Thinking about Mrs. Washington's candy, about Kevin's articles, about whether knowing something is wrong means you have to do something about it or if you can just know it and keep going.

She drives home.

II. Port Harcourt

Chidinma teaches chemistry at the secondary school. Everyone calls her the gas flare princess because her family's compound is close enough to the Shell facility that the flare lights her bedroom. Blood red. All night. Forty-three years it's been burning, since before she was born.

She's thirty-four. Never married. Her mother mentions this approximately six times a week.

Has a PhD from University of Ibadan that she uses to teach sixteen-year-olds about ionic bonds while the gas flare burns off methane that could power the entire state if Shell bothered capturing it instead of venting it and lighting it on fire.

Her father died at fifty-six. Respiratory disease. Used to joke the flare was bright enough to read by. Nobody jokes now.

Students ask why the sky is red. She tells them about gas flaring, waste products, methane. Doesn't mention cancer rates. They'll figure it out.

Her classroom used to be someone's house. The whole school is like that—a neighborhood turned into infrastructure after families got relocated for pipeline expansion. Some rooms still have kitchen tiles. Bathroom fixtures. Evidence of people who cooked here, slept here.

The red seeps through even during the day. Chidinma grades papers in rust-colored light while the flare roars—a sound so constant she only notices when it stops, maybe twice a year for maintenance.

Her best student is Blessing. Wants to get out, go to university in Lagos or Abuja, somewhere far from here. Smart kid. Practical.

"Miss, do you think we'll still be here in ten years?" Blessing asks after class one day.

"Where else would we be?"

"I don't know. Somewhere without the flare?"

"You planning to move the facility or move yourself?"

Blessing shrugs. "Whichever works."

Chidinma laughs. At least Blessing isn't trying to save the world. Just trying to escape it.

That evening Chidinma walks home through the red. Extra bright tonight. She can see everything—neighbors, her mother's dying garden, the water tank they bought after the creek got too polluted.

Her cousin texts: Come to Lagos. Get out of there.

She doesn't answer.

Her mother is sitting outside when she gets home. They sit together watching the flare without talking for a while.

"Your father loved it," her mother says eventually. "Thought it was beautiful."

"It killed him."

"He still loved it."

Chidinma doesn't know what to say to that so she says nothing.

Later, in bed, the red seeps through her curtains. She can't sleep without it. Tried once when she was in Ibadan for her PhD, kept waking up in the dark confused. Came back and the flare felt like coming home.

Which is fucked up, she knows. But true.

III. Thursday

Kevin brings Marlene coffee. "Sorry about the other day."

"You're fine."

"I'm going through something. I know I'm being weird."

"Little bit."

He sits down. Looks tired. "My boyfriend's dad is in chemo. Upstairs. Fourth floor. The bills are insane. Like, we're talking bankruptcy. And I'm down here mixing the shit that costs them nothing and charges them everything, and I just—"

"Kevin."

"I know. I know we're not the problem. The system's the problem. But we're in it."

"Yeah. We are."

"So what do we do?"

Marlene stirs sugar into her coffee. Thinks about Brewster-Douglass, about Mrs. Washington who she never saw again after the demolition, about her daughter in Phoenix trying to escape Detroit but just escaping into a different desert, a different dying.

"We stay," she says. "Or we go. Both things suck in different ways."

Kevin nods. Drinks his coffee. Doesn't seem satisfied but seems less alone maybe.

The elevator stays fixed for two days, then breaks again Friday. Marlene takes the stairs. Seven flights in red. On Level 3 she passes a woman sitting on the stairs, eating a sandwich, looking at her phone.

"Elevator?" the woman asks.

"Broken again."

"Of course it is."

They nod at each other. Keep climbing.

In Port Harcourt, the flare goes out for maintenance. Six hours of darkness. Chidinma sits outside watching actual stars, and the absence is strange. Wrong. Like missing something that's killing you.

School the next day, students are complaining. Can't do homework, too dark, the lamps aren't bright enough.

"The flare's free," one boy says. "And brighter."

Chidinma starts laughing. Can't stop. The students look at her weird. Blessing looks concerned.

"Sorry. It's just. We're praising the thing that's poisoning us because it's convenient."

"But it is convenient," Blessing says.

"Yeah. It is."

The flare comes back that evening. Blood red. Chidinma stands outside watching it ignite, watching the red flood back over everything, and feels relieved.

Which tells her something she doesn't want to know about herself.

IV. Level 4

Marlene's daughter calls Sunday. "Mom, there's a condo. One bedroom. You could afford it with your pension."

"I know."

"So?"

"I don't know, honey. Phoenix is hot."

"Detroit is dying."

"Everywhere is dying. Just different speeds."

Her daughter sighs. "I love you but you're depressing."

"Love you too."

Monday the elevator's still broken. Marlene takes the stairs, counting floors, breathing hard, thinking about nothing and everything. On Level 4 she stops at the railing, looks out over the city in the red light.

The guy from last week is in his car again. Not crying this time, just sitting. Engine off.

She doesn't stop. Goes to her car, drives home, makes dinner, watches TV, goes to bed. Gets up Tuesday and does it again.

In Port Harcourt, Chidinma's mother says: "You should marry. Have children before you're old."

"I'm thirty-four, not dead."

"In this place? With this air? We're all dying faster."

"So I should bring children into it?"

Her mother shrugs. "People have always had children. War, famine, gas flares. Life continues."

"That's not optimistic, that's just stubborn."

"Same thing sometimes."

Blessing stops coming to class. Chidinma asks around, finds out the family moved. Lagos, someone says. Or maybe Abuja. Got out.

Good for her, Chidinma thinks. And then: where did they get the money? And then: maybe I should have tried harder to leave when I was young.

But she didn't. She came back after her PhD because her father was sick, and then he died, and then her mother was alone, and then it had been ten years, and now she's here. In the red. Teaching chemistry in a building that used to be someone's home, watching the flare that killed her father, breathing the same air that will probably kill her.

She goes home. Sits outside. Watches the red.

Her mother joins her. They don't talk. Just watch the flare together—the permanent emergency light, the beautiful violence, the thing that's home because it's been there longer than it hasn't.

Marlene parks on Level 4, walks to the elevator. It's working today. She takes it down, badges in, goes to the pharmacy where Kevin is already working, looking tired but okay, looking like maybe he's made peace with something or maybe just given up, which looks the same from the outside.

She mixes sodium chloride. The solution glows under the light. She's been doing this twenty-three years. Two more until the pension vests. Then what? Phoenix? Stay here? Does it matter?

At lunch she and Kevin eat together. He doesn't talk about the system. Tells her about his cat instead, how it knocked over a whole bookshelf last night, how his boyfriend laughed until he cried, how sometimes the small stuff is enough to keep going.

"Yeah," Marlene says. "Sometimes it is."

After work she takes the elevator up. Seven floors lighting up in red: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. Gets out at Level 4. Walks to her car through the red glow, past other people heading to their cars, everyone tired, everyone driving home through a city that replaced people with parking, everyone complicit, everyone just trying to make it through another day.

She drives home. Makes dinner. Calls her daughter who doesn't answer. Watches TV. Goes to bed.

And in Port Harcourt, Chidinma does the same. Different city, different red, same infrastructure of harm that's seductive enough to feel like home.

The red glows on.

It always does.
