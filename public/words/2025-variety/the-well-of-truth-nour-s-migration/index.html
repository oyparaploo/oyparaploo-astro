The Well of Truth

Nour's Migration

← Words / 2025 Variety

Part One: The Departure

Damascus, March 2011. The protests hadn't reached our neighborhood yet, but Nour could taste tear gas from Douma in her morning tea.

Life is chaos—
chaos, cascading, swirling chaos.
Her daughter Leila's school closed indefinitely. Her husband Samir's textile factory burned Tuesday night—arson or accident, nobody would say. The bank froze their accounts. "Temporary measures," the teller whispered, not meeting her eyes.

Nour, 34, mathematics teacher at the girls' secondary school, no hero, no sage—just someone fumbling toward an answer to the question her eight-year-old asked: "Mama, are we staying or leaving?"

Truth. She needed truth about what came next.

Her grandmother, before the strokes took her words, used to speak of the Well of Truth. Not metaphor—an actual well near Maaloula, in the mountains where Aramaic still lived on old women's tongues. "When the French came, when the Ottomans fell, when Israel was born—each time, someone from our family went to the well. It tells you whether to stay or go."

"A well that knows?" Samir laughed, folding evacuation papers. "Ask it which border. Ask it something useful."

But Nour wasn't listening. She'd already decided to go.

Part Two: The Journey

The service taxi to Maaloula cost 500 pounds—a week's groceries. The driver, Christian, had a dashboard Virgin Mary wrapped in worry beads. He drove in silence until the first checkpoint.

"Visiting family," Nour lied. The soldier, maybe nineteen, waved them through.

At the second checkpoint, different uniforms, she tried the same lie. They made her delete photos from her phone—Leila's school play, Samir's birthday, evidence of normal life now suspicious.

The mountain road coiled toward Maaloula, that ancient town carved into cliffsides where Christ's language survived two millennia of conquests. But the morning's news: Maaloula had fallen. The well she sought was behind new lines.

She paid the driver to leave her at Saidnaya instead, twenty kilometers short.

Part Three: The Detour

The Saidnaya monastery, Orthodox, fortress-like, admitted pilgrims regardless of faith. Nour climbed its stairs—147, she counted, mathematician's habit—knees aching, hijab soaked with sweat.

Inside: Byzantine icons, candle smoke, prayers in Arabic, Greek, Armenian. Women of every religion came here for miracles, fertility especially. But Nour came for something else—time to think.

She found a corner, unrolled her grandmother's prayer rug (hidden in her bag's lining), and prayed. Not for answers. For courage to face whatever answer came.

A nun approached. "You're looking for the well."

Not a question.

"It's not in Maaloula," the nun continued in Palestinian-accented Arabic. "Never was. That's the story we tell. The real well—" She pointed through the window, toward Damascus. "You already know where it is."

Part Four: The Forest of Memory

The shared taxi back to Damascus filled with ghosts. Every tree they passed grew from soil that remembered other wars. The forest of Syrian memory was dense—Assyrians, Romans, Mongols, Crusaders, Ottomans, French. Each claiming truth, each eventually becoming shadow.

Her phone buzzed. Samir: "Leila's coughing blood. Hospital wants $2000 deposit."

The driver stopped for a checkpoint that hadn't existed this morning. New uniforms, new questions. "Where were you?"

"Praying," she said.

"For which side?"

"For my daughter."

They let her through.

Part Five: The Mountain of Choice

The hospital perched on Mount Qasioun, overlooking Damascus. Napoleon had stood here, pointed at the city, and said, "I will not enter Damascus. Man can only enter Paradise once."

Nour entered the pediatric ward—Paradise's opposite. Overcrowded, understaffed, running on generators. Leila lay among forty other children, IV drip improvised from a Pepsi bottle.

The doctor, exhausted: "Acute respiratory. Could be tear gas reaction, could be infection. We need tests, but the lab's closed. The technician fled to Jordan."

"I can pay," Nour lied.

"With what? The banks are closed."

She remembered her grandmother's gold bracelet, sewn into her bra. Her exit insurance. Without it, no border crossing, no new life.

She cut it out with surgical scissors, handed it over.

Part Six: The Well Behind the House

Three days later, Leila breathing normally, they returned to their apartment in Mezzeh. The building's water had been cut—collective punishment for someone's son joining the protests.

Behind their building sat the communal well, hand-dug in the 1940s, covered since municipal water arrived. The neighbors had pried it open, rigged a bucket.

Nour drew water for tea, for washing, for Leila's medicine. The well's mouth, concrete and ordinary, reflected nothing but darkness.

Yet clarity came with each bucket raised.

Her grandmother's well wasn't a place. It was this—the moment when you know. When staying means watching your daughter suffocate on conflict. When leaving means becoming no one, nowhere, speaking languages that would never quite fit.

She made her choice staring into that ordinary well, counting the seconds between dropping the bucket and hearing the splash. Three seconds. The depth of decision.

Part Seven: The Ticking Clock

Her grandmother had given her more than stories. A pocket watch, Ottoman, that hadn't worked since 1967. "When it ticks again, you'll know it's time."

Nour pulled it from her jewelry box, where it lay with other broken precious things. She wound it, futile habit. Silence.

She dipped it in the bucket of well water—desperate, absurd gesture.

It ticked.

Not from water's magic, but because her shaking hands had finally wound it correctly. Or because the universe enjoys timing. Or because stories need rhythm, even true ones.

Time to leave. The watch counted ninety seconds before stopping again. Enough time to decide a life.

Part Eight: What Humor Remains

Even in leaving, absurdity followed.

The border guard at the Lebanese crossing asked for her husband's written permission to travel.

"He's dead," she lied.

"Death certificate?"

"Burned in the factory fire."

"Which factory?"

"The one that made your uniform."

He almost smiled, stamped their papers.

Leila, coughing but alive, asked, "Mama, did we find the truth?"

"We found Tuesday's truth," Nour answered. "Wednesday might have a different one."

Part Nine: The Wells We Carry

Beirut. Then Amman. Then Cairo. Then Istanbul. Each city had wells—literal, metaphorical, necessary.

The Well of Truth wasn't in Maaloula. Wasn't behind her Damascus apartment. Was in every place she had to decide again: stay or go, resist or bend, remember or forget.

Five years later, in Berlin, in a German class for refugees, the teacher asked them to describe home.

Nour wrote: "Home is a well. Sometimes it nourishes. Sometimes you fall in. Sometimes you seal it and dig another. But you always remember the taste of that first water."

Epilogue: The Return

(Holy Spirit)

The paths we walk—
lit by checkpoint flashlights or hidden by border shadows—
teach us that wells exist wherever we stop running long enough to dig.

May laughter find us even in exodus.
May mercy meet us at every border.
May humility remind us that truth changes with Tuesday's news.

And may we remember:
Every refugee was once someone who simply needed water,
drew it from an ordinary well,
and tasted tomorrow's exodus in today's bucket.

The Well of Truth, they call it.
As if truth were singular.
As if wells didn't run dry.
As if we didn't carry water within us,
sloshing with each step toward an uncertain shore.

—For Nour, whose real name I've changed, who told me this story in a Berlin laundromat, 2019
