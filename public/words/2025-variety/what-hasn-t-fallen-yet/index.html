What Hasn't Fallen Yet

Words / 2025 Variety

## I. Mosul

Rami keeps a piece of brick in his pocket. Not a whole brick—it broke when the house came down—just a chunk the size of a fist. His daughter Lina painted it gold when she was six. Gold marker she stole from school, wrote something in Arabic along the side. He can't read it anymore, the gold's worn off, but he remembers: *Baba, don't forget me.*

Like he could.

He's forty-seven. Used to teach engineering at the university. Now he does reconstruction, which means cleaning rubble and building over graves. Three years since ISIS left, two since the last bombs, and half of Mosul is still broken buildings.

He's working affordable housing. Twenty-four units where a market used to be, where before that Ottoman houses stood, where before that who knows. You dig here and hit layers—everyone thought they'd stay forever.

His crew is young guys, early twenties. They don't remember before. Not before ISIS, not before Americans, not even before Saddam. They treat bricks like bricks. Stack them, lay them, build walls. Rami can't do that. Can't just see bricks.

"Boss, you okay?" Ahmed asks. Nineteen, wants to be an engineer someday.

Rami's holding a brick over his head for no reason. Testing weight. Testing something.

"Yeah."

"You sure? You've been standing there for—"

"Hand me the level."

They're building a wall. Load-bearing, nothing complicated. But Rami keeps thinking about weight. A brick is 2.5 kilos. Drop it from the right height, it'll kill you. They're stacking thousands. Tons of potential violence. Calling it housing.

His daughter used to build towers with blocks. Stack them high then knock them down laughing. One day she built a tower and left it standing. Said people lived inside, needed it to stay up. Three days later their actual house didn't stay up.

Ahmed hands him the level. "My sister's supposed to get one of these units."

"She will."

"When?"

"When we finish."

"Boss—"

"That's all I got, Ahmed."

At lunch Rami sits on a brick pile eating his mother's flatbread. The other guys eat together, talk about football, girls, normal shit. He sits separate. Not unfriendly. Just separate.

Takes out the gold brick piece. Turns it in his fingers. The Arabic is gone but he remembers. *Baba, don't forget me.* Like there's a version of him that kept working, kept breathing, and forgot his six-year-old painted a brick gold and gave it to him and died when the ceiling fell.

The brick in his hand, the bricks under him, the bricks in the wall—all recycled from someone's collapsed home. Same materials for construction and destruction. There's probably something there but he's too tired.

"Boss, you're crying," Ahmed says.

"I'm not."

"You are."

Rami wipes his face. Pockets the gold brick. "Break's over."

## II. Manchester

Sadie's daughter keeps asking about the brick mobile. That's what Ruby calls it—a mobile, like the things over baby cribs, except it's bricks hanging on ropes over the entrance to their building.

The building is a converted warehouse. The "art installation" is courtesy of someone's cousin who's "making a statement," according to the landlord who charged them double deposit because Sadie's a single mum.

The installation is bricks on ropes. Just that. Hanging at head height like a threat. Sadie ducks under them every time. Ruby spins them so they click.

"Don't," Sadie says.

"Why?"

"They're not toys."

"What are they?"

Good question. Art? Menace? Both? The landlord said "provocative." What it provokes is Sadie worrying about rope strength.

She's thirty-nine. Three jobs—Tesco mornings, cleaning afternoons, watching other people's kids evenings. The warehouse is all she could afford after the estate got sold to developers. "Affordable housing," they called it. Affordable if you have two incomes and no kids.

Ruby is seven. Smart. Asks too many questions. Obsessed with building—Lego, blocks, cardboard, whatever. Sadie saved for months for a good Lego set. Ruby built it once, took it apart, built something else. "This one's better. It has a garden."

The warehouse is freezing. Industrial heating that barely works. Exposed brick walls Sadie tried to make cheerful with posters. Still feels like living inside someone's architecture thesis. The hanging bricks don't help. Every time she ducks under them: this is someone's art and my constant reminder something could fall on my head.

Ruby's teacher wanted to talk last week. "Ruby's been drawing disturbing images." Showed Sadie papers full of falling buildings, running people, bricks everywhere.

"That's not disturbing. That's Mosul," Sadie said.

"I'm sorry?"

"We watched a documentary. About rebuilding. She's been interested."

"At seven?"

"She asks questions. I answer them honestly."

Now Ruby wants to be an engineer. Build houses that don't fall. Sadie doesn't tell her most houses fall because someone decided they should, not because they were built wrong.

Tuesday, coming home from cleaning, Sadie actually stops under the bricks. Really looks. There's text on some—spray paint, mostly illegible. She steps closer. One says: *For the ones who didn't make it.* Another: *Housing is a right not a luxury.*

She never noticed. Too busy ducking, too busy rushing Ruby past.

Ruby comes up. "Mum, what's that one say?"

Sadie reads: *These could fall. So could you. So could your home.*

"I don't like that," Ruby says.

"Me neither."

Inside, Ruby builds a tower while Sadie makes dinner—beans on toast again—and thinks about how they're one rent increase from gone. The bricks outside are honest at least. Everything else pretends it's stable.

Ruby's tower falls. She laughs. Rebuilds it.

## III. What's Underneath

Rami finds a skull. Construction guys find them sometimes—Mosul has more dead than graves. Protocol is call it in, stop work, wait. But waiting means falling behind schedule and Ahmed is looking at him like: what now, boss?

Rami looks at the skull. Human. Adult. Could be anyone.

"Cover it. We'll call after lunch."

Ahmed nods, relieved. They tarp it over. Keep working.

At lunch Rami sits with the skull. Shouldn't but does. Takes out his gold brick piece. Sets it next to the tarp. Two memorials—one he carried, one he found.

His daughter's Arabic, worn off now. Prayers. Every skull was someone's child. This skull has no gold, no memory, no one except a tired engineer who can't remember if he prayed this morning.

He has a marker in his bag. Gold—for marking measurements. He opens the tarp slightly. The skull is weathered, anonymous.

Writes on it: *You were someone's child.*

Arabic, then English, then Aramaic because Mosul is old.

Ahmed comes over. "Boss, what are you doing?"

"Praying, I guess."

"That's not praying."

"Then what?"

Ahmed thinks. "Remembering."

"Yeah."

They sit there, two men next to a skull on a construction site, and Ahmed says: "My uncle says every brick here has blood in it. All the wars. Says we're building with death."

"He's right."

"But we're building."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

Rami looks at the half-finished housing. Bricks stacked into walls that will hold or won't, house people or won't, stand or fall depending on the next war, the next government, the next decision by people who've never held a brick.

"Because your sister needs somewhere to live. And if we don't build with death, we don't build. Everything here is death. Ground, bricks, air. So we build or we don't. Those are the options."

Ahmed nods. Still looks unsure.

They call it in. Authorities come. Bag the skull. No ceremony, just tag it and add it to the count. Rami keeps the gold marker in his pocket with the brick. Can't seem to let either go.

## IV. Empty Ropes

The bricks disappear one night. Sadie comes home, they're gone. Just ropes left, cut, dangling empty.

Ruby is devastated. "Someone stole them!"

"Good."

"But they were art!"

"They were bricks on ropes, love. Someone needed them probably."

The landlord is furious. Calls it vandalism. Threatens rent increase. Sadie nods, pays, goes inside.

She doesn't miss them. The ropes are still there, still swaying, still reminding her of suspension. At least without the bricks she doesn't have to duck.

Ruby asks: "Why would someone steal bricks?"

"Maybe they needed them."

"For what?"

"Building."

"What kind of building?"

"I don't know. Maybe a house."

Ruby considers this while building. "That's a good reason."

"Don't steal, Ruby."

"But if someone needs a house—"

"Don't steal."

Later, Ruby asleep, Sadie sits at the kitchen table with the eviction notice. Sixty days. After that, who knows. She's already called around. Council housing has a two-year wait. Private rentals want references she doesn't have, deposits she can't afford.

She thinks about the documentary. Mosul. People rebuilding on top of graves. At least there they're building. Here they're just tearing down and calling it regeneration.

## V. Finished

Rami finishes. Three years. Twenty-four units. Ahmed's sister gets one. Twenty-three other families get the rest. It's not enough—Mosul needs thousands. But it's twenty-four more than yesterday.

The day they hand keys, Ahmed finds Rami on a brick pile, holding his gold piece.

"Boss, we did it."

"Yeah."

"My sister's having a baby. Girl. Due in August."

"That's good."

They sit there. The housing project behind them, finished, standing. For now.

"You keeping that brick?" Ahmed asks.

Rami looks at it. At the gold mostly worn off. At the last thing his daughter touched that still exists.

"Yeah."

"Good."

They sit there a while longer. Then they get up and go home.

In Manchester, Sadie and Ruby pack boxes. Ruby's tower is half-built on the floor, abandoned when she realized it would just get knocked down anyway when they move.

"Where are we going?" Ruby asks for the fifth time.

"I'm working on it."

"But where?"

"I don't know yet, love."

Ruby's quiet for a minute. Then: "Will the new place have art outside?"

"I don't know. Probably not bricks on ropes."

"Good. I didn't like those."

Sadie looks at her daughter. Seven years old, about to be homeless, still building things even though they keep falling down.

"Me neither," Sadie says.

They pack in silence. Ruby starts rebuilding her tower, stops, starts again. Outside the empty ropes sway in the wind.

Rami goes home to his mother's house. Puts the gold brick on his bedside table where he can see it. Thinks about Ahmed's sister, about the baby coming in August, about twenty-four families who have keys now to places that might stand or might not.

Doesn't pray. Can't remember how anymore. Just sits there with the brick, with the gold marker in his pocket, with the weight of everything that's fallen and everything that hasn't yet.

Sadie finds a place. Not great—one bedroom, they'll have to share, forty minutes from Ruby's school. But it's something. The landlord wants first, last, and deposit. She doesn't have it.

Her sister in Birmingham offers to help. Sadie says no, then yes, then cries on the phone for ten minutes while Ruby builds a tower in the background.

They move on day fifty-eight. Ruby carries her Lego in a box. Sadie carries everything else. The new place has no art outside. Just a door. Just walls. Just what hasn't fallen yet.
