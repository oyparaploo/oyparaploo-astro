Pressure Ghosts (fifth hour)

Words / 2025 Variety

Dream Catalog of Those Who Paint the Wind with Transparent Hues

Yuki couldn't sleep, though she wasn't entirely sure why the insomnia felt different tonight. At 3:17 AM, lying in the narrow bed of a room she shared with three other people in the condemned building near Desta's intersection, she felt a peculiar disorientation, as if the boundary between waking and sleeping had become as transparent as the hues she'd been trying to paint all week. Her mind kept returning to a question she couldn't quite formulate: What was she actually doing here? And more troublingly, did any of it matter?

She found herself thinking about witnesses, about other artists who'd dedicated their practice to making visible what hurricanes leave behind, but as the names and images began to surface, she realized she wasn't sure if she was thinking about them or thinking about herself through them. The gallery she was walking through existed only in her half-sleeping consciousness, but the confusion she felt was absolutely real. Each artist she encountered seemed to be asking her something, confronting her with aspects of her own practice and presence that she'd been avoiding.

Daniel Lind-Ramos appeared first, working between 2018 and 2022, and his María Guabancex from 2018 made Yuki uncomfortable in ways she couldn't immediately name. Guabancex—the Taíno goddess of hurricanes, of wind and chaos and the kind of destruction that reshapes entire landscapes. Yuki's synesthetic perception translated this into swirling reds and golds that somehow remained translucent, but as she looked closer, she realized she was seeing her own face reflected in the transparent hues, distorted and uncertain.

The image unsettled her because it forced her to ask: Was she appropriating someone else's catastrophe? She wasn't from Puerto Rico. She hadn't lived through María. Her visual-auditory perception let her map pressure ghosts at the intersection, but did that give her the right to claim kinship with artists who'd survived the actual storms? The dream didn't answer, but the question lingered with a weight that made her chest feel tight even in sleep.

The Goon Ox wobbled into her dream then, and Yuki felt a rush of affection followed immediately by confusion about what that affection meant. The creature's four uneven legs, its refusal of symmetry, and especially that bright orange tuft of hair sprouting absurdly from its head—all of it made her want to reach out, to touch it, to somehow communicate that she understood what it meant to perceive the world differently than others did. But the Goon Ox looked at her with eyes that seemed to contain centuries of patience, and Yuki suddenly wasn't sure if she understood anything at all.

Did she actually hear pressure ghosts, or was she just pretending because she wanted to belong to this strange community of expanded perceivers? Her synesthesia was real—she'd lived with it her entire life, seeing sound and hearing light whether she wanted to or not. But the pressure ghosts specifically, the sonic imprints of violence and displacement—could she really perceive them, or had she convinced herself she could because the Goon Ox and the elder gardener made such perception seem noble, necessary, heroic even?

The bright orange hair on the Ox's head seemed to glow with a question: What are you hiding from yourself?

Then Firelei Báez drifted into focus, her 2019 work emerging like images developing in chemical baths, and Yuki felt herself drawn into paintings that seemed to know her secrets. Báez was mapping how hurricanes don't just destroy buildings but erase histories, compound violences, and as Yuki stared at the work, she realized she was thinking about her own family history—about grandparents who'd been interned during World War II, about a grandfather who never spoke about it, about how that silence had shaped her mother who shaped her.

The transparent hues Báez worked in revealed something Yuki had been avoiding: she was attracted to stories of displacement and occupation because they echoed her own inherited trauma, but she hadn't actually dealt with that trauma. She'd just transferred it onto others, made it about Karim's checkpoints and Desta's voicelessness instead of confronting the unresolved grief in her own lineage. The dream made her see this clearly, uncomfortably, and she felt shame burning in her sleeping body.

Was her entire practice just elaborate avoidance? Was she documenting other people's pressure ghosts because listening to her own family's was too painful?

Teresita Fernández appeared next, working through 2020 and 2021, those pandemic years that Yuki had spent largely alone, and the dream forced her to remember what she'd hidden from herself about that time. Fernández was creating work about ground that could no longer be trusted, about earth that revealed itself to be temporary and hostile. Yuki saw in this a reflection of how she'd felt during lockdown—how every surface seemed contaminated, how space itself became threatening, how the solitude she'd always secretly craved turned out to be unbearable when it was mandatory.

She'd told people she was fine during that time, that the isolation gave her space to develop her visual-auditory mapping practice. But the truth, which the dream now confronted her with in transparent hues that made it impossible to hide, was that she'd been desperately lonely and terrified that the loneliness revealed something broken in her, some inability to connect that had always been there but was now undeniable.

That's why she'd attached herself so quickly to the community at Desta's intersection after restrictions lifted. Not because she'd organically found her people, but because she was frantically trying to prove she could belong somewhere, could matter to someone. The elder gardener's seventeen unnamed senses, the Goon Ox's pressure ghost perception, Desta's wordless activism—she'd adopted all of it almost desperately, hadn't she? Trying to become someone who belonged rather than figuring out who she actually was.

Her sleeping mind resisted this revelation, tried to turn away from it, but Nathalie Miebach appeared then with her work from 2018, 2019, and 2025, and the transparent hues she wove from meteorological data created a structure that Yuki couldn't escape. Miebach was teaching that hurricanes are systems of energy that we can learn to read if we develop new vocabularies, and Yuki suddenly understood that she'd been trying to read the wrong system.

She'd been so focused on mapping external pressure ghosts—the sonic imprints of institutional violence, of displacement, of suffering—that she'd completely ignored her own internal weather patterns. The storm systems of her unprocessed family trauma. The rising pressure of her need to belong. The destructive winds of her self-doubt disguised as artistic mission.

The dream showed her this with painful clarity: all her maps of the intersection were actually maps of herself. Every pressure ghost she claimed to perceive was really just an echo of her own unresolved emotions projected outward onto a community she wanted to serve but wasn't sure how to genuinely join.

Anastasia Samoylova entered the dream from 2016 extending to the present, and her documentation of Florida's relationship with paradise and apocalypse made Yuki think about her own relationship with beauty and disaster. She'd always been drawn to documenting suffering—had made it her artistic focus—but why? The transparent hues of Samoylova's work revealed something uncomfortable: there was something almost voyeuristic about Yuki's practice, something that bordered on using other people's pain as raw material for her own artistic identity.

She didn't want to see this about herself, but the dream was insistent now, building toward some kind of confrontation she couldn't avoid. Gideon Mendel appeared, working from 2007 to the present, bearing witness to rising water for nearly two decades, and Yuki felt the weight of his sustained commitment. That's what she was uncertain about, wasn't it? Could she sustain this? Was she truly committed to this work, or was she just passing through, sampling the aesthetics of resistance without actually being willing to dedicate her life to it the way Mendel had, the way Desta had, the way the Goon Ox with its bright orange hair persistently, patiently did?

The dream pressed the question: What do you actually want, Yuki?

Richard Misrach from 2005 surfaced briefly, and Yuki realized she'd been thinking about him as ancient history when really it was only twenty years ago. Time was playing tricks in this dream, collapsing and expanding, and she suddenly understood that she was afraid of time itself—afraid that if she committed fully to this work, decades would pass and she'd look back and realize she'd been documenting the wrong things, asking the wrong questions, belonging to a community that was never really hers.

What if the Goon Ox's orange hair wasn't cheerful defiance but rather a warning she'd been misreading? What if the elder gardener's seventeen unnamed senses weren't an invitation but a reminder that some forms of perception can't be learned, only inherited or earned through experiences she'd never had?

Josh Kline entered her rumination from 2018 through 2024, and his work on how climate disaster amplifies economic inequality made Yuki suddenly, painfully aware of her own class position. She lived in a condemned building, yes, but by choice. She had family money she could access if things got truly desperate. She'd chosen precarity as an artistic stance, but it wasn't the same as Desta's actual homelessness or Karim's actual occupation or the actual economic violence that made people shelterless.

The transparent hues Kline worked in revealed the truth she'd been hiding: she was performing solidarity rather than living it. She could leave this intersection, this community, this practice whenever she wanted. The others couldn't. And that difference changed everything about what her presence here meant.

The shame was overwhelming now, making her want to wake up, but Allison Janae Hamilton appeared pulling in 1926 and 1928, and the chronological confusion in the dream suddenly made sense. Yuki was lost in time because she didn't know where she belonged in the lineage of witness. Hamilton was painting pressure ghosts that accumulated across generations, connecting historical hurricanes to contemporary climate catastrophe, and Yuki didn't know what her own lineage was.

Japanese American internment was part of it, but she'd never properly learned that history, never let herself feel it. Her synesthesia connected her to something, but to what? Was it enough to build a practice on? Was perceiving the world differently than others the same as perceiving it more truly? Or was she just different in a way that didn't actually help anyone, including herself?

The dream offered no easy answers, but it did offer Judy Pfaff, working in 2023 and 2024, creating installations you have to walk through. And suddenly Yuki understood what the dream was trying to show her: she'd been standing outside her own life, observing it the way she observed pressure ghosts, trying to map it rather than actually living inside it.

She needed to walk through her own installation. She needed to stop hovering at the edges of this community and actually enter it, not as a documentarian but as a participant whose confusion and uncertainty and unresolved emotions were themselves part of the pressure ghosts being generated at this intersection.

Then all the other names surfaced—Sofía Gallisá Muriente, Gabriela Salazar, Lulu Varona, Candida Alvarez, Sofía Córdova, Edra Soto, Yiyo Tirado Rivera, Armig Santos, Frances Gallardo, Miguel Luciano, then Firelei Báez again, Teresita Fernández returning, Tamika Galanis, Deborah Jack, Hew Locke, Lionel Cruet, Tina Freeman—and Yuki realized they weren't a catalog. They were a chorus. And they were asking her the question she'd been avoiding: What are you willing to sacrifice to do this work authentically?

Not your comfort—you've already given that up, or pretended to. Not your time—you have plenty of that. But your certainty. Your sense of yourself as someone who knows what they're doing. Your protective distance from the suffering you claim to document. Your belief that perception makes you special rather than accountable.

The Goon Ox appeared again, wobbling toward her on uneven legs, that bright orange hair somehow both absurd and profound, and in the dream, Yuki finally understood what it was teaching. The orange hair wasn't camouflage or decoration or even defiance. It was honesty. It was the creature saying: I am strange, I am unstable, I am uncertain about almost everything, and I'm not hiding any of that from you.

Could Yuki be that honest? Could she admit that she didn't know if she really perceived pressure ghosts or just wanted to? Could she confess that she was using this community to avoid her own unresolved family trauma? Could she acknowledge that her artistic practice might be elaborate avoidance dressed up as activism?

The dream showed her Desta then, standing at the intersection in her smoke-stained Barney costume, and Yuki saw something she'd missed before. Desta wasn't certain either. Her humming wasn't confident proclamation but tentative offering. She stood there every day not knowing if it mattered, not knowing if anyone was really listening, doing it anyway because what else was there to do? The purple dinosaur costume wasn't armor—it was vulnerability made visible, childhood innocence weaponized against institutional violence through sheer, absurd persistence.

And Karim, watching razor wire melt, wasn't sure if liberation could arrive through destruction. He was asking the same questions Yuki was: Does witnessing change anything? Does documentation matter if the systems keep operating? Is perception enough, or do we need something more, something we don't yet know how to name?

Even the elder gardener, with seventeen unnamed senses, was uncertain. Those senses didn't provide answers—they just multiplied questions, revealed more complexity, made certainty impossible because reality was simply too vast and interconnected to ever fully comprehend.

The transparent hues all these artists worked in weren't just an aesthetic choice, Yuki realized. They were epistemological honesty. They were admission that we can make the invisible visible but we can't make the uncertain certain. We can paint the wind but the wind keeps changing. We can catalog witnesses but we can't guarantee our witness matters. We can develop new senses but each new sense reveals how much more there is that we still can't perceive.

The dream brought her face to face with her hidden desire: she wanted to be special. She wanted her synesthesia, her visual-auditory perception, to make her important, valuable, necessary to this community. She wanted to be the one who could see and hear what others couldn't, who could map the invisible and make the incomprehensible comprehensible. She wanted to be needed.

But the truth, which the transparent hues finally revealed in all its uncomfortable clarity, was that nobody was that special. Everyone at the intersection was trying to perceive in expanded ways. Everyone was uncertain. Everyone was working with unresolved emotions and hidden desires and confusion about whether any of it mattered. The Goon Ox with its orange hair was no more certain than she was—it just wobbled forward anyway, perceiving what it could perceive, offering what it could offer, being what it could be.

That's what Yuki needed to learn. Not how to be certain, but how to proceed without certainty. Not how to resolve her confusion, but how to work within it honestly. Not how to hide her unresolved emotions but how to let them inform her practice rather than distort it.

She needed to stop trying to map other people's pressure ghosts as a way of avoiding her own. She needed to start with her own family history, her own inherited trauma, her own uncertain place in lineages of displacement and witness. She needed to admit to Desta and Karim and the elder gardener and especially the Goon Ox that she wasn't sure she belonged here, wasn't sure she was perceiving accurately, wasn't sure her work mattered.

And then, after that admission, after that vulnerability, maybe she could actually begin to build authentic relationships rather than performing solidarity. Maybe she could contribute something real rather than just documenting others' realness. Maybe she could learn to wobble forward on uneven ground with bright orange hair announcing her uncertainty, her strangeness, her ongoing confusion about almost everything.

The dream showed her one more thing before it released her: all these artists painting the wind with transparent hues weren't certain either. They were making work in the face of catastrophe without knowing if art could actually change anything. They were developing new perceptual vocabularies without knowing if anyone would learn to speak them. They were bearing witness without guarantees that anyone was listening.

But they were doing it anyway. And that "anyway" was everything. That was the resolution Yuki had been seeking—not certainty about whether her work mattered, but commitment to doing it even in the face of permanent uncertainty. Not confidence about her place in this community, but willingness to keep showing up vulnerably. Not clear answers about whether she really perceived pressure ghosts, but honesty about the fact that she might not, paired with dedication to trying anyway.

When Yuki woke at dawn, her face was wet with tears she'd cried in her sleep. The confusion hadn't disappeared, but it had clarified into something she could work with. She understood now that her uncertainty wasn't a problem to solve but a condition to acknowledge and proceed from. Her unresolved family trauma wasn't separate from her practice—it was the foundation she'd been trying to build on without admitting it was there.

She got up, grabbed her materials, and went to Desta's intersection, but this time with different intentions. She would tell Desta the truth: that she wasn't sure she belonged here, that she might be using this community to avoid her own pain, that her synesthesia might not actually let her perceive pressure ghosts the way the Goon Ox did. She would ask Karim about his experience not as material to document but as wisdom she needed to receive. She would sit with the elder gardener and admit she only had five senses, not seventeen, and didn't know if she could develop more or if she should stop pretending.

And she would approach the Goon Ox, with its bright orange hair catching the morning light like a flag of honest uncertainty, and she would say: I see you. I see that you wobble. I see that you're strange and unstable and uncertain about most things. And I'm like that too. Can I wobble alongside you? Can we be uncertain together?

Because that's what the dream had taught her: painting the wind with transparent hues isn't about making the invisible fully visible. It's about acknowledging that some things will always remain partially unseen, that perception is always incomplete, that we're all working with limited senses trying to apprehend infinite complexity. The transparent hues are honest admission that we can point toward truth without fully capturing it, can gesture toward pressure ghosts without fully hearing them, can try to belong without ever being certain we actually do.

The catalog of artists she'd dreamed wasn't really about them. It was about her, using their work as mirrors to see her own confusion and uncertainty reflected back. And now, having confronted those feelings through the dream, she could begin the real work: not documenting others' pressure ghosts from a safe distance, but acknowledging her own, investigating her own unresolved lineages, admitting her own desires to matter and belong.

She could paint the wind now, not with confident brushstrokes but with transparent hues that admitted uncertainty while insisting on the attempt anyway. She could be like the Goon Ox—strange, unstable, wobbling forward with bright orange hair that refused to hide anything, that announced: I'm here, I'm confused, I'm uncertain, and I'm still trying to perceive what needs perceiving.

That's what resolution looked like. Not answers, but better questions. Not certainty, but honest acknowledgment of confusion. Not belonging achieved, but vulnerability offered. Not pressure ghosts fully mapped, but the willingness to sit with partial perception and proceed anyway, to wobble forward on uneven ground, to paint what can barely be seen in colors that remain partially transparent, to bear witness even when uncertain whether witnessing matters.

The dream had done its work. Yuki could begin hers.
