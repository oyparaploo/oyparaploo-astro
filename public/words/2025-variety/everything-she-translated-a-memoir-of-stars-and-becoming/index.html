Everything She Translated

Words / 2025 Variety

A Memoir of Stars and Becoming

---

Froansios kept a starling. Not a finch or canary like normal people—a starling with oil-slick feathers that knew seventeen languages, none of them human. The bird's name was a whistle she could produce but I could never imitate, something between wind through bones and water over stones. It would perch on her shoulder while she worked in the workshop, whispering what sounded like secrets from before words existed, messages from the time when meaning lived in the spaces between sounds. She'd nod along, completely serious, her fingers never pausing in their work, occasionally responding in this clicking language she claimed the bird had taught her during the long winters when they were snowed in together, just the two of them and the gradually diminishing woodpile.

"He remembers being a dinosaur," she told me once, the bird preening her hair with disturbing gentleness, each strand arranged with the precision of someone preparing holy relics. "All birds do. That's why they look at us like we're temporary—because to them, we are. We're just the latest experiment in consciousness, and not even the most interesting one."

Of all the things I've seen—Northern Lights over Michigan that looked like the sky was bleeding green music, my daughter's birth when time folded in on itself and I understood infinity, that summer the lake turned pink with algae and everyone thought the world was ending but it was just beginning differently—Froansios remains the most inexplicable. She was, I realized too late, multiple creatures wearing one woman's name, each creature having its own relationship with gravity, with time, with the burden of being seen.

---

The Swan House

Her apartment above the Polish bakery was tiny, maybe 500 square feet, but she moved through it like she had acres, like space bent around her to accommodate her presence. The walls seemed to exhale when she entered, making room. Like a swan on water, if swans made furniture and talked to birds and cried while cutting onions not because of the onions but because sometimes beauty arrives disguised as dinner preparation. Every movement deliberate but somehow liquid, as if her bones were only suggestions her muscles considered but didn't always follow. Even reaching for coffee cups looked choreographed by someone who understood grace as engineering, who knew that the arc between wanting and having contained the entire universe.

The bakery below would start at 3 AM, and the yeast-sweet smell would rise through her floorboards like ancestral memory, like the earth remembering what it was before humans decided to organize it into property lines. She'd wake with the smell, say it was like being born into bread, which made no sense until you'd spent a morning there, surrounded by that ancient alchemy of flour and time.

"Privacy," she said, arranging dried herbs she'd grown on the fire escape—rosemary for remembrance, sage for forgetting, thyme for the space between—"is just practice for being public. I'm rehearsing my real self so I don't forget the lines out there, in the world that wants me to be smaller, quieter, less pterodactyl."

We'd talk for hours in that kitchen, conversation flowing like silk being pulled through fingers—smooth until it caught on something true that needed careful working through, those snags where honesty had left its sharp edges. She'd knead bread while discussing Spinoza, her hands finding rhythm that matched her thoughts, each fold of dough a proposition, each rise a proof of something invisible but undeniable.

"Descartes was an idiot," she'd say, folding sourdough with the confidence of someone who'd argued with dead philosophers and won. "I think therefore I am? Backwards. I transform, therefore I continue. Thinking is just one transformation among many—not even the most important one. My cells replace themselves without consulting my consciousness. My dreams reorganize my memories without permission. The real work happens in the dark, while the mind is looking elsewhere."

The herbs on her windowsill turned toward her when she moved, which I tried not to think about too hard, filed it in the growing category of "Things About Froansios That Suggested Physics Had Exceptions."

---

The Tiger Politics

Outside was different. The swan became a tiger, and I mean that less metaphorically than you'd think. Her posture would literally change in the doorway—spine elongating, shoulders rolling back, something prehistoric entering her gait.

She'd stride into town meetings like she was hunting, shoulders rolling with this predatory grace that made council members lean back involuntarily, their bodies recognizing a predator before their minds caught up. Her voice could cut glass when she wanted it to, could make grown men remember being scolded by their mothers, could make the air itself pay attention. "You've caged this whole town in your small thinking," she said once, and I swear the lights dimmed, as if electricity itself was embarrassed to illuminate such cowardice. "But cages built from fear are weak. Watch me walk through the bars."

She was wildly, ferociously independent. Tied herself to no party, no ideology except the radical notion that people deserved to exist fully, in all their messy, unproductive, non-economic glory. When developers tried to demolish the low-income housing, she literally chained herself to a bulldozer, but not before she'd painted it bright pink in the night, written "THIS MACHINE EATS DREAMS" on its side. When they arrested her, she gave legal advice to everyone in the holding cell, started what she called "Jail University," teaching constitutional law from memory. The charges got dropped after she somehow convinced the prosecutor's mother to join her protest, something involving tea and a conversation about what kind of world we leave for grandchildren we'll never meet.

Ga Holly See Oh—this performance artist who lived in a shipping container she'd painted to look like the inside of a heart, complete with arterial hallways and ventricular bedroom—did a whole series of paintings of Froansios's tiger years. Used these impossible pigments she made from crushing expired medications and mixing them with honey, purple that looked like bruised hope, gold that hurt to look at directly but you couldn't stop looking. "She's every woman's rage walking around in a sundress," Ga Holly said, adding another layer of translucent fury to the canvas. "Watch how she never apologizes for her stripes. They're earned. They're beautiful. They're warnings."

The Salamander Summer

I found her on Highway 49 one August evening, the asphalt still breathing out the day's heat in visible waves. She'd spent three days protesting the pipeline, hadn't eaten anything but rainwater and whatever fell from trees. She was laying on the warm asphalt, completely dazed, possibly hallucinating, definitely transforming. Not quite human anymore—her skin had gone translucent and damp, like rice paper left in the rain, and she moved like something amphibious, belly close to the ground, testing each surface before trusting it with her weight.

"I'm salamander today," she explained matter-of-factly, as if this clarified everything. "Sometimes you have to get low to remember what rising means. The earth has different stories down here, older ones, from before we decided to stand upright and ruin everything with ambition."

That was the summer she met Yan Segal. He'd lost everything in the floods—his partner Marcus drowned trying to save their photo albums, those irreplaceable images of a life that now existed only in Yan's increasingly unreliable memory. Their house became driftwood scattered across three counties, pieces of their kitchen in Kentucky, their bedroom in Ohio, their life dissolved into geography. Yan wandered the edges of town like a ghost who'd forgotten he was supposed to be dead, or maybe remembered he was supposed to be alive but couldn't figure out how. His clothes had given up remembering what shape they were supposed to hold, hung on him like suggestions of fabric.

Froansios recognized something in his devastation—the particular emptiness of someone who'd been hollowed out but was still expected to walk around, pretending the shell was enough. They'd sit together in this unglamorous silence, two people who'd been reduced to their most basic components, like elements on the periodic table before anyone figured out how to combine them into something useful. She was toad-like then, absorbing moisture from the air, her skin processing humidity like external lungs, needing help she'd never admitted to needing before because need felt like failure and failure felt like death but now death didn't seem like the worst thing, just the most permanent.

Then Anya arrived—the refugee who moved like winter, all quiet encompassing cold that made you appreciate warmth when it came. She had these bottles of water she'd carried across three continents, said they were blessed by a saint whose name couldn't be pronounced by living tongues, only by the dying who suddenly understood all languages in their last moment. Rose blossoms pressed between pages of a book in a language her children would never learn, because assimilation meant forgetting, and forgetting meant surviving, and surviving meant betraying everything you'd been.

"Recovery needs witnesses," Anya said in her musical accent, the one that made English sound like it was apologizing for its limitations, offering the water to Froansios and Yan. "In my country, we say the wounded heal the wounded best—they know where not to touch, which memories to avoid, when silence is medicine and when it's poison."

The Whale Years

When Froansios got sick—something in her bones that doctors couldn't name, so they just called it "idiopathic" which is medical for "we don't know but it's your body's fault"—she transformed again. Became vast and patient like those whales that dive so deep they have to slow their hearts to almost nothing, each beat separated by long minutes of faith that the next one would come.

She'd lie in bed for days, breathing with this oceanic rhythm that made you unconsciously sync your own breath to hers, until the whole room was breathing as one organism. I'd sing to her—old songs my grandmother hummed while shelling peas, melodies that tasted like summer before air conditioning, when heat was something you moved through slowly, respectfully. Froansios would listen with her whole body, like whales do, sound traveling through her bones, each note finding its home in a different vertebra.

"Did you know," she whispered once between waves of pain that left her gasping like she'd been underwater, "whale hearts are the size of small cars? All that space for blood. All that weight for love. Maybe I'm growing a bigger heart. Maybe it just feels like dying because dying is the ultimate expansion—becoming everything by being nothing."

Kai would visit—this kid meteorologist with hair that defied physics, each strand reaching in a different direction like it was receiving separate weather reports. Barely out of college but already speaking weather like poetry, treating low pressure systems like they were moody friends who needed understanding. He'd set up his equipment in her room, tracking pressure systems in her breathing, making charts of her pain like it was a storm system, which maybe it was.

"Tomorrow's forecast," he'd announce cheerfully, pointing to his homemade graphs that used colors instead of numbers, emotions instead of measurements, "impossible sunshine after impossible rain. Look, the barometric pressure of hope is rising right here, see where the purple turns gold? That's Tuesday. Tuesday you'll feel human again. Wednesday, maybe more than human. Thursday is uncertain, but beautifully uncertain, like a door you're not sure you should open but will anyway."

The Star-Milking Years

Between the sickness and the end, Froansios told stories about being young, when she was "like a cosmic cow" standing in fields that existed mostly in dreams, that place where Michigan meets memory meets mythology.

"All night long, I milked the stars," she'd say, making pulling motions with her hands, fingers remembering the rhythm. "Different stars gave different milk. Polaris gave direction—thick like cream, good for when you're lost, but too much and you can't change course. Venus gave love—thin, sweet, goes bad if you don't use it quick, curdles into bitterness if you try to save it. The Pleiades gave community—had to milk all seven at once or it would curdle into loneliness, and lonely milk makes you sick in ways doctors can't diagnose."

She'd describe storing this star-milk in mason jars in her parents' basement, saving wonder for the lean years ahead like her grandmother had saved bacon grease and string, because you never knew what kind of hunger was coming. The jars glowed blue in the dark, she swore, though she could never prove it because looking at them directly made them go dark, like quantum physics or love or God—things that changed when observed.

Anya understood this completely, would nod along like Froansios was reading from a shared scripture. She'd bring her own nectar—honey mixed with cardamom and the taste of elsewhere, that flavor that's not quite anywhere but definitely not here, something that made you dream while awake, made you remember things that hadn't happened yet. "We all eat light differently," she'd say, pouring tea that steamed in patterns like birds going home or letters in an alphabet that hadn't been invented. "Some people swallow darkness, digest it into smaller darkness. The lucky ones learn to digest both, to make nutrition from whatever the universe is serving."

---

The Owl Transformation

Froansios's last decade, she went nocturnal and wise, a transformation so gradual we only noticed when she stopped existing during daylight altogether. Grew these enormous eyes that seemed to see through time, past the present into what was coming and what had passed, everything happening simultaneously in her dilated pupils. She'd perch on her porch at the edge of town where streetlights gave up and stars took over, that liminal space where civilization admitted defeat, swaying slightly like trees do when they're listening to their roots, to the underground rivers that connect everything to everything else.

She developed this way of talking where every sentence contained three meanings—past, present, and future braided together like DNA if DNA could speak. "Women must remain partially unfaithful to their children," she'd tell young mothers who came seeking advice, drawn by some invisible magnetism that pulled the lost toward her porch. "Not in love—never in love—but in identity. Keep something back. A room in your heart where your children have never lived, where you can be the person you were before their need rewrote your name. Otherwise, when they leave, you're just an empty house haunted by the ghosts of their needs, and ghost needs are hungrier than living ones."

She knew this from experience. Three children, each one taking pieces of her she had to learn to regenerate, like a starfish if starfish could feel the phantom pain of missing arms. "If you become only mother," she'd say, watching bats hunt moths around the porch light, that ancient drama of hunger and escape played out nightly, "what happens when the role ends? You're left holding a costume with no play to perform it in, no stage, no audience, just you in an empty theater wondering who you were before the lights came up."

The Cat Revelation

When Froansios died—a Tuesday that felt like Sunday, rain falling upward in the wind, gravity itself confused by grief—she died in my arms. That's when I understood: she'd been a cat all along. Nine lives or ninety or nine hundred, each one complete, each one a different answer to the same question that nobody remembered asking but everyone knew was important.

Her last breath was this purr that traveled through my bones, depositing memories in my marrow where they'd live as long as I did, longer maybe, memories being the only thing that survives the body. I suddenly saw all her forms at once—swan, tiger, salamander, whale, star-milker, owl, cat—all true simultaneously, like those pictures that are two things depending on how you look, but this was everything depending on how you existed.

Ga Holly See Oh painted this final portrait using pigments mixed with ash from love letters Froansios had saved—not her own, but strangers' letters she'd found in estate sales, other people's love becoming the medium for her memory. The painting shows a woman containing every creature she'd ever been, all existing in the same body without conflict, like an orchestra where every instrument plays a different song but somehow it's all one music. It hangs in the library now, in the section between Biology and Mythology, because the librarian couldn't decide which was more accurate. Children stare at it and immediately understand something adults have to learn through loss.

---

What Continues

Sometimes I wonder if my daughter has Froansios's eyes—not the color but the capacity, that ability to see the swan in the accountant, the tiger in the librarian, the whale in the boy who stands too still at recess like he's listening to something the other children can't hear.

The workshop still smells like her—sawdust and star-milk and that oil she used on the bird's feathers, something she made from rendering memories until they became liquid. Her kids run it now, each teaching their own version of transformation. The eldest makes furniture that doubles as protest signs, chairs you can sit in or carry to city hall. The middle one builds houses for hermit crabs and humans with equal care, says shelter is shelter, size is just perspective. The youngest documents everything, believes memory is the only inheritance that matters, that DNA is just memory in physical form, that we're all just our ancestors' memories walking around in meat costumes.

Yan Segal recovered, slowly, like ice becoming water becoming steam, each phase necessary. He carries Froansios's salamander summer with him like medicine, takes small doses when the world feels too vertical. Anya opened a tea shop that's really a church that's really a hospital for people whose wounds don't show on X-rays, the kind of hurt that lives in the space between cells. Kai became a famous meteorologist who includes emotional weather in his forecasts, tells people to expect scattered anxiety with a chance of unexpected joy. Ga Holly See Oh keeps painting Froansios in new forms—Froansios as wind, as Wednesday, as the space between words, as the moment before sleep when you're neither awake nor dreaming but something else, something more.

And me? I'm still writing her down, still getting it wrong, still trying to capture water in my hands, which is to say I'm doing something impossible but necessary, like love or memory or breathing.

Go, go, go away, Froansios would whisper to each of her forms, not dismissing but blessing them, like a mother sending children into the world. Make room for what comes next.

The words became my practice, my religion, my morning coffee ritual. Every dawn, I tell yesterday's self to go away gently, make room for who I'm becoming. It's harder than it sounds—we get attached to our suffering, mistake familiarity for safety. It's easier than staying the same—staying the same is death by a thousand unchanged days.

We're all creatures, Froansios taught me, all transforming whether we admit it or not. The lucky ones know it. The brave ones show it. The wise ones celebrate it, even when it means being a toad on Tuesday and a star on Saturday, even when your children don't recognize you, even when you don't recognize yourself in the mirror but you recognize yourself in the sky, in the water, in the space between breathing in and breathing out.

That's the thing about carpenter's daughters—they know how to build selves like furniture: functional, beautiful, and when necessary, able to be taken apart and rebuilt into something completely different using the same essential materials, the same bones and dreams and stardust we're all made of, just arranged in a new pattern that makes everyone dizzy until they realize they've been looking at it wrong, that there is no wrong, that everything is just everything wearing different faces, trying on different names, dancing different dances to the same cosmic rhythm that started before the first star thought about shining and will continue after the last star forgets how to stop.
