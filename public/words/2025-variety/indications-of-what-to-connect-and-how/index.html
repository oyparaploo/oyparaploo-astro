Indications of What To Connect and How

Words / 2025 Variety

---

A group of former museum workers who were fired for "mishandling artifacts" start an underground school teaching people the difference between knots that bind, loops that return, and ellipses that promise what can't be said—discovering that connection itself is a political act.

---

"Connection and continuation," Vessel Works wrote on the warehouse wall with a leaking marker, then added three dots. Then three more. Then stopped. Started again somewhere else. The marker cost $1.29 at Dollar Tree. The warehouse was free—abandoned since 2008, owner lost to bankruptcy, city forgot to demolish.

They'd all been fired the same week. Vessel for letting visitors touch everything—the Grecian urn worth $2.3 million, the textile that dissolved at skin's pH. Chanson Rubble (before her archivist days) for creating labels like "This Cost Someone's Rent" under a million-dollar painting—literally true, the collector had evicted twelve families to afford it. Lint Chin for using a Rothko to clean up coffee—accident, but nobody believed her. Museum workers. $12-15 an hour. No union. At-will employment. At-will termination.

Now they taught from this warehouse by the docks. Broken windows. Pigeons inside. Perfect. Heating bill: none. Electric stolen from the street light. Water from rain through the roof.

"A knot is a... wait, let me show you," Vessel said to whoever showed up—three kids definitely skipping Jefferson High, two elderly women from the shut-down senior center on Prospect, one banker looking lost.

She grabbed rope—found in dumpster. Wire—stripped from abandoned cars. The banker's tie—Brooks Brothers, $95, worth more than her weekly groceries. A kid's shoelace—already broken, reknotted twice. Started tying everything to everything. Not explaining. Just tying.

"When we tie—" she started.

"We make things that can't—" Chanson interrupted.

"Come undone easy," Lint finished, yanking on the banker's tie now connected to a pipe that hadn't carried water since the factory closed.

The banker—Queue, lost his job at KeyBank, whistle-blew on mortgage fraud, blackballed from finance—tried pulling free. Couldn't. Laughed nervously. "This is assault—"

"This is connection," the elderly women said. Together. Same time. They'd walked in speaking in unison and nobody knew why and nobody asked because it was perfect. Later: widows, married to the same man, different decades, became friends at his funeral, started speaking as one voice after.

"Knots are when we commit," Vessel said, tying more things. The kids' backpacks to chair legs—chairs from the demolished public school. Chanson's belt to a doorknob—door led nowhere, wall behind it. "Two things become—"

"Inseparable," everyone said, feeling it. The banker thinking of his mortgage, underwater, knotted to a house worth less than owed.

"But look—" Lint pointed at the knots. "Lumps! Ugly bumps! You can see exactly where things got joined."

"They don't hide it," one kid said, getting it. Destiny. Thirteen. Mom worked three jobs. Still poor.

"They celebrate—" the elderly women started.

"The tangle!" everyone finished.

Second week. The warehouse messier. Someone had brought a couch—street find, bedbugs probably, but horizontal is horizontal. Someone else had brought groceries but forgot refrigeration didn't exist. Smell of rotting produce mixing with pigeon shit and industrial chemicals from the brownfield next door.

"Loops!" Vessel announced by making one with her whole body, walking in circles. Hip hurt—arthritis, no insurance, walked anyway.

"Watch—" She grabbed copper wire, stripped from the walls by scrappers, left behind as worthless. Threaded it through everyone's hands, around pillars tagged with "DEMOLITION 2019" (never happened), through broken windows, back to the start.

"Unlike knots that stay—" Chanson said, following the wire.

"Loops move," Lint demonstrated, running the circuit despite her knee—torn ACL, emergency room said $45,000 to fix, she said fuck it, limped instead.

They had everyone walking loops. But the warehouse was full of crap—had to climb over things, under things, around things. Queue tripped, fell into the rotten groceries—$47 worth of produce from Save-A-Lot, would have fed someone for two weeks.

"Ideas loop like this," he said from the floor, covered in what might have been yogurt. Expired. Like his finance license. "Returning but—"

"Different!" Destiny said. "Each time carrying new... new something."

"Understanding," the elderly women said, but this time one started the word and the other finished it. Their unison was evolving. Like grief. Like solidarity.

"This isn't repetition," Vessel said, on her fifth loop, stepping exactly where Queue had fallen but differently.

"It's spiral!" Chanson added, arms spreading to show expansion.

"Development?" Lint asked, not sure.

"Development," everyone confirmed, feeling dizzy from circling. From hunger. From possibility.

Third week. The warehouse had become something between school and disaster site. More people coming. Some living there now. Sleeping bags from Walmart—$19.99, cheapest warmth available. Next to art supplies—stolen, gifted, found. Next to garbage that might be art or might be garbage or might be both.

Vessel just pointed at the wall where she'd written "Connection and continuation..." Except now there were dozens of unfinished sentences everywhere. The walls looked like thoughts having seizures. Like the inside of her head since the firing.

"Ellipses," she said. Then stopped talking for twenty minutes.

Everyone sat in the silence. Uncomfortable. The banker checking his phone—screen cracked, Sprint cut off last month. The kids poking each other. The elderly women breathing in perfect unison—in together, out together, like one body with two heads, four lungs, one breath.

"These dots mean everything we can't—" Chanson finally said. Throat hurt. No insurance for the strep that was probably worse now.

"Say," Lint whispered.

"The labor that stays invisible..." Queue added, thinking of janitors at KeyBank, made $9 an hour to clean offices where millions moved through screens.

"The thoughts that just..." Destiny gestured vaguely.

"Trail off into..." the other kid continued. Marcus. Destiny's brother. Different fathers. Same struggle.

"..." the elderly women said. Actually said ellipses. Made the sound of three dots. The sound of everything unsaid about forty years of marriage to the same insufficient man.

Vessel brought out trash. Not clean museum-ready trash. Actual trash. Sticky. Smelly. A crushed water bottle with something growing in it—algae, bacteria, life finding a way.

"In a museum," she said, holding it like the Holy Grail, "this is artifact and symptom both."

"Worthy of preserving and—" Chanson gagged a little at the smell.

"Evidence of the shit system!" Lint finished.

"Museums can't critique without admitting they're part of—" Queue started.

"The loop!" everyone shouted, seeing it, the snake eating its tail, the institution feeding on what it claims to stand against. The museum's ecology initiative while running climate control for empty galleries. The diversity fellowship while firing Black workers. The community access programs at $25 admission.

They started what Vessel called "alchemical purification" but what looked like making a bigger mess.

"Physical cleaning is just... who cares about dirt," Lint said, literally pouring motor oil on her hands—her ex's, he'd left it when he left her for someone who didn't limp. Wiping it everywhere.

"But finding what's essential in the garbage..." Vessel held up a styrofoam container like it was speaking to her. From the Chinese place that closed. Family business forty years. Gentrified out.

"Not making it pretty—" Chanson added.

"Making it ITSELF!" the kids screamed, understanding in their bones what adults had to learn.

Everyone brought their own trash. Real trash. Things they'd thrown away and dug back out. Coffee grounds—Starbucks, when they could afford it. Broken phones—planned obsolescence made physical. One elderly woman brought her husband's ashes—"Ultimate garbage," she said, and her unison partner nodded. Cost $3,000 to cremate. Cost nothing to keep. Cost everything to carry.

"We're doing archaeology on right now," Vessel announced, documenting everything with Chanson's phone—screen shattered, only half worked, perfect metaphor.

"Urgent archaeology!" Chanson added, fake-filming the chaos.

"Taking care of civilization while it makes more—" Lint gestured at the growing pile.

"WASTE!" everyone finished. The warehouse echoed. Pigeons scattered. Returned. Loops.

The warehouse was becoming impossible. Forty people now. Some days sixty. Teaching and learning blurred together. Kids teaching bankers about knots—which bills to pay, which to ignore, which would tangle you forever. Elderly women teaching everyone about unison—turns out they'd been married fifty years combined to Jerome, started speaking together after his funeral, nobody's one person after that long, after that much shared insufficiency, after that much love despite.

"Each thing keeps its history," Vessel said one night, everyone assembled in the mess. Her own history: MFA Yale, adjunct teaching, museum education, fired, this.

"Value comes from how things relate—" someone added.

"Not from making them same!" someone else.

"The whole emerges from tension—" Queue said.

"Balance—" one elderly woman.

"Between parts," the other finished.

"Nothing loses itself—" Destiny started.

"To belong," everyone completed.

Someone asked: "But how do you know what connects to what?"

Vessel started to explain about micro-decisions, about choosing this not that, but stopped. Instead, she just started connecting things. Randomly. No, not randomly. By feel. By weight. By need. By something.

"Every choice invisible in the final—" Chanson said.

"But each rework shows another way—" Lint added.

"We're demonstrating—" Queue said.

"That meaning—" the elderly women in perfect sync.

"Emerges from—" the kids together.

"Choices!" Vessel finished. "What to connect! And how!"

The museum sent someone. Offered them jobs again. "But with protocols," the representative said, stepping carefully through the garbage-art-life covering the floor. Heels worth more than anyone's monthly food budget.

Vessel made a knot in the air. Everyone saw it somehow. The gesture of permanent commitment.

Chanson walked a loop around the representative, coming back changed. Each revolution carrying new information.

Lint held up three fingers. Then three more. Then stopped. Everything that couldn't be said about power and art and who decides what's garbage. Ellipses made physical.

They stayed. The warehouse getting messier and more organized simultaneously. Teaching knots that show their lumps—the ugly beautiful places where things join. Loops that develop through return—the evolution that comes from repetition with difference. Ellipses that hold what escapes—the unsaid that says everything.

"Connection and continuation..." Vessel wrote again, this time in pigeon shit mixed with paint. The materials at hand. The only materials that mattered.

Never ending. Always choosing. What to tie. What to loop. What to leave suspended in the space between naming and knowing...

The elderly women said, in perfect unison: "This is how everything..."

"...really works," everyone else finished, not in unison but in harmony, each voice finding its place in the chaos of connection, each knot necessary, each loop evolutionary, each ellipsis holding more truth than any complete sentence ever could.

---

We are all learning what to connect and how, discovering that every knot is a commitment that shows its lumps, every loop a chance for spiral development through mess, every ellipsis an admission of what escapes—creating assemblages where chaos and order coexist, where meaning emerges not from protocols but from the lived choice of what to tie together, what to let circulate, what to leave suspended in the permanent incompletion that is the only real completion we know...
