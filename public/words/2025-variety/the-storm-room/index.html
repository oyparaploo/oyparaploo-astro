The Storm Room

← Words / 2025 Variety

Later, Jing would think about wings—not the bread shaped like birds that they baked that morning, not the storm birds her student's daughter imagined in dough, but the invisible wings we give each other when the ground gives way. How we lift one another without meaning to, without planning, without even touching. She'd think about how a room with no walls could be the strongest shelter, how an accordion inherited from a dead mother could teach a banker to fly, how seventeen neighbors gathering at 2 AM could become, briefly, one creature with thirty-four wings, all beating toward morning.

But that was later. First, there was the storm.

Jing hadn't meant to become the person everyone turned to after storms. It had started three years ago, after Hurricane Isabel, when she'd opened her music studio at 2 AM because she couldn't sleep and found seventeen neighbors gathered in the hallway, all wakeful, all wordless, all waiting for something they couldn't name. Now it was just what happened: storm hits, people gather, Jing opens doors—first the studio, then later Briano's bakery where she had a key, then the community garden where they'd built what Briano called his "storm room," though it wasn't a room at all, just four benches facing each other under empty sky.

This morning—if 4 AM counted as morning—she stood in the bakery watching David knead dough by candlelight, the power still out from last night's weather. He was her newest accordion student, a fifty-year-old banker who'd inherited his mother's instrument and came to lessons with the grim determination of someone fulfilling a deathbed promise. But here, his hands working the dough, he looked different. Younger. Or maybe older in a better way, like someone who'd finally stopped trying so hard.

"Wing it," Briano had said earlier, holding up the dead electric mixer, and they'd all laughed—Jing, David, the collection of insomniacs and shift workers who materialized whenever the world tilted wrong. But the phrase stuck with her. It was what she'd been telling David at yesterday's lesson, his fingers trembling over the accordion keys while rain streamed down the windows. "That's all any of us do," she'd said, and watched his spine straighten, finding balance he didn't know he'd lost.

Now David shaped dough into something that might have been a bird or might have been abstract art. His seven-year-old daughter, delivered by his exhausted ex-wife an hour ago ("I have to get to the hospital, please, just this once"), watched with the intensity of someone memorizing a magic trick.

"Why does it grow?" the child asked.

"Invisible life," Briano said, appearing from the back room with more flour. "Yeast doing what it needs to do for future generations of yeast. Coincidentally feeding us."

Jing thought about her grandmother's accordion, sitting in her studio three blocks away. How her grandmother had played in Montreal cabarets, how the music had traveled through time and space to land in Baltimore, in David's reluctant hands, in this moment where he was finally understanding that playing by ear wasn't failure but freedom.

The child started humming—something tuneless but rhythmic, and Jing recognized it as the exact tempo of "Le Parapluie," the French chanson she'd been teaching David. Seventy beats per minute, the pace of a slow walk, a held breath, a heart at rest. Without thinking, she began harmonizing, and then David joined in with a bass line he'd never have attempted at his lesson, and even Briano started drumming on the counter with wooden spoons.

"This is it," Jing thought, watching David's daughter shape her dough into what she called "storm birds." This incomplete fragment of humanity, assembling something sweet from something broken. Through the fogged window, she could see the community garden, where later today they'd relocate the storm-damaged beehives. The honey would taste of thunder and flowers—she knew this somehow, the way she knew that David's mother had played accordion while his father was dying, that Briano had built the roofless room after his divorce, that everyone here was hoping some particular pain would be over soon.

The bread went into the oven at 375 degrees. The candles flickered. Someone mentioned that the power should return by noon, but nobody moved to leave. David tasted the sourdough starter—sour, sweet from added honey, salty from their working hands, with a pinch of chili Jing had added for shock, for wake-up, for the reminder that safety shouldn't make you sleepy.

"Your mother," Jing said to David, "she played that song, didn't she? The one about the umbrella?"

David's hands stilled. "Every night at the end. I hated it."

"And now?"

"Now I know she was hoping the pain would be over soon. His and hers."

The child looked up. "Whose pain?"

"Everyone's," David said simply, and his daughter nodded as if this made perfect sense, which maybe it did.

By six, the bread was done—dozens of wing-shaped loaves, each one different, each one imperfect. The first customers arrived to find the lights still off, the register dead, Briano making change from a cigar box. They bought everything, understanding without being told that they were purchasing more than bread.

"Same time next storm?" David asked, helping his daughter into her coat.

"It's not always storms," Jing said. "Sometimes it's just Tuesday. Sometimes it's just that someone needs to wing it with company."

She walked with them to the community garden, where Briano's storm room waited—those four benches under nothing, protecting nothing, everything. David's daughter ran ahead, arms spread like wings, and David said, "I'm going to play at the open mic next week. My mother's song. In French."

"You don't speak French," Jing observed.

"I'll wing it," he said, and she heard in his voice what she'd been waiting three months of lessons to hear: the understanding that imperfection was not failure but starting point, that incomplete fragments could make a whole, that sometimes the best shelter was no shelter at all.

Later, alone in her studio, Jing played the accordion—her grandmother's, David's mother's, everyone's and no one's. The notes tasted bitter-sweet in the afternoon light. Through the window, she could see neighbors working in the garden, relocating the hives, their hands sticky with honey that would shock against the memory of wind. Tomorrow David would come for his lesson and play something almost right. Tomorrow Briano would bake regular bread in regular shapes. Tomorrow the lights would work and the world would pretend to be solid.

But today had happened—the wing-shaped bread, the candlelight, the child's humming, the harmony they'd found without trying. Today they'd made a room without walls and called it shelter. Today they'd taken what remained and made it into morning.

She thought of David's mother, playing accordion through her husband's dying, and her own grandmother, singing in Montreal smoke, and David's daughter spreading her arms like wings, and all of them connected by this inadequate, portable, temporary shelter of sound and flour and hope.

"Wing it," she said to the empty room, and played on, knowing that somewhere in the city, in other rooms both walled and wall-less, others were doing the same—making it up as they went, finding the next note by ear, trusting that imperfect flight was still flight, that incomplete songs were still songs, that shelter could be nothing more than people gathering after storms to make bread shaped like the possibility of escape, the promise of return, the wings we give each other when the ground gives way.
