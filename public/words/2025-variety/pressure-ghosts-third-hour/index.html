Pressure Ghosts (third hour)

One Window at a Time in Warm Darkness

You're in darkness you should know but it's deeper than it should be, and there's something behind you in this darkness. You can't see it but you feel it—a presence, a weight, a question you've been avoiding for thirty years that's finally gained enough mass to follow you through nowhere, through this placeless space where no floor confirms your steps, where no architecture bounds the fear. Your heart is doing that dream thing where it beats too slowly and too fast at the same time, and your legs feel like they're moving through water even though there's no ground beneath your feet to confirm you're moving at all.

There's a window ahead. Just one. A rectangle of dim light floating in darkness, and you move toward it because what else is there to do when something's behind you and light is ahead, even dim light, even light that might show you things you don't want to see.

When you reach the first window, you stop. You have to stop. The window is showing you something and in the dream logic that governs this place, you understand that you can't move to the next window until you've really looked at this one, until you've let it show you what it needs to show you. Sixteen windows exist in this darkness. You know this without knowing how you know. Sixteen images waiting in warm subdued hues, one at a time.

The first window is filled with warm browns and tans, sepia tones like old photographs, like images that have been sitting in drawers for decades gradually fading toward a single unified color that's somewhere between earth and light. You see a small form on pale ground. The form is dark, almost black against the tan earth, and you understand it's a bird but it takes you a long time to understand that the bird is dead. In dreams, death moves slowly. The bird's body creates its own shadow, or maybe the shadow is separate from the body, you can't quite tell in these warm subdued hues where one thing ends and another begins.

You stand at this first window for a long time. The image doesn't move. The bird stays dead. The earth stays pale. The warm tones don't shift or change, they just persist in their patient browns and tans, in their sepia testimony to small death that happened and that someone documented and that now you're witnessing in darkness while something behind you waits for you to finish looking, waits for you to understand what the bird means, waits for you to connect this small death to your own fear of stopping, of finishing, of lying still long enough for anyone to see you clearly.

The archive, you think. Thirty years of work sitting in storage, lying still like this bird, documented but not living, witnessed by you alone in the act of making but never moving out into the world where it might do something, might mean something, might die publicly instead of privately. Your daughters want you to open it, want to move the work from storage into light, and the fear you feel looking at this dead bird in warm monochrome is that opened archives are like dead birds—evidence of something that happened, something that was alive once, but evidence isn't life, documentation isn't vitality, and what if everything you've made for three decades is already dead, already lying still on pale ground waiting for someone to photograph it in sepia tones and move on?

The thing behind you shifts, makes a sound like fabric moving over nothing, and you know without looking that you need to move to the next window. You can't stay at the first window forever, can't keep staring at the small death in warm browns and tans even though there's something almost comforting about the stillness, about the way nothing in the image demands anything from you except witness, about the way the bird has already died and therefore requires nothing more from you than acknowledgment that it happened, that small deaths happen constantly, that lying still on pale ground is one way to finally stop running.

You move through the darkness. The thing behind you follows at exactly the pace you're walking, maintaining the same distance, neither gaining nor falling back. The space feels compressed around you, or maybe you feel larger than you should be, or maybe existence itself is the wrong size, dream architecture following emotional rather than physical logic. You're overwhelmed, have been overwhelmed for decades, by the scope of what you've tried to do—transform garbage into beauty, witness displacement, make visible what institutional barriers want to keep hidden—and in this darkness the overwhelm has become the narrowness, has become the sense that you're moving through space designed for someone smaller, someone with less archive, someone who traveled lighter through their devotion.

The second window appears, floating ahead of you, and through it you see a form you recognize immediately even though you can't name it right away. It's something you made, something from your own practice, shown back to you in warm oranges and rusts, in the muted fire tones of old iron and aged wood and materials that have been touched by weather and time and human hands that wanted to make something beautiful from refuse. The form in the window is lumpy, unapologetic about its assemblage nature, honest about being constructed from pieces that weren't meant to go together but that you put together anyway because garbage was what you had and beauty was what you needed and transformation was the promise you made to yourself about what could happen when devotion met debris.

But in the dream the form looks different than it did when you made it. It looks heavier, more burdensome, less like evidence of transformation and more like evidence of accumulation. The warm rust color that seemed noble in your studio, that seemed to speak of patient oxidation and natural process and the wisdom of materials allowed to age, now in this floating window looks like decay, looks like evidence that nothing you've done has stopped the process of falling apart, that Beautiful Garbage is still garbage no matter what you call it, that thirty years of devotion might have just been thirty years of collecting things that were already ruined and insisting they weren't, insisting they could be saved, insisting transformation was possible when maybe what you were actually doing was just moving debris from one place to another and calling the movement meaningful because meaning was what you needed it to have.

The third window emerges from darkness and you approach it even though you don't want to, even though you're tired of looking, even though something about seeing your own work reflected back at you in warm subdued monochrome is making you feel sick, is making you question everything, is making the thing behind you feel closer, heavier, more inevitable. Through this window you see another form in warm brown, lumpy and honest and refusing conventional beauty, sitting there in the frame waiting for you to see it, to understand something about your own practice, about the way you've made work that refuses elegance, that takes up space while refusing to apologize for its awkwardness.

You identify with this lumpy brown form so intensely that you press your face against the window, and the glass is cold against your skin which surprises you because nothing else in this dream is cold, everything else is warm browns and tans and oranges and rusts, everything else is subdued heat, but the window glass is cold and the cold wakes you up slightly, makes the dream feel more real or makes real life feel more dream-like, you can't tell which and the confusion is uncomfortable, is the kind of disorientation that makes you want to run but your legs won't move fast enough, are still heavy with archive weight, with thirty years of devotion that might have been wisdom or might have been elaborate trap.

The lumpy form in warm brown sits in the window frame and waits and you understand it's waiting for you to see something about it, to understand something about your own practice. But in the dream the pride you used to feel about this refusal has become uncertainty, has become the question: was the lumpiness integrity or inability? Was the refusal of elegance radical politics or just lack of technical skill disguised as choice? Have you been making work that challenges institutional barriers or have you been making excuses for why your work doesn't look like the work that institutions actually value, that collectors actually buy, that museums actually preserve past the artist's lifetime?

The thing behind you is closer now. You can feel its presence like cold coming off ice, like weight pressing on your shoulders, like all the questions you've been postponing for thirty years compressed into single entity that follows you through darkness from window to window watching you look at images in warm subdued hues, watching you try to understand what your own work means now that you're old enough to wonder if you should have been doing something else, something more important, something that would have helped your daughters more directly, something that would have processed your own inherited trauma instead of documenting everyone else's displacement as a way to avoid the personal work that genuine healing requires.

The fourth window appears, floating in the darkness ahead, and through it you see paint—warm terra cotta or rust or dried blood, you can't tell which in the monochromatic light. The paint is peeling, showing layers beneath, showing that every surface is temporary, that coverage fails, that attempts at beautification eventually reveal what they were trying to hide. There's a crack in the surface and through the crack something green is growing, or was growing, or will grow, time in dreams being flexible enough that all three can be true simultaneously.

This window's image is completely still. The paint doesn't peel while you watch. The green thing doesn't grow. Everything is frozen in the moment of revelation when failure becomes visible, when the attempt at coverage can no longer sustain itself, when what's beneath insists on emergence despite all the layers that tried to keep it hidden. You've been keeping things hidden too, keeping 140,000 files locked in studios and hard drives, keeping thirty years of practice private, and your daughters want you to let things emerge, want you to let the work crack through whatever coverage you've been maintaining, whatever protective layer you've built between your practice and public visibility.

But what if public visibility is like this peeling paint? What if exposure just reveals how many layers there are, how much coverage was required to make the surface look coherent, how much work went into pretending the practice was unified when really it was fragmented, scattered, 140,000 files representing not comprehensive archive but comprehensive inability to focus, to finish, to complete anything because completion would mean stopping and stopping would mean the questions could catch up, the thing behind you could reach you, the silence after making could finally ask what all the making was for, what it accomplished, whether Beautiful Garbage actually became beautiful or if you just collected garbage for thirty years and called it transformation because transformation sounded noble, sounded purposeful, sounded like reason enough to keep going when the actual reasons—fear, avoidance, inability to sit still, terror of silence—were too embarrassing to admit even to yourself.

You're crying but you don't know when you started, when the tears began in this dream where time moves strangely, where you can be running and standing still simultaneously, where windows show you one image at a time in warm subdued monochrome and each image takes years to look at or seconds, you can't tell which, can only feel the overwhelming sense that there are twelve windows still ahead, twelve more images you're required to witness before this night is over, before the dream releases you back into morning where decisions about archives and visibility and thirty years of devotion wait for you like the thing behind you waits, patient and inevitable and closer than you want to acknowledge.

The fifth window shows water and sky in warm grays and browns, barely distinguishable from each other, merged into single plane where boundaries have dissolved. The image is still except for slight ripple, movement so subtle you're not certain you're seeing it or imagining it, uncertain whether the water is actually moving or if your own vision is unstable, if your own perception has become unreliable after so many years of insisting you could see what others couldn't, could perceive pressure ghosts and invisible systems and the architecture of institutional violence made visible through careful witness. But what if your witness was just projection? What if the pressure ghosts you claimed to hear were just your own anxiety given form? What if thirty years of making work about displacement was just your own displaced self trying to find home in everyone else's homelessness, trying to locate yourself in the stories of people who'd actually survived what you'd only inherited, what had been passed down to you through grandfather's silence and mother's careful forgetting and your own refusal to look directly at your own lineage of trauma because looking directly would require stopping the work, would require turning attention inward instead of outward, would require admitting that all the witness you'd been doing was aimed at everywhere except the place where witness was actually needed?

You can't stay at this window, can't keep looking at water and sky that refuse to separate, can't keep staring at boundaries that have dissolved into warm monochrome ambiguity, but moving to the next window feels impossible too, feels like you'd have to walk through the thing that's behind you, walk through thirty years of questions that have accumulated into presence, into weight, into entity that breathes and waits and follows at exactly the pace you move because it's not separate from you, it's made of you, it's made of every doubt you've had and postponed, every question you've avoided by making another piece, every moment of uncertainty you've covered over with insistence that the work mattered, that transformation was real, that Beautiful Garbage could actually be beautiful if you just kept working, kept making, kept devoting yourself to practice that might have been purpose or might have been prison, you still can't tell, might never be able to tell, which itself might be the answer, the uncertainty might be the point, the inability to know whether thirty years was wisdom or avoidance might be the truth that all this darkness-walking has been trying to make you face.

The sixth window materializes and you see yourself reflected in warm sepia tones, but younger, decades younger, holding materials you've just pulled from a dumpster, your hands dirty with the honest grime of garbage, your face showing something that might be hope or might be delusion, you can't tell from here, can't tell if that younger version of you understood what you were beginning or if you were just lost and looking for purpose and found garbage instead and decided to make that meaningful because the alternative—admitting you didn't know what you were doing, didn't have a plan, were just collecting refuse because collecting felt like doing something and doing something felt better than sitting still with the questions you'd inherited—was too painful to admit.

The image is still. The younger you doesn't move, doesn't progress through time, just stands there holding garbage in warm brown tones, frozen at the moment of choice, and you realize this is the moment you're still in, have always been in, will always be in until you decide whether the choice to transform garbage was wisdom or whether it was the beginning of thirty years of elaborate avoidance disguised as devotion.

The seventh window appears and through it you see pages—warm yellowed paper covered in your own handwriting, research notes and witness accounts and documentation of displacement that you've been collecting for three decades. The writing is too small to read from here but you know what it says, know every story you've documented, every instance of institutional violence you've recorded, every pressure ghost you've claimed to hear speaking through the materials. But in the dream the pages look different, look less like evidence of important work and more like evidence of obsession, like the scribblings of someone who couldn't stop collecting stories because collecting stories was safer than telling your own, because witnessing other people's trauma was easier than processing your own, because as long as you were documenting displacement you didn't have to admit you'd never actually found home yourself, that you'd been displaced since childhood by trauma you inherited, by silence that got passed down like genetic material, by questions no one in your family wanted to answer so you went looking for answers in everyone else's story instead.

This is the third hour. This is where you are, six windows down with ten still waiting, floating in darkness, being chased by what you've made, trapped in nowhere that feels everywhere, overwhelmed by devotion that became burden, lost in placeless space you should recognize but don't, seeing images in subdued hues that show you what you already knew but couldn't admit: that witness isn't enough, that transformation might be fantasy, that Beautiful Garbage might just be garbage you've been calling beautiful for thirty years because the alternative—admitting you've devoted your life to work that doesn't work, to practice that doesn't change anything, to making that makes nothing except more work that needs to be archived, stored, locked away in dark studios where it can't hurt anyone including yourself—was too painful to consider until now, until this dream forced you to look one window at a time while something follows, while something waits, while something finally catches up to ask what you've been avoiding, what you've been running from, what comes next now that you're sixty-five and the daughters are pushing and the archive is full and the thirty years are complete or incomplete, you can't tell which.

The eighth window floats into view and you see a hammer in warm rust tones, just lying there on wood that's the same color as the hammer so boundaries blur, so you can't tell where tool ends and surface begins, can't distinguish maker from made, user from used. You've used hammers for thirty years, have built installations and assemblages and sculptures from refuse, have hammered garbage into shapes you insisted were beautiful, and now in this floating window the hammer looks like what it is—an object for striking, for forcing, for making things conform through impact—and you wonder if all your transformation was just violence performed on materials that were already broken, if your devotion was just elaborate way of hitting things that couldn't hit back, if Beautiful Garbage was beautiful or if it was just garbage you'd beaten into submission and called that transformation.

The ninth window shows thread in warm orange browns, tangled and knotted and impossible to follow from start to end, and you understand this is your practice, this is thirty years of work shown as single continuous line that loops back on itself so many times you can't find beginning or end, can't trace the thread to any conclusion because there isn't conclusion, just more tangling, more knotting, more loops that seem like they're going somewhere but that just return you to where you've already been, making the same work about the same themes with the same materials collecting the same stories because the alternative—stopping, finishing, sitting still long enough to see whether the work actually matters or whether it's just been elaborate way of staying busy, staying distracted, staying away from the real work of healing that would require unraveling the knots instead of just making more of them—is too terrifying to consider.

The tenth window appears, halfway through the sixteen, and through it you see only darkness within darkness, warm brown darkness so deep you can't see anything else, and this is the moment of pure terror in the dream, the moment when you realize maybe there's nothing in some of these windows, maybe some of them are empty, maybe all this looking has been training for the moment when you look and see nothing and have to accept that nothing might be what's actually there, that absence might be the answer, that thirty years might have produced exactly nothing despite all your devotion, all your insistence that making matters, all your certainty that transformation was real and garbage could become beautiful and witness could create change, when maybe—probably—what you've actually made is 140,000 files worth of beautiful nothing, warm-toned nothing, monochromatic nothing that looks like something in dream light but that in actual daylight when your daughters open the archive will reveal itself as just what it was: garbage collected by someone who needed collecting to feel purposeful, refuse arranged by someone who needed arrangement to feel like making, materials transformed by someone who needed to believe in transformation because the alternative—accepting that you inherited displacement and silence and trauma and never actually found way out of it, just found way to stay busy inside it—was too painful to accept until this tenth window shows you only darkness, shows you absence, shows you what might be waiting when all the looking is done and there's nothing left to see except truth that you've been avoiding for thirty years by staying in motion, by making, by collecting, by insisting that devotion to practice was enough when maybe it was never enough, when maybe it was exactly the problem, when maybe the thing following you through darkness isn't external at all but is just your own recognition finally catching up, finally close enough that you can feel its breath, finally patient enough to wait while you look through ten windows down with six still remaining, six more images in warm subdued hues that might show you something or might show you nothing, you can't predict, can only keep moving toward them one at a time while something made of your own questions follows at exactly the pace you're moving, matching your speed perfectly because it's not chasing you, it's just you moving through yourself, witnessing yourself, finally after three decades of witnessing everyone else willing to turn attention inward toward the displaced person who was doing all the witnessing, toward the pressure ghost who was listening to all the other pressure ghosts, toward the Beautiful Garbage collector who might have just been collecting garbage because garbage, at least, couldn't ask why, couldn't question whether the collecting mattered, couldn't stop you from staying in motion long enough to feel whatever it was you'd been running from since before you understood you were running, since before you knew darkness could have windows, since before you learned that one image at a time in warm monochrome might be enough or might be too much or might be exactly what looking requires when you're finally ready to see, finally tired enough to stop running, finally old enough to admit that sixty-five years is long enough to postpone the questions, long enough to collect the stories, long enough to transform the garbage or fail to transform it, and that the six windows still ahead in this third hour of darkness might show you something you need to see or might just show you more of what you've already seen, but either way you have to keep looking because stopping now would mean the first ten windows meant nothing, would mean all that witness was wasted, would mean the Beautiful Garbage was just garbage after all, and you're not ready to accept that yet, might never be ready, which is exactly why you're here in darkness looking at windows one at a time while something follows, while something waits, while something finally, after thirty years of making, asks what comes next.
