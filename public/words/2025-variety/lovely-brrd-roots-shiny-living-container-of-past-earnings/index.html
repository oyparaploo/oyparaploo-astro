Lovely Brrd Roots Shiny Living Container Of Past Earnings

Words / 2025 Variety

Imagine standing in front of your work—the piece you poured your heart into—feeling both exhilarated and terrified, like holding your breath before diving into deep water. It’s yours, undeniably yours. And yet, the same thought circles back, persistent and unrelenting: will this ever be enough? Will I ever be enough? Art, after all, is a paradox—it gives you wings while weighing your pockets with stones. The freedom to create is untamed, but the tether of survival doesn’t loosen easily. And so, you learn to navigate the in-between, balancing passion and pragmatism, rebellion and surrender, chaos and clarity.

There’s humor in this tightrope walk. Think of the person who eyes your work at a craft fair, tilts their head, and asks, “So...what’s the purpose of this?” The purpose? You resist the urge to reply with “What’s the purpose of your favorite song?” or “What’s the purpose of sunshine?” Instead, you smile faintly and say something polite, knowing all the while that art defies purposes—it exists to breathe, to connect, to speak in ways logic never could.

Or picture this: you’re knee-deep in a project that feels more alive than anything you’ve touched in weeks. Your hands move instinctively, the hours dissolve. You forget about overdue bills, unanswered texts, and dinner plans. You’re free. Then comes the inevitable, a well-meaning voice from across the room asking, “But how do you make money doing this?” And there it is, the spell broken in an instant. It’s not their fault, really—it’s the question that haunts most creators. Can passion coexist with practicality? Should it? You don’t know, but you keep creating anyway.

And then there are those miraculous moments—fleeting but unforgettable—that remind you why you began this journey at all. Someone pauses in front of your work, their face lighting up, their breath catching. “This speaks to me,” they say quietly. It’s not a paycheck, but it’s purpose. It’s proof that what you make matters, even if only for one person. In that instant, the doubts don’t disappear, but they soften, just enough for you to keep going.

Art demands vulnerability. It asks you to stand, exposed, in front of an audience that may or may not understand what you’ve poured into your work. It’s the unfinished painting that reveals your own insecurities, the poem that feels too raw to share, the song that holds more of your tears than melody. And still, you choose it. You choose to hold space for contradictions—beauty and frustration, freedom and uncertainty, joy and the ache of effort.

But art isn’t just struggle. It’s light breaking through the cracks. It’s the sound of your tools as the sun hits them, golden and warm. It’s the quiet satisfaction of losing yourself in a rhythm, of chasing something infinite within the finite. In the unassuming details of the everyday—the frayed edge of fabric, the brushstroke that changes everything—you find something extraordinary. Something that breathes, not just through your work, but through you.

Here’s the truth: there might not be an answer to the riddle of earning versus creating. It will always be a question, lingering like a shadow at the edge of every choice. But maybe art doesn’t need an answer. Maybe it only needs you—your hands, your heart, your willingness to show up even when it feels impossible. Because creation is its own kind of rebellion. It’s a reminder that beauty exists in the chaos, that meaning can grow from struggle, that the act of making is itself a victory.

Take this with you: you don’t need certainty to create. You don’t need perfection. You only need to trust that the work you do matters, even in ways you might never fully understand. Art transforms—it doesn’t erase difficulty, but it gives it shape, depth, connection. And in that connection, there’s immortality, a kind of magic that defies logic but feels undeniably real. So keep making. Keep chasing the infinite. And let that be enough. Let that be everything.

From Marks
