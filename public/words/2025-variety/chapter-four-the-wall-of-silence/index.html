Chapter Four

The Wall of Silence

← Words / 2025 Variety

The tramontane had not stopped all night.

It tore at shutters, bullied signs, slapped ropes until knots complained. The whole town seemed to vibrate with it, every surface pressed into reluctant percussion. By morning, even the harbor sounded altered. The tide’s steady shhh… slap was frayed at the edges, hiss turned sharper, as if the sea itself were sandblasted.

Troll Tearsen hadn’t slept. The floorboards in her hotel room creaked with every gust, tallying the hours like a metronome with a grudge. She lay awake and listened to doors clicking down the corridor, a radiator knocking its thin complaint, the building’s old bones speaking in thermal shifts. On the other side of the wall, Libaux Torrini coughed once and went quiet. She wondered if he’d fallen asleep or if he simply knew how to disappear more completely into noise than she did.

When she stepped outside, gulls had already begun. Their laughter pitched higher by the wind, they copied each other in thin, cutting cries—kyak-kyak-kyak—that slashed against the citadel and came back thinner, meaner. Echo as erosion.

They met Aïcha at the town hall.

The foyer amplified footfalls and then swallowed them, a trick of marble and height that made people sound important as they entered and unimportant by the time they finished crossing the room. Aïcha carried a banker’s box heavy with paper and placed it on a table with a thud that rolled around the hall and died in the corners.

“Miller Corbal was digging where no one wanted her,” Aïcha said, bracelets ticking like glass beads on a jar. “Cross-border placements. Signatures that don’t match. Children moved on paper, not in reality. She found three anomalies and asked for more files. She was—”

“Persistent,” Libaux said. His tone made it sound like a virtue and a flaw.

“Right,” Aïcha said. “And dangerous, once she said a name.”

Troll reached for a stack of photocopies. Paper rasped sharp—rrrsk—and the fluorescent light above them buzzed along in a single anxious note. Names marched down the pages in neat lists. Departments. Contractors. Consultants. The letters lacked faces but not weight.

She found it three pages in, a small stamp blurred by the copier but legible enough to put the room on its axis.

Fredda Stremler.

A consultant’s stamp on a transfer review. Another on a funding memo. The echo of a signature in bureaucratic ink.

“Why here?” Troll asked, under her breath. “Why this town?”

Aïcha spread her hands. “Collioure has always been a passage. Refugees over the mountain, soldiers through the harbor. Things that are not meant to be found arrive here. Things that are meant to be found disappear.”

A door at the end of the hall boomed shut. The sound traveled up the stairwell, spread out, and vanished.

Troll drew a small circle around Stremler’s name with her pen. The ink caught and whispered against the paper—ssst—like a blade testing cloth.

“Show us where Miller went,” she said.

They climbed to the citadel by a path that had learned to funnel wind. Every switchback made its own vowel—whrr… grrr… whooo—as though the stones were organ pipes and the tramontane the player. Vinegar gusts found the holes in their clothes and slithered in. The sea, when Troll looked back, seemed not to move but to contract and relax as one great muscle.

Inside the citadel, the smell changed: dust woven with salt, cold ash, old beeswax. The chapel’s door complained on its hinges with the long, slow inhale of a sleeper refusing to wake.

“Is this where you met her?” Libaux asked.

“Not me,” Aïcha said. “The caretaker.”

The chapel swallowed their words. The space was shaped for reverberation: stone arches, high vaulting, air that felt older than breath. Troll tried a quiet “hello” to test the room. The walls multiplied the syllable at once—lo… lo… lo…—then gave them back damp and diminished, as if unwilling to return anything in the condition it was taken.

On a side table in the sacristy, a small stack of pages waited beneath a paperweight. A wartime list of relocations, crisp copies of older pages, yellowed in their originals. Troll slid the weight aside and turned the top sheet. The paper carried the faint sandpaper sound of old records too often touched.

At the corner of one copy, something round and green caught light.

A marble.

Taped into place with a cross of brittle cellophane, as if to anchor the page against a wind no one else could feel. Another page, another marble: clear, unblemished, a small star-trap for the chapel’s lamps. A third, cracked in a white lightning vein that threw small shivers of light onto the stone.

“Miller taped them there,” Aïcha said. “Placeholders. She said a thing remembered is a thing kept in place. Perhaps she meant herself.”

Troll touched the edge of tape with her fingernail. The plastic lifted with a sound like an old whisper. The marble rocked in its cross and touched stone—tik—shockingly loud in a room that amplified even thought.

She left it where it was and stepped back, throat tight.

A cough echoed from the nave, doubled and then doubled again. A thin man with a lantern shuffled in, shoes scuffing the floor in small sandpaper sighs. His face had the color of old parchment, every crease a place where dust had claimed residence.

“This chapel remembers,” he said. His voice wore reverb like a cloak.

“Remembers what?” Troll asked.

“The ones who passed,” he said. “The ones who didn’t. It remembers all the names. Walls are better at keeping silence than men.”

Libaux took a step forward. “Did you know Miller Corbal?”

The caretaker weighed his answer as if the room might refuse it. “She had the look of someone chasing a bell that never stops ringing. She asked about files from after the war. She asked about consultants who came to ‘help.’ That man—Stremler—has walked these halls. Years ago. Papers that were here became not here.”

“Files about children?” Troll asked.

The caretaker’s lantern glass clicked in its frame as the wind found a seam. “About the ones who were moved to be safe,” he said softly, “and the ones who went missing because safety is a word men use when they mean something else.”

His cough returned, longer, the chapel forcing it to sound like a crowd. When he turned away, the lantern painted a thin gold circle on stone, then none. His footsteps receded in duplicates, like a procession of ghosts leaving by the side door.

They stood in the thick of that multiplying quiet for a while. The marbles on the pages, small as knuckles, seemed to grow heavier by presence alone.

Troll’s phone vibrated once in her pocket, a private tremor inside the giant space. Miyake Coscia. She didn’t take it out. A second vibration followed—a voicemail. She closed her eyes as though that might also silence whatever had been recorded.

“Let’s copy the pages,” Libaux said. “And then we’re done with this echo chamber.”

They worked quickly, the copier wheezing soft complaints, each whirsythm joined to the wind’s low vowel outside. Troll laid her palm flat on the glass to press out a wrinkle; her heartbeat tapped a small tattoo into the machine’s casing, a private metronome lost beneath the room’s engineered boom.

When they were finished, Troll touched the green marble again with the back of a knuckle. It barely moved in its tape cross. Still, the tik it made was a pin dropped in a cathedral. The sound ran the aisles and hid in the apse.

She slipped one copy of the wartime list into her satchel. The paper made a dry, decisive sound.

The path down from the citadel felt steeper in descent, each step a small negotiation. The wind had eased, enough that edges could be heard again—the papery sound of an old poster unsticking from a wall, the dutiful clank of a flag ring hitting flagpole, two voices arguing about nothing with voices that said everything was at stake.

At the foot of the hill, the harbor resumed its breath. Nets slapped boats with softer whup… whup now, less frequent, a tired swimmer turning. Halyards tapped their metal syllables: ping… ping-ping. A scooter passed on the far road and failed to backfire, as if even engines were careful not to produce a jump-scare after a night like this one.

They took a bench near the church and let the wind finish calming. Troll opened her satchel and slid out the copy of the wartime list. Names etched into logics that had felt clean at the time. The list wore its order like perfume, something applied to mask.

Her voicemail waited. She put the phone to her ear.

Static first, thin as frost. Then Miyake’s voice, careful as always, polite even with grief: “She’s sleeping more. Breath is… it’s light. She asked if the marbles are still on your shelf.” A soft pause. The hiss of the machine continued. “I told her they are. Come if you can.”

The archive in Troll’s chest creaked, a shelf bowing. She turned her face toward the sea and let the phone go silent. The tide said nothing helpful in reply.

Libaux stared out at the water. “If Stremler came to the citadel, he came because it meant something to him, not because it meant something to her. People like that want their footsteps to echo in old stone.”

“Echo and record,” Troll said. “There’s always an audience.”

“Is there?” Libaux asked, not looking at her. “Or are we just two people applauding at the right moments to keep him performing?”

A gull laughed as if it had been holding the line for that cue.

They walked the quay for an hour of nothing. The market awnings flapped themselves dry. A florist hosed down her patch of stones and the water made comic loop-sounds in the drain—glugluglug—before vanishing. Luc crossed the square with headphones around his neck and an expression that said the world had not yet decided to be reasonable.

“Any luck?” he asked.

“Only that the wind got bored,” Libaux said.

Luc grimaced. “Audio likes wind. It composes.”

“Stremler likes rooms that will hold his after-voice,” Troll said. “Do you know where a man like that goes when the church is done with him and the club is closed?”

Luc thought a moment. “He rents a view and calls it privacy.” He pointed with his chin toward a row of apartments where shutters were painted a blue too young for the building beneath them. “Top floors. They always want sky and glass.”

“We all like glass,” Troll said. “Some of us make it sing.”

They didn’t find Stremler that day. They found evidence of his preference: concierge notes about a polite man who never needed housekeeping but always asked for help with the balcony door; a courier who had delivered a package with no return address, weight recorded at exactly one kilogram; a neighbor who admired the cleanliness of his shoes. Fragments, each with the tone of something that would swear it never met a crime if called to the stand.

By evening the sea had become patient again. The light slid into the water without defending itself; boats rocked with small consenting noises; the town loosened its jaw.

They went back to the narrow wall of stone where the tide combed the harbor edge and sat like two people who were through pretending they didn’t want to hear anything. Troll took from her pocket the single marble she had not known she had taken—the one taped to Miller’s copy that had loosened under her finger and ended up in her coat by some crooked path of intention and accident. Green glass, white coil.

She set it on the wall. It hummed imperceptibly with the small tremors everything hummed with at the sea. A faint gust rolled it a centimeter. It found the smallest fall of grit, paused, and tapped the edge.

Tik.

Libaux’s hand moved fast. He caught it between finger and thumb before gravity could do its work. “Careful,” he said. “Evidence.”

“Or memory,” Troll said.

“Sometimes the difference is paperwork.”

They didn’t speak for a while. Behind them, an alley cat investigated a bottle, sent it spinning—prrrrt-prrt-prt—the glass wagging in tiny circles before settling. Somewhere, a couple laughed too quickly, the sound of a joke not good enough but good enough to pretend.

Troll took the marble back and closed it into her palm. For a moment it warmed there, or pretended to, the way objects do when they’re allowed to become symbols.

“We go to Paris,” Libaux said. “If Pigalle is where Miller wrote that the voices go, then that’s where the performance wants its audience.”

“We go to Paris,” Troll repeated, not to agree but to hear how the city’s name sounded in this harbor air.

She heard in it the microphone’s feedback she had not yet met, the accordion thread across a Metro tunnel, the pigeon percussion, the way neon insists in the ear when the eyes are closed.

The sea flexed. The town breathed. The marble in her hand held its silence like a story.
