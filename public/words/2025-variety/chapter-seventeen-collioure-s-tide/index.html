Chapter Seventeen ~ Collioure’s Tide

Words / 2025 Variety

The train sighed them back into the south as if exhaling an old story. Collioure rose out of a blue that knew how to pretend calm. The harbor’s curve caught the light and refused to return it, nets breathed their salt-wet rhythm, and the tramontane kept its opinions to itself for once.

Sound had changed since they were last here. It was cleaner at first listen—less clatter, fewer loose vowels—but beneath the postcard lived a new undernote, the kind of low mmm a throat makes when speech is about to happen and then decides not to.

Troll Tearsen stood at the quay where Miller Corbal had once been brought back by the stones, the memory uninvited and correct. Libaux Torrini leaned on the sea wall with a posture that said he trusted the water but not the men who rented it.

Aïcha met them with two coffees and the expression she reserved for rooms that tried to lie politely. “Banyuls customs,” she said, handing Troll a paper cup that smelled like effort and heat. “RIVAJE, with a J. Crates marked ‘acoustic braces.’ Cleared as cultural logistics.”

“Braces for what?” Libaux asked.

“The sea,” Aïcha said. “You’ll hear.”

They walked the breakwater. Bolts shone new against old stone. Stainless brackets hugged the granite at regular intervals, each with a thin plate set just above the spray line. To the eye: reinforcement. To the ear: a low vowel the water pretended not to be making until you noticed its breath arriving with a pitch.

Troll placed her palm against a plate. It vibrated not with the wave, but half a beat after, as if a conductor with a smug metronome had been hired to supervise the Mediterranean. “Delay,” she said softly. “He’s tuned the tide.”

“You can say braces if you want to remain invited to lunch,” Aïcha said. “The fishermen call them ‘singing clamps’ and spit when they pass.”

A fisherman spat, on cue. He raised his chin at them, not hostile, simply including them in the day’s physics. “Nighttime is worse,” he said. “When the swell thinks too much. The wall hums. The boats complain. We sleep inside a throat.” He twitched a net with his wrist. The floats knocked together with a tok-tok as if agreeing.

Luc waved from the municipal office balcony. His headphones hung around his neck like a reluctant saint’s collar. “I have the cough of the sea,” he said when they climbed. “Listen.”

He rolled tape. The harbor arrived through speakers made for bitterness—halyards pinging thin bells, gulls laughing in the register where ridicule lives. Underneath: a low, sustained tone around nineteen cycles, swelling when a wave hit the plates, decaying with a precise tail.

“Your Meuller’s note from Austria?” Luc continued. “Nineteen hertz. He wrote me a paragraph asking for impulse response and then apologized for the paragraph. I gave him what he wanted and none of what he didn’t.”

Troll closed her eyes and followed the tone with her jaw. It sat where headaches are born. “He’s moved from rooms to coastlines,” she said.

“Rooms are greedy. Coasts are eternal,” Luc said. “If you want a myth to carry, you staple it to a thing that outlasts you.”

Aïcha set an invoice on the desk: Entrepôt → Banyuls. RIVAJE’s clean stamp. A notation at the bottom: SILLON / tranche 2 in a hand too careful to be honest.

“Sillon,” Libaux read. “Furrow. Wake. Or groove.” He knocked his knuckles on the desk, a private metronome. “He’s cutting a groove into the coast.”

“Where do we pull first?” Troll asked.

“Banyuls,” Aïcha said. “They installed along the caves and the outer quay. And—” She glanced toward the citadel, where stone remembered every century including this one. “Someone with a key visited after hours and left nothing people want to write down.”

The citadel’s steward was a thin man with a lantern and a face like a folded map. He had invented new ways to sigh since their last meeting. “They came with quiet,” he said. “They left with less.” He led them into the chapel. Light hung in a way that suggested apology. The pages where Miller had once taped marbles had been returned to their shelves; the tape marks remained like pale crosses where someone had forgotten to finish a prayer.

“Locks?” Troll asked.

The steward lifted a ring of keys. “They work. So did theirs.”

Near the sacristy door, fresh adhesive shone where nothing should have shone. Troll touched it and lifted a strand. It echoed faintly—her gloved finger translated glue into a thin, high wire that could have been noise or alarm or both.

“Transducers here?” Libaux asked.

The steward shook his head. “No machines that confess. But the chapel has leaned toward the hum from outside. It is a polite room. It tries to harmonize.”

Troll stood where a person stands to measure their own breath against a space. She said her mother’s name under it. The sound did what sound does here: multiplied gently, returned human, not miraculous. For once, the room resisted becoming part of a program. It helped.

Back on the breakwater, the sea rehearsed its single sentence: shhh… slap. Between syllables the plates gave their engineered reply. mmm. The sun pretended to be warm and failed but earned points for effort.

Libaux crouched by a bracket. “Four bolts. Two bond lines. Transducer under the plate. A cheap transmitter feeding itself into the clamps down the line. Like the mountain, only wet.”

Troll rolled a marble across the top course of stone. Clear glass clicked once against a scar in the granite. The sound sank into the stack and came back as a note a touch higher, like the wall had wanted to clear its throat and only managed a polite cough.

“Meuller?” she asked.

Luc had already sent him live. Meuller’s reply arrived as numbers and a sentence: Tail 1.9s, mild flutter. Your brace spacing = tuned delay. Pull every third and the groove falls apart. Also: your click on granite rings a D sharp—sorry, my brain won’t stop naming things.

Aïcha watched the fishermen watch them. “We’ll be on the front page of what passes for a newspaper if we start unscrewing bolts without a vest that says ‘right.’”

“Do we have a vest that says right?” Libaux asked.

“I can ask the mayor to borrow one from his conscience,” Aïcha said. “He keeps it in a drawer under ‘photographs with ribbon-cuttings.’”

They waited for dark because dark has always been when work sneaks out. While they waited, Troll and Luc listened to the day’s tapes. Somewhere between halyard and gull, a sound repeated that shouldn’t have: a tik rolled thin, almost swallowed. Troll leaned toward the speaker as if proximity would teach her a language.

“Where?” she asked.

Luc pinched and scrolled. “Here.” He isolated the lane near the church steps. The marble shop had been closed for years; the man who’d sold floats had retired to a bar stool with a view of a different wall. Still: tik. Once, then not again, which was worse.

They followed the lane. The shop windows reflected them as poorer people. The door was new, pretending to be old. Inside, a faint light moved and then stopped when Troll knocked with knuckles that were tired of not making noise.

A woman in her forties opened after a beat that tested patience. She was dressed in “I am not here,” which only works if both parties agree. “Closed,” she said.

“We’re friends of sound,” Troll said. “We brought no receipts.”

The woman held the door the way one holds a thought that might try to leave. She looked at Troll’s coat, which looked like it had a badge in it even when it didn’t. “I don’t make toys,” she said. “Only glass.”

On a workbench: spheres not yet spheres. Molten thought cooled to caution. In a tray by the door: three polished marbles seeded with tiny bubbles in deliberate veins. A contact mic grinned from under felt. The woman saw Troll see it and lifted her chin. “He pays,” she said. Not apology. Confession, bare.

“Fredda Stremler?” Libaux asked.

“I did not ask for the name,” she said. “He sent orders. Specific faults. He sent a small piece of wood and asked me to roll them upon it to listen.”

“Did you hear anything?” Troll asked.

“I heard money,” she said. “And loneliness.” She looked at her hands. They were marked with the heat that teaches patience. “I made floats once,” she said, glancing toward the retired shop across the lane. “Nothing sang then. Now everything does.”

“Will you make more?” Troll asked.

“Will you stop him?” she countered. “I would like my hands to be boring again.”

They left with nothing but the small mercy of her answer. Night took the edges off Collioure and replaced them with more edges. The sea kept its appointment with the wall.

Aïcha had found a vest. It didn’t say right, but it said Travaux, which in France can be made to mean most things if you carry a clipboard and refuse to apologize. A fisherman lent them a wrench. “You remove mine, you buy me a beer,” he said. “You remove his, I buy you one.”

They started with every third. Bolts squealed like an admission that had been rehearsed. The first plate came free, heavy with the story it had been telling the sea. The hum along the wall thinned to a thread, then found another path and returned, smaller.

Troll pressed her ear to stone. The granite’s old breath was under there, waiting to be allowed. They pulled a second. The tone broke like a line finally understanding it was not connected to the sentence it had been in. The third was stubborn—salt had licked it into place—but Libaux has never been polite with stubborn things. He leaned, and the bolt learned humility.

The harbor changed in a way ordinary ears might not have awarded a verb to. The fishermen heard it. They paused their talk mid-lie and looked up, eyes narrowing as if they’d seen a small weather change in a lover’s mood. The sea sounded less haunted. It was still itself; it had been given back some of itself.

Luc sent Meuller the before/after. Meuller replied like a man who had found a nail in a map. Loop broken, tail shortened. You’ll need two more along the caves to collapse tranche 2. Banyuls tomorrow. Wear shoes. Don’t drown.

“Tell him we’ll wear shoes,” Libaux said.

A message from Helene Klausmun blinked the valley into the room: Two more fence posts pulled. The circle fell quiet before dawn. The dairy road sounds like a road again. Come back when you can. The chapel is keeping its silence for you. Troll read it twice and put it where good news goes when there’s work.

They walked the outer quay, listening for notes where there should have been tide. In one gap between stones, a small amplification chamber had formed, an accidental sermon box. Troll leaned into it and said her mother’s name. The chamber tried to return it and failed. The sound came back secular, generous.

“You’ve started telling rooms the truth,” Libaux said. “They’re telling it back.”

“Some of them,” she said.

At midnight a skiff nosed along the far side of the harbor with lights low and a cargo that wanted to be invisible. Aïcha pointed with her chin. “Our J,” she said. The crates wore the modest faces of furniture, stickered fragile in three languages.

“We should say hello,” Libaux said.

“We should let them think we cannot swim,” Aïcha said. “Tomorrow we’ll be at the caves when they are awake enough to fail.”

They stood where the breakwater remembers every storm and watched the skiff plot a sly line. The plates they had not pulled hummed their little ambition and were lost beneath slop and slap. The ones they had removed lay in a neat stack on the quay, silent as contrition.

When they left the water, Collioure went back to practicing sleep. Doors snicked. A cat performed a scale on a fence. A faulty shutter thudded once, remembered itself, and refused to do it again.

Troll’s phone lit with Uma Arouje: Your names appear in three unpleasant emails. Do what you came to do and do it cleanly. No speeches. A second from Mage Meuller: Banyuls: outer quay, cave mouth, and the tourist board’s sculptural nonsense that looks like a wave. They hid a transducer in the nonsense. Of course they did. A third from Miyake Coscia: The jar is waiting. The room is quiet. Take your time. Bring back no noise you don’t want to keep.

Back at the municipal office, Aïcha locked the file cabinet with a sound a room makes when it wants to believe in paper. Luc played them one more capture. In it, the tide’s breath walked down the quay and did not trip. The undernote was still there, but it had been reduced to a suggestion. The harbor had remembered its own vowel.

“Tomorrow,” Libaux said. He touched the stack of removed plates the way a man taps a sleeping animal to check if it plans to wake.

“Tomorrow,” Troll echoed. She rolled a marble across Luc’s desk—blue, seeded—and the tik it made was nothing but itself. No halo, no underfloor choir. Just glass telling the truth about wood. Collioure let that be enough, which for tonight it was.
