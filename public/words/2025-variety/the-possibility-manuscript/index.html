The Possibility Manuscript

Words / 2025 Variety

Part I: The Yellow Room

The storage unit's fluorescent bulb died the moment I stepped inside, leaving only the emergency exit sign to cast everything in a sickly yellow-to-red gradient. I'd inherited Unit 447 from my brother Marcus, dead three weeks from what the police called "death by misadventure"—their way of saying they couldn't explain the gunshot wound that matched no weapon they could find.

His notebooks lay scattered across the concrete floor, pages bleeding ink where a pipe had leaked. But the photographs survived, sealed in plastic: eight identical shots of our childhood bedroom fan, each from a slightly different angle. On the back of the last one, in his paranoid scrawl: "The pattern changes when no one's watching."

I am a forensic accountant. I find hidden money, trace laundered funds, follow paper trails to their criminal ends. But Marcus had left me something else—a different kind of trail, one that led through art galleries, psychiatric wards, and eventually to a manuscript that shouldn't exist.

The first clue wasn't in his photos but in what he'd circled in red marker on a gallery flyer: "Holy Possi!" An exhibition at an abandoned hospital, one night only, artist unknown.

Part II: The Gallery in Ward Seven

The Bethlehem State Hospital had been closed since 1987, but someone had strung extension cords through the broken windows, powering a single room where Marcus's work hung on water-damaged walls.

"You're the sister," said the woman running the illegal gallery. She wore a lab coat with someone else's name—Dr. Rosen—embroidered over the pocket. "He said you'd come."

The central piece stopped me cold: a riot of colors—yellow, orange, red, green, purple—with letters scattered throughout. When I stepped back, they spelled "POSSIBILITY," but when I moved closer, they reformed into "IMPOSSIBILITY." The letters shifted based on viewing angle, an optical trick Marcus had spent years perfecting.

"He was documenting something," Dr. Rosen said. "The space between what we see and what's actually there."

She led me deeper into the hospital, past rooms where Marcus had installed his work. The fan photographs, arranged in a grid on a surgical room wall. Abstract patterns—dark shapes against fiery backgrounds—in the morgue. And in what had been the secure ward, a collage that made my stomach turn: hands gripping gun magazines, buildings emerging from chaos, mechanical gears, stars.

"This was his last piece," she said. "Finished the night he died."

At the center of the collage, barely visible unless you knew to look: a fragment of medieval manuscript, creatures writhing in the margins.

Part III: The Archivist

Dr. Sarah Delacroix agreed to meet me at the Morgan Library. She specialized in medieval manuscripts, particularly their marginalia—the drawings monks added to sacred texts.

"Your brother contacted me six months ago," she said, pulling on white gloves. "He believed the marginalia contained coded messages about traumatic events the monks couldn't directly document."

She opened a 12th-century psalter. In the margins, fantastic creatures twisted through geometric patterns identical to Marcus's abstract paintings. The yellow-to-red gradient of the illuminated letters matched exactly the lighting in his final photographs.

"Look closer," she said, handing me a magnifying glass.

The creatures weren't random. They formed letters, barely visible: P-O-S-S-I-B-I-L-I-T-Y.

"The monks were documenting something," Sarah continued. "Events that hadn't happened yet. Your brother found seventeen manuscripts with the same hidden word, all created centuries apart, all by different hands."

"That's impossible."

"That's what I thought. Until I found this."

She showed me a photograph from 1892: asylum patients in a group therapy session. On the wall behind them, barely visible, the same pattern of letters. The photographer was Marcus Ashford. Our great-great-grandfather.

Part IV: The Fan Photographs

I spread Marcus's eight fan photographs across my apartment floor, looking for what he'd seen. Each image showed our childhood bedroom fan from a slightly different angle, the shadows forming different patterns on the wall.

3 AM. Coffee cold. Eyes burning. Then I saw it.

The shadow patterns weren't random. When arranged in the correct order, they formed a map—specifically, a floor plan of Bethlehem State Hospital. The fan blades' shadows marked locations: the gallery, the morgue, the secure ward. And one location I hadn't visited: the basement archives.

I drove back to the hospital as dawn broke, the sky shifting from black to deep purple to that familiar yellow-red gradient. The basement door had been sealed since the 1960s, according to the faded inspection notice. Marcus had broken the seal.

Inside: filing cabinets full of patient records. And in Cabinet 7, Drawer 4, a file with our family name.

Part V: The Inheritance Pattern

Patient Name: William Ashford
Admitted: 1953
Diagnosis: Prophetic Delusions
Note: Patient creates elaborate artworks claiming to document future events. Particular obsession with the word "possibility" which he insists will "unlock" in 2024.

William was our grandfather. The file contained his drawings—the same creatures from the medieval manuscripts, the same hidden letters, the same gradient patterns. And photographs of his cell walls, covered in calculations that hurt to look at, equations that seemed to bend space around them.

Dr. Rosen appeared beside me. I hadn't heard her come down.

"I was William's student," she said. "Not a doctor. He taught me to see the patterns before they committed him."

"You're ninety years old?"

"Time works differently when you can see all its possibilities at once." She pulled out a canvas bag. "Marcus understood this. The word isn't just 'possibility.' It's a key. The manuscripts, the photographs, the paintings—they're instructions for seeing through time's gradient."

She handed me a gun. Marcus's gun. The one the police never found.

"This killed him?"

"No. This saved him. He's not dead. He's in the gradient—the space between yellow and red, between possible and impossible. The gun is just how he got there."

Part VI: The Secure Ward Revelation

We returned to the secure ward where Marcus's collage hung. Dr. Rosen pointed to specific elements: the gun, the building, the gear, the star.

"Each symbol represents a choice point," she explained. "The gun: the moment of decision. The building: the structure of reality. The gear: the mechanism of time. The star: the navigation point between possibilities."

She had me stand exactly seventeen feet from the collage. At that distance, the scattered elements aligned into a three-dimensional image—not quite a room, not quite a door, but something between.

"Your brother discovered what the monks knew, what William knew, what our ancestor Marcus knew: reality has margins, just like manuscripts. And in those margins, other possibilities exist."

The collage began to pulse, the colors shifting through that familiar gradient. The letters spelling "possibility" emerged not from the artwork but from the air itself, floating between us and the wall.

"Touch them," Dr. Rosen said.

Part VII: The Gradient Space

My hand passed through the floating letters and I was elsewhere—not quite the hospital, not quite not the hospital. The walls breathed that yellow-red gradient. Marcus stood there, holding his camera, photographing something I couldn't see.

"You found it," he said without looking at me. "The family gift."

He showed me his viewfinder. Through it, I could see the hospital as it actually was: not abandoned but full of patients, all creating art, all documenting the same patterns. And not just from 1987—from every time period simultaneously. Medieval monks worked alongside Victorian asylum patients, contemporary artists, future beings I couldn't quite perceive.

"We're not crazy," Marcus said. "We're archivists. We document the possibilities before they collapse into reality."

He pointed to a monk illuminating a manuscript. The creature he drew would become, in 400 years, the shadow pattern cast by our bedroom fan. The fan's shadow would become, in our grandfather's equations, the formula for seeing through time. The formula would become, in Marcus's art, the doorway I'd just stepped through.

Part VIII: The Manuscript Margins

In the gradient space, Dr. Rosen showed me the complete manuscript—not a physical book but a living document written across time itself. Every margin note, every piece of art, every photograph was a page.

"Your family carries the sight," she explained. "Each generation sees one aspect. The first Marcus saw the letters. William saw the equations. Your Marcus saw how to step between. And you—"

"I see the connections. The audit trail."

Every seemingly random element suddenly revealed its purpose. The hot rhapsody of yellow-to-red: the visible spectrum of time. The fan photographs: a mechanical clock counting moments that haven't happened yet. The gun collage: the violent rupture needed to break through. The mirrored faces in brown and gray: all the selves that exist in parallel possibilities.

"But why 'possibility'?" I asked.

"Because that's what we're preserving," Marcus said. "Every possibility that doesn't become reality still exists here, in the margins. We're making sure nothing is lost."

Part IX: The Choice Architecture

Dr. Rosen handed me Marcus's camera. Through its viewfinder, I could see what he'd been photographing: not the gradient space itself but the moment of choice that created it.

Every person who'd ever stood at a crossroads appeared as a double exposure—one self taking each path. The hospital was full of these quantum ghosts, people who'd chosen differently, their unlived lives playing out in parallel.

"This is what drives people mad," Dr. Rosen said. "Seeing all the possibilities at once. That's why they called it a hospital. We're not treating mental illness. We're treating temporal overflow."

I photographed a monk choosing which creature to draw in a margin. In one possibility, he drew a griffin—in our timeline, that became the gear in Marcus's collage. In another, he drew a serpent—that became the spiral in our grandfather's equations.

"Every choice creates a new margin," Marcus explained. "And every margin needs documentation."

I understood then why he'd left me the storage unit. Not to solve his death but to continue the work.

Part X: The New Archivist

I returned to the physical world through the collage, landing hard on the hospital floor. Dr. Rosen was gone—she always had been, according to the records. She'd died in 1967, a patient who'd claimed she could teach people to see through time.

But her lessons remained, encoded in Marcus's work, in our grandfather's equations, in our ancestor's photographs, in the monks' marginalia.

I kept the storage unit but transformed it. The walls now hold the complete collection: every piece of evidence that possibility exceeds probability. The fan photographs form a clock that runs backward. The collage opens a door for those who know how to look. The manuscript pages reveal new creatures weekly, their messages shifting based on choices not yet made.

Students come, thinking they're here for forensic accounting lessons. I teach them to audit reality instead—to find the discrepancies where possibility leaked through, to trace the paper trail of unlived lives.

Last week, one of them—Jamal, whose grandmother painted the same patterns in refugee camps—brought me a photograph. His family, walking across a desert, casting impossible shadows. In the shadows: letters, scattered but readable. P-O-S-S-I-B-I-L-I-T-Y.

"My grandmother said the word would unlock in 2024," he said.

I showed him Marcus's final notebook, the one I'd found stuck to the storage unit floor. The last entry, dated the night he died: "The word isn't possibility. It's possibilities. Plural. All of them, at once."

Epilogue: The Living Manuscript

The hospital still stands, officially abandoned, actually the most populated building in the state. Every night, artists, accountants, archivists, and madmen slip through the collage doorway to document the margins.

We're writing the possibility manuscript—not the history of what happened but the archive of everything that could happen, did happen, will happen in the spaces between choice and consequence.

Marcus isn't dead. Death was just one possibility. In the gradient space, he continues his work, photographing the shadows cast by events that haven't occurred yet, documenting the creatures that live in the margins of reality, spelling out words that won't be invented for centuries.

The gun that killed him sits in my desk drawer. Also, it doesn't exist. Both states are true, depending on which angle you view from, like the letters in his "Holy Possi!" piece that spell different words based on perspective.

I've learned to read the hot rhapsody of transformation—the yellow-to-red gradient that appears at every moment of change. I've learned to see the grid of possibilities in every mechanical repetition. I've learned that violence and creation are sometimes the same gesture, that mirrors show not just reflection but multiplication, that in the smoky geometry between states, faces emerge that belong to no single timeline.

The monks knew. They hid it in their margins. Our ancestor knew. He hid it in his photographs. Our grandfather knew. He hid it in his equations. Marcus knew. He hid it in his art.

And now I know. I hide it in plain sight, in a storage unit that's also a portal, in accounts that audit reality, in a manuscript that writes itself across time.

The word "possibility" still shifts when I look at it, sometimes becoming "impossibility," sometimes "responsibility," sometimes "visibility." But that's the point. The word itself is marginal, existing in the space between meaning and meaninglessness, between sense and sensation.

Late at night, when the gradient light falls just right, I can see them all—every version of Marcus, every version of myself, every version of everyone who's ever lived—all creating the same endless document, all drawing the same impossible creatures, all spelling out the same shifting word.

We are the possibility manuscript. We are the margins where reality admits what it cannot contain. We are the archivists of everything that never happened, and therefore always is.
