The Sound of Belonging

← Words / 2025 Variety

Listening to the Harmony of Earth, Memory, and Ourselves

There was no silence left. Not a square inch.

The air hummed—not with the convenience of machinery or the scurry of modern life, but with the primordial music of a world alive. The haunting bark of unseen creatures echoed across rice paddies drenched in moonlight. Ants marched to their delicate rhythms, bees whispered in a language only the wind understood. And in the greenhouse world, wind was more than wind. It stirred the fern-like leaves, drawing ridges on translucent wings. It was a composer, an unseen hand coaxing symphonies from the living and the land.

"What is harmony without choice?" The thought pulsed through me, unbidden. "If the world hums on its own, are we its collaborators—or its audience?"

At 27, I was searching for the answer to a question I couldn’t articulate. It began with one of those path trips, an escape from the ceaseless chatter of cityscapes. A friend once accused me of running away, and she wasn’t wrong. But the noise of the city overwhelmed—horns, alarms, the endless buzz of a world demanding attention but offering none in return. In the greenhouse world, the noise was different. The land spoke louder than I had ever imagined, and for once, I didn’t need to respond.

"Let your ears guide you."

The ologist’s words seemed to hang in the air like mist. She was an unlikely companion, a flawed scientist whose own fractures mirrored mine. Her hands, sticky with palm oil, carried the residue of what had been burned, felled, replaced. She spoke of the earth not as a resource but as a memory.

The past folded into the present as she spoke.
"The land wasn’t always music," she said, her voice reverent. "It swarmed without song, alive but silent. Then came the shift. Life learned to cry out."

"Cry out for what?" I asked, though the answer seemed to linger between us.

"To be heard," she said simply.

Her stories made me ache in ways I couldn’t explain. Haunting barks rippling through soil. Winds bending reeds into instruments. Insects whispering under the weight of dusk. Each sound an answer to the same question: Where do I belong?

Her voice trembled: "Reciprocity wasn’t optional. It was instinct. To belong meant to give as much as you took."

The future folded into our steps. I could see it in her gaze—in the way her eyes flickered toward the carbon-heavy air, toward the cracked landscapes where fields had traded harmony for efficiency. Her stories of bellowing aloes, long before their voices were drowned in machinery, hung heavy in the mist.

"Maybe the question isn’t just belonging," I said suddenly. "Maybe it’s remembering. How much we’ve forgotten."

Elias joined us later. The wanderer. The craftsman. His calloused fingers were a map of labor, but he confessed he hadn’t built anything in years.

"I used to carve violins," he said, his voice heavy. "And then, I couldn’t anymore. My hands wouldn’t listen."

The ologist glanced at him, her gaze steady, unjudging. "Perhaps they weren’t listening because you forgot how to speak to them."

The ebb shifted into a moment of intensity.

We stood together as the fireflies wove their golden threads through the air, patterns both serene and chaotic. In the rice paddies, a storm began to churn. The serene landscape of whispering fields warped as the wind thickened, bending stalks and sending water into frantic ripples. The soft hum of the greenhouse rose, joined by the clash of nature against nature—chaos within harmony.

"Do you feel that?" Elias whispered, his voice unsteady now.

I nodded, the weight of it pressing against my ribs.

"The sound of belonging," the ologist said.

"Belonging to what?" Elias’s voice cracked.

"To everything. To now, before, and whatever comes after."

The humor slipped in—an unexpected respite.
Elias turned and chuckled dryly, "Well, if everything, why did it break my violins? The sound of belonging doesn’t seem to love me much."

"Maybe it’s waiting for you to listen," the ologist replied, with a pointed smile.

Humor faded back into heartbreak.
Elias stared at his hands, flexing his fingers as if testing whether they could trust him again. "Maybe I should’ve been better at listening before."

And then the cyclical weight of it struck me—a sound, a hum, a rhythm that began and ended with the same unspoken truth: we had always belonged to the land. It was we who had forgotten.

The fireflies danced, their patterns deliberate, unknowable. I thought of the years I spent running—from my mother’s sorrow, from my own.

"Perhaps belonging requires listening," I whispered. "Not fixing. Not claiming. Just listening."

The greenhouse hummed in response, a sound both ancient and new. Elias tilted his head back, watching the sky fold into shadows.

"I think," he said softly, "I need to try carving again."

The ologist touched the palm oil still clinging to her skin, her expression thoughtful. "We begin where we end. Creation, connection, remembering."

For the first time, the rhythm of the land seemed to settle—serenity balanced by the whispers of chaos, humor woven with heartbreak. Harmony without perfection. Belonging without answers.
