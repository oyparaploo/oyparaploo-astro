Everything Buried in Silence Still Glows…

Words / 2025 Variety

Everything Buried in Silence Still Glows ~ A Braided Essay on Breath, Memory, and Belonging

Part One: The Authority of Light

I died on a Tuesday at 3:47 PM, the exact moment London's air quality hit its worst recorded level. But death, I discovered, is less departure than translation. I am writing this from the space between breaths, the pause between heartbeats, the clearing where light bends sideways because straight lines are for the living.

My name was Amara Johnson. I died in the Bermondsey laundromat fire with my daughter Ruby, age seven. We are remembered now in photographs on a memorial wall, in the building's strange breathing, in the way dryer 17 still runs despite being broken.

But memory is not where we live. We live in the clearing—not a place but a frequency, where all the stories converge.

Part Two: The Clearing Assembles

The Living Witnesses

They arrive separately, drawn by different crises:

Chijioke comes first, exhausted from his "Innocent Flailing" performance, bruises purple as gospel truth. He seeks the clearing in Burgess Park because buildings overwhelm him with their remembered breath.

Skylar follows, her pink N95 mask unable to filter the weight of environmental grief. She's been tracking how pollution thins boundaries between worlds, makes visible what was always there.

Donny arrives third, the HVAC tech who fixed dryer 17 and swears it whispered his name. He's fifty-two, skeptical of everything except what his hands have touched.

Maya, six, wearing her unicorn-decorated inhaler like jewelry, leads her parents here because "the air tastes different where the light bends."

They don't know each other yet. But the clearing knows them.

Part Three: When Light Disobeys

The First Bending

At 3:47 PM—always 3:47—the light changes. Not gradually but grammatically, like a sentence rewriting itself mid-thought. It doesn't fall; it arrives sideways, at angles physics hasn't approved.

Chijioke sees it first: a shaft that paints the snow (there's always snow in the clearing, even in July) in colors without names. "That light has no source," he says.

Maya responds with six-year-old certainty: "It has every intention."

The shadows begin to move—not cast but self-generated, light's twin rather than consequence. They dance with the curiosity of stories waiting to be invited in.

The Puddle Window

A puddle of melted snow begins to hum—not with sound but with recognition. Its surface lifts, not the water but its reflection, forming a window suspended midair.

Through it, they see:
- A grandmother (mine) humming over akara batter in Lagos
- Chijioke at seven, flailing in his school play while his grandmother defends him
- Ruby (my daughter) learning to whistle between cupped hands
- A woman (me) on a rooftop, laughing at the moon the night before the fire

Skylar understands first: "The clearing is showing us what got buried in silence."

Part Four: The Characters Emerge

From Memory to Presence

The clearing manifests its regular inhabitants—not people but presences I've come to know:

Temi: A bird made of unfinished stories, her feathers dark as forgotten ink. She carries everyone's incomplete narratives.

Ijeoma: An ancient tree whose bark cracks with laughter older than language. She is Chijioke's grandmother, translated into root and ring.

Adebola: A beetle with a shell that reflects futures, offering monologues to puddles about the burden of prophecy.

Chinelo: Pure logic incarnate, always trying to solve the paradox of existing after existence ends.

They aren't metaphors. In the clearing, metaphor and reality have no boundary.

Part Five: The Performance Continues

Sacred Flailing in Sacred Space

Chijioke begins to move—his innocent flailing finding its true context. Here, graceless movement becomes prayer. His bruises glow faintly purple, evidence of every time he chose expression over safety.

Maya joins immediately, her inhaler bouncing rhythmically. "We're trying to fly without wings!" she announces.

Donny, surprising everyone including himself, starts moving too. "Haven't done this since I was four," he says between breaths.

Skylar documents with her phone, then stops. "This can't be captured. It has to be entered."

She flails.

Part Six: What the Dead Know

My Intervention

I want to tell them everything:
- That innocence isn't lost in death, only clarified
- That breath continues in frequencies they can't detect
- That every laundromat, every clearing, every space holds all its histories simultaneously

But the dead don't lecture. We whisper in the pause between snowflakes:

Silence is not absence. It's an invitation.

Chijioke hears it first—recognizes it from his grandmother's wisdom about stillness being movement without calculation.

Part Seven: The Integration

When Times Collapse

The clearing does what it always does at 4:13 (26 minutes after death-time): it folds temporal boundaries.

Suddenly, they see:
- The laundromat before the fire, Margaret Chen taking photos of regulars
- The warehouse where Chijioke performs, the Jesus Horse materializing
- London's future skyline, clean air achieved but at costs not yet imagined
- Ruby at twenty-seven, the age she would be, working as an atmospheric scientist

"Time isn't linear here," Skylar breathes. "It's crystalline. Every moment exists simultaneously."

Part Eight: The Science of Impossible Spaces

Emmett Arrives

Of course Emmett finds them—the performance artist who adapted to pollution, whose body registers impossible oxygen levels. He brings equipment: electromagnetic readers, infrared cameras, his evidence-costume of medical debris.

"The clearing is a convergence point," he explains. "Where enough trauma, joy, and transformation happen, space develops consciousness."

His machines go haywire. The readings show:
- Oxygen levels that shouldn't sustain life but do
- Electromagnetic signatures of people not present
- Temperature fluctuations that spell words in thermal imaging: BELONG

Part Nine: What Rises

The Full Manifestation

At 5:00 PM, the clearing reveals its purpose. Every person who died breathing bad air, every building that remembered too much, every moment of innocent movement despite danger—they all converge here.

The puddle windows multiply, showing:
- Samuel's grandmother in Accra, still dancing in her gas mask
- The Jesus Horse, perpetually transforming between states
- Every bruise from every performance, glowing like constellations
- The memorial wall expanding beyond the laundromat into infinite documentation

"We're not just in the clearing," Donny realizes. "We ARE the clearing."

Part Ten: The Teaching

What We Came to Learn

Ijeoma speaks (trees speak here): "Every breath you take contains every breath ever taken. Air doesn't die; it circulates. Memory doesn't end; it transforms."

Adebola adds, sardonic but sincere: "Belonging isn't earned. It's recognized."

Temi opens her wings, revealing stories written in languages not yet invented: "Everything unfinished continues elsewhere."

Chinelo concludes: "Logic fails because love succeeds."

Part Eleven: The Return

But Never the Same

They leave the clearing as the light unbends, returns to ordinary angles. But they carry it now—the frequency, the knowing.

Chijioke's next performance includes a moment of stillness where everyone in the warehouse feels the clearing open briefly, impossibly, in the space between heartbeats.

Skylar's environmental research shifts to studying "convergence points"—spaces where the boundary between air and memory dissolves.

Donny returns to fixing HVAC systems but leaves small offerings in the vents: photographs, whispered names, acknowledgment of what the buildings hold.

Maya draws it constantly: the clearing where light bends, where shadows dance independently, where her inhaler isn't necessary because the air itself breathes for you.

Part Twelve: What Persists

The Final Braid

I remain in the clearing with Ruby, with Margaret Chen, with everyone who transformed rather than simply ended. We are the pause between breaths, the space between heartbeats, the light that bends because straight lines are for those still becoming.

But here's the secret: everyone is still becoming. Death doesn't stop it. Buildings don't stop it. Toxic air doesn't stop it. We all continue, just in different frequencies.

The clearing appears whenever someone needs it:
- In laundromats at 3:47
- In warehouses during innocent flailing
- In the pause before Maya asks strangers their favorite color
- In the moment Donny hands someone a mask without being asked
- In the space where Skylar's data becomes prayer

Every Tuesday, they return to where they first found it—or it found them. The light still bends. The puddles still show windows. The shadows still dance independently.

And I whisper, we all whisper, the collective breath of everyone who discovered that belonging doesn't require bodies:

Everything you buried in silence is still glowing.

Oh, there you are.

Welcome.

You always belonged here.

---

The Clearing appears in Burgess Park every Tuesday at 3:47 PM for those who need to remember that breath is borrowed, belonging is birthright, and death is just another form of becoming. Bring your bruises, your masks, your unfinished stories. The light will bend for you.
