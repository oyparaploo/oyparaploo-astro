Brrd Roots ~ Threads of Resilience: A World Unraveling

Words / 2025 Variety

and the Quiet Strength to Rebuild

How Isolation, Fear, and Fragile Systems Shape Collapse
—and the Bridges That Carry Us Forward

The world wasn’t just fraying—it was unraveling, thread by thread, like a sweater caught on a nail. In Beijing, Minister Liang sat at his desk, staring at a report that outlined the latest tariffs imposed by the United States. The numbers were grim, and the paper felt rough under his fingertips, as though it resented being touched. He ran his thumb along the stamped insignia of the Ministry, its raised edges smooth and cold—a reminder of authority that felt more like a cruel joke. “We’re caught between a rock and a hard place,” he muttered, his voice barely audible over the hum of the air conditioning. “And it’s the people who will pay the price.”

Liang leaned back in his chair, which groaned in protest, as if it too was tired of holding everything together. He thought about his daughter, who had asked him the night before, “Papa, why does it feel like the world is breaking?” Her voice had been soft, almost afraid of the answer. Liang hadn’t known how to respond. How do you explain to a child that the weight of the world is an inheritance none of them had asked for, but all would carry?

Across the ocean, in Washington, D.C., the Oval Office echoed with President Trump’s booming voice. “They think they can beat us? They’re dreaming!” he shouted, slamming his hand onto the Resolute Desk. The polished wood gleamed under the harsh light, but the force of his gesture left a faint smudge, dulling its sheen. “If they want a trade war, then a trade war is what they’ll get! We’ll fight tooth and nail—and we’ll win!” His advisors exchanged uneasy glances, their silence speaking louder than words. One of them scribbled something on a notepad that looked suspiciously like *“Please stop yelling.”*

Back in Beijing, Liang stared out his office window. The smog blurred the skyline, turning the city into a watercolor painting left out in the rain. He thought about the real estate bubble that had loomed over them for years, the delicate balance that had been struck to prevent collapse. But now, it all seemed precarious again. Beyond the glass, the streets bustled with ordinary life—people carrying groceries, children trailing behind them—but Liang could see the cracks in this veneer. The weight wasn’t visible, but it was there.

In Xiangsheng, a village where time seemed to move differently, a farmer knelt on dry, cracked earth. His hands, calloused from years of work, trembled as he held the remnants of his harvest—a stalk turned brittle and brown under the relentless sun. He pressed it between his palms, the fragments falling like dust onto the dirt below. “Even the soil fights us now,” he said quietly, his voice carrying the weight of defeat.

And then, as if summoned by the collective anguish of the world, the Prophet appeared.

No one knew his name or where he came from. He moved like a shadow through Beijing’s corridors of power, Washington’s marble halls, and Xiangsheng’s dusty streets. He carried nothing but a crooked staff, its surface worn smooth by years of travel. His robe was marked by dust and ash, as though he had walked through lifetimes to reach this moment. His eyes, deep and unyielding, held the quiet wisdom of someone who had witnessed cycles of collapse and renewal.

In Beijing, the Prophet stood behind Liang, his shadow long across the dim light of the office. Liang turned, startled, his grip tightening around the crumpled report in his hands. “Do you wonder why the ground beneath us feels so unstable?” the Prophet asked, his voice steady, resonant.

“Who are you?” Liang asked, his words faltering. “How did you—”

“The ground cracks under the weight of isolation,” the Prophet continued, ignoring the question. “You build walls and call them strength. You impose tariffs and call them strategy. But these walls hold no people, only fear. And fear fractures all it touches.”

Liang stared at the man, his own doubts rising like smoke. “And what would you have us do? Tear it all down?”

“Tear down what divides,” the Prophet said simply. “And with your bare hands, build bridges where you once raised barriers.”

In Washington, the Prophet found Trump pacing the Oval Office, frustration emanating from every movement. “Why won’t they just back down? They’re stubborn, they’re reckless, they—”

“Because they do not trust the ground beneath your feet,” the Prophet interrupted, his tone unwavering.

Trump stopped mid-sentence. “What does that mean?” he asked, his voice sharp with indignation.

“It means that trust cannot grow where fear reigns,” the Prophet replied. “You cannot demand trust. It is earned in moments of vulnerability and shared understanding. It is never born from threats or the shaking of fists.”

In Xiangsheng, the Prophet stood beside the farmer as he stared at the barren earth, his hands trembling from years of effort with little return. “What do we do when the earth refuses to give?” the farmer asked, his voice breaking.

“You endure,” the Prophet said. “And in your endurance, you nurture the seeds of renewal. Even barren soil remembers the life it once carried.”

The bleakness deepened. In Beijing, the streets filled with murmurs of uncertainty. Liang walked through a warehouse filled with unsent goods, his fingers brushing against the cold steel of a shipping container. It was smooth, unyielding—a stark reminder of the barriers they had yet to overcome. Outside, animal carcasses—left to waste in a world misaligned—lay abandoned, their forms twisted and haunting. Liang turned his face away but felt their presence linger.

In Washington, protestors gathered outside the White House, their signs a mosaic of fury and exhaustion. One sign read, **“Promises Are Not Solutions,”** its letters scrawled in uneven strokes. Another said simply, **“We Are Breaking.”** Inside, Trump sat at the Resolute Desk, his hands gripping its edge tightly, as though holding onto it would steady the chaos around him.

And in Xiangsheng, the farmer stood in his fields after a storm had taken what little remained of his crop. He picked up a broken watering can, its rusted edges sharp against his palm, and carried it toward his home. But even in the loss, a neighbor appeared, carrying a sack of seeds. “It’s not much,” she said softly, “but it’s something. We’ll plant again—together.”

Amid the wreckage of the world, moments of hope began to emerge. Liang fed a stray dog outside the warehouse, watching as its tail wagged faintly. Trump’s advisors convinced him to focus on dialogue, on groundwork—a small step, but a step nonetheless. And in Xiangsheng, the farmer and his neighbor planted the new seeds side by side, their hands steady despite the uncertainty around them.

The world remained fragile, yet something stirred in its cracks. Somewhere, the Prophet walked on, his staff tapping against the earth like a heartbeat. He whispered to the wind: **“Even in the darkest moments, there is light. Walk toward it. And walk together.”**

Liang’s thoughts drifted to his daughter, who had asked him the night before, “Papa, if we’re so strong, why do you look so sad?” Her question lingered like smoke in the air, impossible to grasp but equally impossible to ignore. He hadn’t known how to answer. How could he explain to her that strength wasn’t always enough? That sometimes, even the strongest pillars cracked under the weight of the world?

Across the ocean, in Washington, D.C., the White House was a hive of activity. President Trump paced the Oval Office, his voice booming as he addressed his advisors. “They think they can outlast us?” he shouted, slamming his hand on the Resolute Desk. The polished wood gleamed under the harsh light, but the force of his gesture left a faint smudge. He ran his fingers along the edge, the smooth surface cool beneath his touch, but it did nothing to quell his frustration. “We’ll show them who’s in charge! If they want a fight, we’ll give them one!” His advisors exchanged uneasy glances, their silence a tacit acknowledgment of the chaos unfolding around them.

Back in Beijing, Liang stared out the window of his office. The smog blurred the skyline, turning the city into a ghostly silhouette. He thought about the statement China had released earlier that day: *We do not want a trade war, but if the United States insists, we will fight to the end.* The words echoed in his mind, a mix of defiance and resignation. “Our economy is resilient,” the statement had said. “We will endure.” But Liang knew the truth. The trade war would cause dislocations. It would exacerbate the economic downturn that had already gripped the country for years. The managed popping of the real estate bubble had been necessary, but it had left scars that were still healing.

In the rural village of Xiangsheng, a farmer stood in his fields, staring at the dry, cracked earth beneath his feet. His calloused hands held a wilted stalk, the leaves brittle and crumbling. He crushed it in his palm, the dry fragments falling to the ground like ashes. “Even the soil fights us,” he said quietly to no one, his voice heavy with defeat.

And then, as if summoned by the collective despair, the Prophet arrived.

No one knew his name or where he came from. He appeared in Beijing, Washington, and Xiangsheng, moving like a shadow through the corridors of power and the streets of ordinary people. He carried nothing but a crooked staff, its surface worn smooth by years of travel. His robe was dusted with something that might have been ash or breadcrumbs—it was hard to tell. His eyes, dark and steady, seemed to hold the weight of centuries, as though he had seen this all before.

In Beijing, he found Liang sitting alone in his office, the report crumpled in his hands. Liang’s fingers brushed against the paper’s rough edges, the texture a reminder of the tangible weight of decisions. “Do you wonder why the world feels like it’s unraveling?” the Prophet asked, his voice calm but unyielding.

Liang looked up, startled. “Who are you? How did you get in here?”

“The world unravels because you pull at its threads,” the Prophet continued, ignoring the question. “You build walls and call them strength. You impose tariffs and call them strategy. But walls do not protect. They isolate. And isolation is the beginning of collapse.”

Liang frowned, his hand tightening around the crumpled report. “And what would you have us do? Tear it all down?”

“No,” the Prophet said softly. “But remember that resilience is not the absence of pain. It is the ability to endure it. And endurance is not built on walls. It is built on bridges.”

In Washington, the Prophet found Trump pacing the Oval Office, his frustration palpable. “Why won’t they just back down?” Trump shouted, his voice echoing off the walls.

“Because they do not trust the ground beneath your feet,” the Prophet said, his tone steady.

Trump stopped, turning to face him. “What’s that supposed to mean?”

“It means that trust is not built in threats or tariffs. It is built in the quiet work of understanding. You cannot demand it. You must earn it.”

And in Xiangsheng, the Prophet stood beside the farmer, watching as he knelt in the dirt, his hands trembling as he tried to salvage what he could. The farmer brushed his fingers against the soil, the texture dry and grainy, a shadow of the fertile earth it had once been. “What do we do when the earth no longer gives?” the farmer asked, his voice breaking.

“You endure,” the Prophet said. “Not because it is easy, but because it is necessary. And in your endurance, you find the seeds of renewal.”

The bleakness deepened in the weeks that followed. In Beijing, a misstep in negotiations led to a temporary halt in trade, leaving warehouses filled with unsent goods. Liang walked through one of these warehouses, his hand brushing against the cold steel of a shipping container. The surface was smooth but unyielding, a stark reminder of the barriers they had yet to overcome. Outside, a stray dog scavenged among the debris, its ribs stark beneath its matted fur. Liang crouched down, extending a hand, but the dog flinched and ran. He stayed there for a moment, his hand hovering in the air, the weight of failure pressing down on him.

In Washington, Trump’s insistence on escalating the trade war led to protests outside the White House. The signs were a chaotic mix of demands and frustrations. One read, **“Tariffs Hurt Us All,”** its letters scrawled in uneven strokes. Another simply said, **“Enough.”**

And in Xiangsheng, the farmer watched as his crops withered under the relentless sun. He knelt in the dirt, his hands trembling as he tried to salvage what he could. The Prophet stood nearby, his shadow long and still.

Amid the darkness, moments of hope began to emerge. In Beijing, Liang returned to the warehouse with a piece of bread from his lunch. The stray dog approached cautiously, its tail wagging faintly. Liang smiled for the first time in weeks, the simple act of connection a balm against the weight of his responsibilities.

In Washington, Trump’s advisors convinced him to focus on groundwork, to build trust before demanding results. It was a small step, but it was a step nonetheless. And in Xiangsheng, the farmer planted new seeds, his hands steady despite the uncertainty that surrounded him.

The world remained fragile, the path uncertain. But somewhere, the Prophet walked on, his staff tapping against the ground with the rhythm of an ancient song. He whispered to the wind: **“Even in the darkest times, there is light. You must choose to walk toward it.”**

“You endure,” the Prophet said. “Not because it is easy, but because it is necessary. And in your endurance, you find the seeds of renewal.”

Amid the darkness, moments of hope began to emerge. In Beijing, Liang returned to the warehouse with a piece of bread from his lunch. The stray dog approached cautiously, its tail wagging faintly. Liang smiled for the first time in weeks, the simple act of connection a balm against the weight of his responsibilities.

In Washington, Trump’s advisors convinced him to focus on groundwork, to build trust before demanding results. It was a small step, but it was a step nonetheless. And in the rural village, the farmer planted new seeds, his hands steady despite the uncertainty that surrounded him.

The world remained fragile, the path uncertain. But somewhere, the Prophet walked on, his staff tapping against the ground with the rhythm of an ancient song. He whispered to the wind: **“Even in the darkest times, there is light. You must choose to walk toward it.”**
