Mother Tongues and Family Lines

The Exhalation I

I. Tauranga Moana

Aroha films her granddaughter making angels in the black sand, holding the phone sideways because she's sixty-three and never learned the vertical way, and honestly, this feels right—getting the whole frame, the child and the surf and the sky.

Maya wears the white communion dress. It's Tuesday. Regular Tuesday. But Maya announced at breakfast that the beach required the fancy dress, and who was Aroha to argue with that logic?

"Do another one, Nana. Film me doing another."

The sand receives Maya's small body—arms sweeping up, legs fanning out. Each angel slightly different, slightly the same. Maya exhales hard, and the breath hangs visible in the afternoon light.

"Did you get it? Did you see my soul?"

"I got it, love."

"Was it a good soul or a regular soul?"

Aroha pauses. "What's a regular soul?"

"Like Uncle Hemi's. He burps at dinner."

And despite everything—despite the weight she carries, despite what she knows is waiting down the coast road—Aroha laughs. "Yours is definitely good."

Later, after Maya's mother collects her (the exchange brief, careful, everyone performing civility), Aroha drives alone. She stops at the dairy. Hemi behind the counter asks how she's keeping.

"Good, good."

They both know it's only half true.

She's been cleaning houses since the council redundancy. Three days a week, her knees complaining. The other days she volunteers at the kohanga reo, watching children younger than Maya learn their language better than she ever did.

The field appears after the bend. Three years of restoration work—Ellen and the young ones pulling out gorse and broom. Now the kōwhai blooms, and the harakeke, and flowers whose names Aroha is still learning.

She pulls over. Gets out.

The field moves in the wind. Gold and purple and green.

But she makes herself look at the other spot too. Where the stones form outlines—human and animal shapes, arranged after the accident. Forty meters from where the blue SUV ended, every surface coated in dark residue.

The investigation revealed what investigations reveal: paranoia, ramming, train crossing. Two dead, one destroyed, one in prison. The man in the blue vehicle had been seventy-one. Māori. Retired teacher. Had his mokopuna in the back seat. A girl Maya's age.

Aroha kneels beside the stone outlines, presses her palms to the earth. It's warm. Almost body temperature. She closes her eyes and breathes out, expelling something nameless.

Ellen had told her once: "We're not just planting natives. We're apologizing."

Aroha had thought: to whom?

She doesn't know how long she kneels there. When she stands, her cream linen dress bears dark soil across the front. She doesn't brush it off.

Driving home, she passes the accident site three times before realizing she's circling. The episodes again. Lost time. She grips the wheel, forces herself toward town.

II. Manitoulin Island

Thomas Brennan has been staying at his sister's cottage on the island for four months. Indefinite leave from his insurance job in Thunder Bay. His wife said he needed "perspective." His therapist said he needed "space to process." His sister just said "come stay" and didn't ask questions.

He's fifty-nine. Divorced last year. Kids grown, scattered, occasionally texting.

Every morning he walks the beach, collecting stones. Smooth ones. Round ones. He arranges them in his sister's yard—no particular pattern, just placing them. His sister watches from the kitchen window and doesn't comment.

Thursday a little girl appears at the property line. Maybe six or seven. Watching him place stones.

"What are you making?"

"I don't know yet."

"That's a weird answer."

"I guess it is."

She comes closer. Studies his stone arrangement. "It looks like my mom's face cream."

Thomas looks at the stones. She's not wrong. "What's your name?"

"Penny. We're renting next door for the summer. Mom says you're having a difficult time."

"Did she."

"She said I shouldn't bother you. But you're outside, so."

Thomas almost laughs. "Fair point."

Penny sits on the grass, watches him place more stones. After a while she says: "My dad doesn't live with us anymore. Mom says he's on a journey."

"Is he?"

"I think he's in Mississauga."

This time Thomas does laugh. A real one. First in months.

Penny visits every morning after that. Doesn't help with the stones. Just sits. Talks. Tells him about her guinea pig (Sir Fluffington), her teacher (mean), her friend's pool (way better than the lake).

One morning she brings her mom. Woman in her thirties, apologetic smile.

"I'm Claire. I'm sorry if Penny's been bothering you."

"She hasn't."

"She says you're building something."

"I'm really not."

Claire looks at the stone arrangement. Hundreds of stones now, covering part of the yard. No pattern. No design. Just stones.

"It's nice," she says, which is kind of her.

After they leave, Thomas keeps placing stones. His sister comes out with coffee.

"You're talking to people now. That's good."

"Just a kid."

"Still. It's something."

Thomas doesn't respond. Places another stone.

His therapist calls later. Weekly check-in. Asks how he's doing.

"Fine."

"Really?"

"I'm placing stones on my sister's lawn. A six-year-old named Penny thinks I'm building something. Her guinea pig is named Sir Fluffington."

"And how does that feel?"

"Absurd. But okay. Absurd and okay."

The therapist makes a note. Thomas can hear the pen scratching. "That's good, Thomas. Absurd and okay is good."

III. Crossing

Aroha posts the video of Maya making angels. No caption. Just the video.

Her daughter texts: "Why are you posting that?"

"Just am."

"It's from months ago."

"So?"

Her daughter doesn't respond.

That night, Aroha scrolls through comments. Mostly people she knows. Nice things. Heart emojis. One comment from someone she doesn't recognize: "We do this too. Manitoulin Island, Canada. My daughter arranges stones. Says they're prayers."

Aroha clicks the profile. A woman named Claire. Photos of a lake, a child, a man arranging stones in a yard.

She doesn't message. Doesn't comment. Just looks.

Two people on different continents, doing things with their hands. Sand angels. Stone arrangements. Trying to make meaning from materials. Or not trying. Just doing.

IV. Thursday

Aroha takes Maya to the field. Ellen is there, planting with others.

Maya runs to where she planted last time. "Nana, look! It's bigger!"

The harakeke is bigger. Not much. But bigger.

They plant together. Aroha shows her how. Dig, place, pat down. Simple as that.

"Why do we plant here, Nana?"

"To bring back what was here before."

"Before what?"

"Before people took it away."

"Why'd they take it?"

"Lots of reasons. None of them good."

Maya considers this while digging. "Did people die here?"

Aroha's hands still in the soil. "Yes."

"That's sad. But at least there are flowers now."

At least there are flowers now. This accounting children do. Subtract lives, add blooms. The math doesn't work, but they say it anyway.

On Manitoulin Island, Thomas places stones with Penny watching.

"Are you done yet?" she asks.

"I don't think it's the kind of thing that gets done."

"That's annoying."

"Very."

"So why do it?"

Thomas looks at his stones. Hundreds of them now. His sister's entire yard. Covering the grass. No pattern. No purpose. Just stones.

"I genuinely don't know, Penny."

"Adults are weird."

"Yes. We are."

Penny's mom calls her for lunch. Before she goes, she says: "Can I add one?"

"A stone?"

"Yeah."

"Sure."

She picks up a stone—small, pink-ish, smooth—and places it carefully among the others. "There. Now it's ours."

After she leaves, Thomas looks at the pink stone among all his gray ones. Something about it makes him want to cry and laugh simultaneously. A child's stone in his field of grief.

He takes a photo. Posts it online. Caption: "Collaborative project. Artist: Penny (age 6)."

His ex-wife comments: "Glad you're doing something."

His son comments: "Weird but okay Dad."

His daughter comments: "Is that a face cream pattern?"

Thomas laughs alone in his sister's yard.

V. Angels and Stones

Aroha and Maya go to the beach after planting. Maya insists on the white dress.

"That dress is for special occasions."

"The beach IS special, Nana."

They've had this conversation before. Aroha suspects they'll have it many more times.

Maya makes angels. Aroha films. The dress fans out. The breath exhales visible.

"Did you get it?"

"I got it."

"Was my soul good this time?"

"Very good."

"Better than Uncle Hemi's?"

"Much better."

Maya giggles. Runs to the water. Aroha follows slower, her knees reminding her of age.

They stand at the water's edge as sunset paints everything gold.

"Nana, look." Maya points at Aroha's dress. "You have dirt on you."

The soil stain from kneeling. Still there. Permanent now probably.

"That's from the ground."

"Why were you kneeling?"

"Just was."

Maya kneels too. Right there in the sand. Presses her palms flat. "Like this?"

"Yes."

They kneel together as darkness comes. Two bodies. Two outlines in sand.

When they stand, Maya says: "I'm hungry."

Because that's how it works. Kneel for the dead. Stand for the living. Plant flowers. Make dinner. Film angels. Breathe.

On Manitoulin, Penny's mom knocks on Thomas's door.

"Penny wants to know if you'll come to dinner. Fair warning: she'll make you look at photos of Sir Fluffington."

Thomas hasn't been to anyone's house in months. Hasn't wanted to. But he says: "Okay."

Dinner is spaghetti and garlic bread. Penny does make him look at guinea pig photos. Seventy-three of them. All essentially identical.

"He's very photogenic," Thomas says.

"Right?!"

Claire apologizes. "She's obsessed."

"It's fine. I needed to see seventy-three photos of a guinea pig named Sir Fluffington. I didn't know that until now, but I did."

Penny beams.

After dinner, they sit on the porch. Penny inside watching TV. Claire says: "She really likes you."

"I really like her. She's absurd."

"She is that."

"In a good way."

"I know."

They sit in comfortable silence. Then Claire says: "I saw your stones. Online. Someone in New Zealand commented. Said they plant flowers where people died. In car crashes."

"Did they."

"Yeah. Said their granddaughter makes angels in black sand. Films them."

Thomas looks at his stone field. Claire's pink stone among the gray.

"We're all just doing things, aren't we? Planting. Arranging. Filming. Making it visible somehow."

"I guess so."

"Does it help?"

"I don't know. But I keep doing it."

"Yeah. Me too."

VI. What Remains

Aroha takes Maya home. Makes spaghetti. They eat with dirt under their fingernails.

Maya tells her about school, about a boy who ate paste, about how her teacher said they'd be learning about native plants.

"Nana, can we go back tomorrow?"

"To the beach?"

"Yeah. In the white dress. The good one."

"That dress is for—"

"Special occasions. I KNOW." Maya rolls her eyes. "The beach is special."

Aroha, who has lived sixty-three years and lost time and cleaned houses and planted flowers, says: "You're right. It is."

Later, Maya asleep in the spare room, Aroha stands outside. The harbor. The lights. The land that remembers.

Her phone buzzes. A notification. Someone else commented on her video. A woman in Canada. A photo of stones. A child. A message: "We do this too. Different materials. Same reason maybe."

Aroha doesn't respond. Just looks.

Two women. Different continents. Making things visible. Sand angels. Stone arrangements. Small ceremonies for losses too big to hold.

The dead stay dead. The trains keep running. The episodes keep coming.

But Maya sleeps in the next room. The field grows. The kōwhai blooms.

And tomorrow they'll go to the beach, and Maya will wear the white dress, and Aroha will film her making angels, and they'll both exhale into the salt air.

On Manitoulin, Thomas places one more stone before bed. Penny's pink one catches the moonlight.

In the morning, he'll place more. Penny will watch. Maybe add another. Claire will bring coffee. His sister will shake her head fondly from the window.

It doesn't fix anything. But it's what they have.

These tender accountings. These small resurrections. These exhalations of souls.

It's enough.
