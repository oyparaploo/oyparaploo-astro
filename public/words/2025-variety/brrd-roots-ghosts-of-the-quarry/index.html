Ghosts of the Quarry

Tales of Chaos,

← Words / 2025 Variety

Absurdity, and Relentless Humanity

[ How Systems Grind People Down and Why We Endure Through Defiance, Laughter, and Connection ]

The announcement arrived with all the elegance of a goose careening into traffic. China had decided, in its infinite wisdom, to cut off exports of critical minerals—rare earths, those vaguely magical elements that quietly powered the gears of progress. Phones, cars, weapons, even the fancy electric toothbrush Jessica had once impulsively bought—they were all tied to these minerals, and now the world was running on empty. Cue the chaos. It was as if someone had unplugged modernity while everyone was still mid-sentence.

In Baotou, Li Wei stood at the lip of the quarry, staring into its vast, unfeeling expanse. The machines groaned and rumbled around him, oblivious to the drama their output—or lack thereof—was causing. Wei looked like he’d been sculpted from the same earth he mined: solid, worn, a little jagged. His father had worked here too, until the day he’d dropped in the middle of his shift, a victim of heart failure and a lifetime of labor that left no space for rest. The company had sent flowers in a vase so cheap it practically shattered if you breathed near it. “We value your sacrifice,” the note had said, which was hilarious given the lack of any actual sacrifice on their part.

Wei glanced down at his hands, calloused and cracked. “The earth doesn’t care about us,” he muttered to himself. He wanted to laugh, but the sound got stuck somewhere between his chest and his throat. It wasn’t even the earth that was indifferent; it was the systems—the machines, the quotas, the drones. Yes, there were drones now. They buzzed overhead, tracking bathroom breaks as though human dignity were some sort of optional feature.

Meanwhile, halfway around the globe in Ohio, Jessica was locked in a staring contest with the half-assembled battery on her workbench. It looked like the aftermath of a particularly contentious science fair—the wires tangled like spaghetti, the faint smell of burnt plastic wafting ominously into the air. Lithium, cobalt, all the rare stuff—they were in critically short supply, and Jessica was supposed to salvage what she could. “You’re a hero,” her supervisor had told her earlier that week. “Keep it up, Jess.” He had then handed her a pen with the company’s logo on it, as though stationery were an adequate substitute for global stability.

Jessica rubbed her temples and sighed. This battery was not just a physical problem; it was existential. Her brother had worked with her in the same garage, cracking terrible dad jokes as they built prototypes together. He had believed in clean energy, in progress, in the idea that people could actually make the world better. Until, of course, he didn’t. The layoffs had come, his hope had shriveled, and eventually, he was gone. Now Jessica stood in the wreckage, surrounded by wires and memories, wondering if anything she did would ever make a difference.

The world around them wasn’t just breaking—it was unraveling. In Baotou, Wei’s coworker, Chen, had taken to wearing his GPS tracker on his hat as an act of rebellion. It was oddly stylish, though the drones didn’t appreciate the aesthetic. When they finally found him wandering around the quarry, he was slapped with two weeks’ suspension. Chen seemed thrilled about it. “Guess I finally get some time off,” he joked to Wei, who tried—and failed—not to laugh.

In Ohio, Jessica’s factory was undergoing its own breakdown. Corporate headquarters had sent out inflatable balloon animals shaped like lithium ions, accompanied by a memo titled, “Resilience Is Fun!” Predictably, the balloons were stabbed, deflated, and repurposed as hats within hours. One manager attempted to hold a meeting while wearing three balloon ions on his head. Jessica watched the spectacle in silence, debating whether it was more absurd than tragic. She landed on both.

And yet, even in this carnival of absurdity, there were sparks of humanity—small, fleeting, but stubbornly persistent. One night, Wei and his coworkers gathered by the edge of the quarry, passing around a bottle of cheap baijiu. The sun set behind them, smearing the sky red, and for a moment, it felt as though the earth itself was bleeding. “My dad used to say the earth gives and takes,” Wei said, staring into the darkness. “But mostly, it just takes.” The others murmured their agreement, too tired to add more but too human to stay silent.

Jessica found her tether in her daughter, Emma. One evening, she walked into the living room and saw Emma wrestling with duct tape and a broken flashlight. “Emma, what are you doing?” Jessica asked, her voice hovering somewhere between exasperation and disbelief. Emma didn’t even look up. “I’m fixing it,” she replied simply. “We need it, right?” Jessica knelt beside her, pulling her into a hug, her own exhaustion momentarily giving way to something softer. “Yeah,” she whispered. “We do.”

Through it all, the contrasts were impossible to ignore. The systems were ruthless, grinding people into dust, yet humanity persisted—in laughter, in resilience, even in rebellion. At the quarry, Wei planted a single flower, its delicate petals defiantly bright against the barren stone. Jessica’s team began cobbling together batteries out of junk parts, which they nicknamed “Franken-cells.” Against all odds, some of them actually worked.

And somewhere, in the absurd wreckage of their world, Wei and Jessica clung to the same stubborn truth: We endure, not because we’re unbroken, but because we refuse to stop trying.
