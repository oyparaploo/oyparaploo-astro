Brrd Roots ~ The Prophet’s Whisper:

Words / 2025 Variety

Navigating Chaos, Connection, and the Fragility of Power

How Flawed Systems, Human Struggles,
and Quiet Acts of Hope Shape a World on the Brink

The air in Beijing was thick, like it had decided to sit on everyone’s chest just to see how long they could hold their breath. Minister Liang sat at his desk, staring at a report that outlined China’s reduced reliance on U.S. financial services and consulting firms. The paper felt rough under his fingertips, its edges curling slightly from the humidity. He ran his thumb along the corner, the texture grounding him in the moment. “We’re going to hurt ourselves,” he muttered, his voice barely audible over the hum of the air conditioning. “But in the long run, it’s the only way.”

He leaned back in his chair, which let out a groan of protest, as if it too was tired of holding everything together. Liang rubbed his temples, his fingers pressing into the tension that had taken up permanent residence there. He thought about his daughter, who had asked him the night before, “Papa, why do you always look so sad?” He hadn’t known how to answer. How do you explain to a child that the weight of the world feels like it’s been stapled to your shoulders?

Across the ocean, in Washington, D.C., the White House buzzed with activity. President Trump paced the Oval Office, his voice booming as he spoke to his advisors. “I want negotiations at the top!” he shouted, slamming his hand on the Resolute Desk. The polished wood was cool and smooth beneath his palm, but the force of his gesture left a faint smudge. “Xi Jinping and me—face to face. That’s how deals get done!” His advisors exchanged uneasy glances, their expressions a mix of “Please don’t yell at me” and “This is a terrible idea.” They knew the Chinese system didn’t work that way. Deals in China were meticulously crafted by officials, layer by layer, before they ever reached Xi Jinping’s desk. A summit between the two leaders without groundwork? It was like trying to build a skyscraper on quicksand.

Back in Beijing, Liang stared out the window of his office. The smog blurred the skyline, turning the city into a watercolor painting left out in the rain. He thought about the fiasco with President Volodymyr Zelenskyy of Ukraine—the chaos, the lack of decorum. “We can’t risk Xi Jinping being treated the same way,” Liang said to himself, his voice firm but tinged with worry. “Not when the stakes are this high.”

And then, as if summoned by the collective uncertainty, the Prophet arrived.

No one knew his name or where he came from. He appeared in Beijing, Washington, and Kyiv, moving like a shadow through the corridors of power and the streets of ordinary people. He carried nothing but a crooked staff, its surface worn smooth by years of travel. His robe was dusted with something that might have been ash or breadcrumbs—it was hard to tell. His eyes, dark and steady, seemed to hold the weight of centuries, as though he had seen this all before.

In Beijing, he found Liang sitting alone in his office, the report crumpled in his hands. Liang’s fingers brushed against the paper’s rough edges, the texture a reminder of the tangible weight of decisions. “Do you wonder why the tunnel feels endless?” the Prophet asked, his voice calm but unyielding.

Liang looked up, startled. “Who are you? How did you get in here?”

“The tunnel is long because you built it that way,” the Prophet continued, ignoring the question. “You filled it with layers and walls, trusting that complexity would protect you. But complexity is not strength. It is a maze.”

Liang frowned, his hand tightening around the crumpled report. “And what would you have us do? Tear it all down?”

“No,” the Prophet said softly. “But remember that even the longest tunnel has an exit. You must decide whether you will walk toward it or keep building walls.”

In Washington, the Prophet found Trump pacing the Oval Office, his frustration palpable. “Why won’t they just agree to meet?” Trump shouted, his voice echoing off the walls.

“Because they do not trust the ground beneath your feet,” the Prophet said, his tone steady.

Trump stopped, turning to face him. “What’s that supposed to mean?”

“It means that trust is not built in summits or speeches. It is built in the quiet work of understanding. You cannot demand it. You must earn it.”

And in Kyiv, the Prophet stood in the shadow of the presidential palace, watching as Zelenskyy addressed his advisors. “The world is volatile,” Zelenskyy said, his voice weary. “Nothing is decided.”

The Prophet stepped forward, his staff tapping against the cobblestones. The sound was rhythmic, almost soothing, as though it carried the wisdom of the earth itself. “Volatility is the language of change,” he said. “It speaks when the old ways no longer hold. But change is not chaos. It is a choice.”

The bleakness deepened in the weeks that followed. In Beijing, a misstep in negotiations led to a temporary halt in trade, leaving warehouses filled with unsent goods. Liang walked through one of these warehouses, his hand brushing against the cold steel of a shipping container. The surface was smooth but unyielding, a stark reminder of the barriers they had yet to overcome. Outside, a stray dog scavenged among the debris, its ribs stark beneath its matted fur. Liang crouched down, extending a hand, but the dog flinched and ran. He stayed there for a moment, his hand hovering in the air, the weight of failure pressing down on him.

In Washington, Trump’s insistence on a summit without preparation led to a public rebuke from Chinese officials. The headlines were brutal, the criticism sharp. Trump slammed his fist on the Resolute Desk again, the sound echoing like a thunderclap. “We’ll fix this,” he growled, but even he seemed unsure of how. Outside the White House, protestors gathered, their signs a chaotic mix of demands and frustrations. One sign read, **“Promises Are Not Policy,”** its letters scrawled in uneven strokes.

And in Kyiv, Zelenskyy faced protests from citizens demanding clarity in a world that offered none. He stood at a podium, gripping its edges so tightly his knuckles turned white. The wood was worn smooth from years of use, its surface a silent witness to countless speeches. “We will find a way,” he said, his voice steady despite the chaos around him. But as he stepped away, he caught sight of a child in the crowd holding a tattered teddy bear, its seams bursting. The sight stopped him in his tracks, a reminder of what was truly at stake.

Amid the darkness, moments of hope began to emerge. In Beijing, Liang returned to the warehouse with a piece of bread from his lunch. The stray dog approached cautiously, its tail wagging faintly. Liang smiled for the first time in weeks, the simple act of connection a balm against the weight of his responsibilities.

In Washington, Trump’s advisors convinced him to focus on groundwork, to build trust before demanding results. It was a small step, but it was a step nonetheless. And in Kyiv, Zelenskyy met with community leaders, their hands clasped in solidarity as they vowed to rebuild trust from the ground up.

The tunnel remained long, the path uncertain. But somewhere, the Prophet walked on, his staff tapping against the ground with the rhythm of an ancient song. He whispered to the wind: **“Even in the darkest tunnel, there is light. You must choose to walk toward it.”**
