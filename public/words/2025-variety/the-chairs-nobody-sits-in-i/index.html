The Chairs Nobody Sits In

← Words / 2025 Variety

I. Tahlequah

Mae's grandmother left her seventeen wooden chairs and a note that said: Don't let anyone sit on these. They're busy.

Mae is thirty-six. Works at the Cherokee Heritage Center doing tours for school groups and tourists who want to hear about Trail of Tears and pottery and how "spiritual" Native people are. Eleven years of this. She's tired.

The chairs are in her living room now. Against every wall, draped with her grandmother's shawls—the fancy ones with beadwork and fringe from powwows. One chair has safety pins all over it, hundreds of them. Her grandmother was seventy-three and never heard of punk but it looks exactly like some DIY thing.

"Why safety pins?" Mae asked her once.

"Because I had them."

That was five years ago. Her grandmother died three years ago. The chairs have been here since.

Derek keeps trying to sit on them. Not to be a dick. Just automatically.

"Don't," Mae says.

"Why not?"

"Because you can't."

"They're chairs, Mae."

"Not those ones."

This fight happens twice a week minimum. He thinks she's being stubborn. She probably is. But every time someone moves toward one of these chairs she feels something protective rise up.

The one with safety pins is her favorite. Her grandmother spent months on it, pinning each one individually. Flowers, stars, geometric patterns. From far away it looks celebratory. Up close it looks like labor.

Derek says: "Your grandmother was weird."

"I know."

"And now you're being weird about her weird chairs."

"Yeah."

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

He's still here. They're still fighting about it. Last week he moved the safety pin chair six inches to vacuum. Mae moved it back. He moved it again. She moved it back. They didn't speak for two days.

Her friend Carla came over, saw the living room. "Jesus, Mae."

"I know."

"You can't even sit down."

"I sit on the floor."

"That's—"

"I know."

Carla tried to ask more but Mae didn't have answers. Just has seventeen chairs taking up every available space. Her grandmother left them and said they're busy and Mae doesn't know what that means but she's keeping them anyway.

At work she does her tours. Same script about pottery techniques, about removal, about resilience. A kid asks if she lives in a teepee. She says no. A woman asks her to say something in Cherokee for a video. She says a few words. They applaud like she's done a trick.

She goes home to seventeen chairs that don't have to perform anything.

Derek says: "We need to talk about the chairs."

"Okay."

"They're taking over."

"I know."

"So?"

"So nothing. They stay."

"For how long?"

"I don't know, Derek. Until I figure it out."

"Figure what out?"

She doesn't answer because she doesn't know.

II. Wayanad

Suma's father left her eight chairs and a workshop full of wood scraps. No note. Just the chairs, lined up in the front room, each covered in embroidery and shells and bits of mirror.

Suma is forty-one. Works as a guide at Wayanad Wildlife Sanctuary. Shows tourists elephants, tells them about medicinal plants, about tribal customs. They ask if she lives in the forest. If she "talks to animals." If she knows the "old ways."

She's Adivasi. Indigenous. But they treat her like an exhibit.

Her father was a carpenter. Made furniture for government offices, temples, anyone paying. His last ten years he stopped making useful things. Started making chairs you couldn't sit on.

"Why?" she asked once.

"They're already sitting on us."

She didn't understand then. Thinks she does now.

The chairs are strange. One is covered in cowrie shells and glass beads, looks festive. But you can't sit—everything would break. Another has fabric draped over it like a waiting person. The one by the door is wrapped entirely in red thread. Took him a year. He died two months after finishing.

Her cousin Arun thinks she's crazy. "Sell them. Get good money."

"No."

"They're just taking up space."

"That's the point."

"That doesn't make sense, Suma."

"I know."

He visits from Bangalore, complains about the chairs blocking the walkway, about how she could have a proper living room if she'd just get rid of them. She nods, doesn't move them.

Her niece asks: "Aunty, why can't we sit?"

"Because they're not for sitting."

"Then what are they for?"

"I don't know. Being here."

Her niece looks confused. So does everyone.

At work the tourists photograph everything. The elephants, the trees, her in her guide uniform. One man asks her to "pose traditionally." She stands there. He takes the photo. Says thank you like she's done him a favor.

She goes home to eight chairs that don't pose for anyone.

Her sister calls from Chennai. "Arun says you're being stubborn about Father's chairs."

"They're staying."

"But why?"

"Because he made them to stay."

"That's not—"

"I know what it's not. They're still staying."

Her sister sighs. Changes the subject.

Suma sits on the floor in her front room, surrounded by her father's chairs. Doesn't know what to do with them. Can't sell. Can't use. Can't explain.

Just keeps them.

III. Performance

Mae does five tours Thursday. On the third one, a man interrupts her pottery demonstration to ask if she's "full-blooded."

She finishes the demonstration. Answers questions. Smiles when they take photos.

Goes home. Derek's made dinner. They eat in silence on the floor because the table is covered in papers and the chairs can't be moved and they're both tired of fighting about it.

"Your supervisor called," he says. "About your vacation days."

"Okay."

"She says you have seventeen unused days."

"Seventeen?"

"Yeah."

Mae looks at the chairs. Seventeen chairs. Seventeen days. Her grandmother had seventeen children but only three survived. Seventeen is important somehow but Mae doesn't know how.

Derek follows her gaze. "It's just a coincidence."

"Maybe."

"They're just chairs, Mae."

"I know."

"Do you?"

She doesn't answer.

That night she dreams about her grandmother pinning safety pins one by one, for months, making patterns. In the dream Mae asks why. Her grandmother doesn't answer, just keeps pinning.

She wakes up. The chairs are still there. Still draped, beaded, pinned. Still taking up the room. Still refusing to explain themselves.

In Wayanad, Suma does her morning tour. A woman asks if tribal people still "live in harmony with nature." Suma says yes because that's the answer they want. The woman nods, satisfied, takes a photo of Suma with the forest behind her.

Suma goes home. Her sister has called again. Left a message about "being reasonable" and "moving forward" and "letting go."

Suma deletes it.

Sits on the floor. Looks at the chair wrapped in red thread. Her father spent a year on it. A whole year wrapping thread in silence. She watched him do it but never asked why. Figured he'd tell her when he was ready.

He never did. Just finished it and died and left her eight chairs she doesn't understand.

She doesn't know if she's supposed to keep them forever or if there's some point where she's allowed to let them go. Doesn't know if her father would be angry or relieved if she sold them.

Just knows she hasn't yet.

IV. Still There

Mae's been living with the chairs for three years. Derek's been complaining for three years. Nothing's changed except they fight about it less now. Just accepted it. The chairs stay, they sit on the floor, life continues.

Carla stopped asking about them. Just brings a cushion now when she visits.

Nancy at work stopped asking too. Marked Mae down as "eccentric" probably. Doesn't matter.

The tourists still ask their questions. Mae still answers. Still does the performance, explains the pottery, smiles for photos. Then goes home to seventeen chairs that don't perform anything.

Last week a chair fell over. Just tipped, the shawl caught on something. Mae set it back up, adjusted the beadwork. Noticed how worn it's getting. The fabric fraying. The safety pins starting to rust in places.

They won't last forever. Eventually they'll fall apart. Eventually she'll have to decide what to do with them—repair them, or let them go.

Not yet though.

Derek asks less now about when she'll get rid of them. Maybe he knows the answer. Maybe there isn't one.

They're just there. Taking up space. Being.

In Wayanad, Suma's been living with her father's chairs for five years. Arun stopped complaining after year three. Just steps around them now when he visits.

Her niece is older, doesn't ask about sitting anymore. Just accepts that some chairs are for sitting and some aren't.

The tourists still ask their questions. Still want authenticity, tradition, performance. Suma gives them what they want, then goes home to eight chairs that don't give anyone anything.

Last month the thread on one chair started unraveling. Just a little, where her father knotted it. Suma doesn't know how to fix it. Doesn't know if she should.

Not yet though.

Her sister asks less about moving forward, letting go. Maybe she understands now. Maybe not.

The chairs are still there either way. Covered, wrapped, draped.

Not doing anything. Not meaning anything specific. Not explaining themselves.

Just there.

In Tahlequah and Wayanad, in living rooms full of chairs nobody sits on, two women exist alongside their inherited furniture.

Not understanding it. Not resolved about it. Just living with it.

Which is all there is, really.

The chairs take up space. The women sit on floors. Life continues around the refusal to be useful, around the insistence on being without purpose.

And that's it.

No connection. No revelation. No neat conclusion.

Just chairs.

Still there.
