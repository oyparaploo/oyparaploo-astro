Brrd Roots ~ Innocence ~ A Clearing Beneath Light That Wandered Instead of Falling Straight

The snowfall had spoken—gently, persistently—but it was the light that arrived like a sentence rewritten in a brighter tongue.

It didn’t descend like typical sunlight. It didn’t stream through gaps or trickle in soft pastels. No, this light sliced through the clouds as if they’d been holding secrets too long. It arrived without warning, without context—a sideways gleam from an unknown sky, bending at angles that logic hadn’t pre-approved.

Temi saw it first, a shaft of brilliance that painted the snow near Ijeoma’s trunk in colors she couldn’t name. It wasn’t gold. It wasn’t silver. It was like… memory made visible.

She blinked. “That light has no source.”

“And yet,” Ijeoma murmured, “it has every intention.”

The shadows, previously innocent and wistful, began to dance. Not wildly, but with curiosity—as if rehearsing for a story they hadn’t been invited to until now.

Even Adebola tilted her polished head upward, tiny sparks reflecting off her shell in fractal shards. “This wasn’t scheduled,” she muttered.

Temi laughed. “Nothing real ever is.”

More beams followed, slicing through the grey above as though the world had been layered in sleep—and someone, somewhere, decided to gently pull back the covers.

One ray landed directly on a small puddle formed by melting snow. The puddle, until now anonymous, began to shimmer with a soft humming sound, like the clearing was remembering its own favorite lullaby. Then it rose—not the water, but the reflection. It lifted out of the puddle slowly, calmly, forming not a mirror but a window suspended in midair, showing scenes from other clearings, other times: a grandmother humming over akara batter; a child learning to whistle between cupped hands; a woman alone on a rooftop, laughing at the moon.

Chinelo approached the shimmering window. “This is not logical,” she said. “Which is perhaps why it is flawless.”

The others drew near—not crowding, but gathering. The way humans lean into a campfire even when they’re not cold.

From above—yes, from above—a new sound descended. It wasn’t music, but it remembered music fondly. It was not language, yet every ear recognized it. It said: Everything you buried in silence is still glowing.

Temi suddenly remembered the story she never finished writing.

Ijeoma’s bark cracked softly with the rhythm of an old name she hadn’t heard in decades.

Adebola sighed. “I’ll allow one metaphor. Just one. This light feels like forgiveness with excellent posture.”

And then came the shadows again—but brighter now. No longer cast, they seemed self-made—light’s twin, not its consequence. They wrapped around each creature like tailored understanding. As if saying, Yes, I see who you tried to be. And I like who you became instead.

Then the clearing lifted. Not physically—but perceptually. The snow no longer carried weight, but buoyancy. Words like yearning and waiting began to dissolve. No one noticed they were warmer. No one mentioned the frost fading from Ijeoma’s branches. They were too full of... belonging.

Someone giggled. It might have been the light itself. Or maybe a leaf that remembered spring ahead of schedule.

Eventually, the sky began to reseal, not with clouds but with understanding.

The beams retracted—not rudely, not completely. Just enough to let mystery tuck itself back in. But the shimmer lingered in the puddles. The snowflakes glittered like punctuation marks catching a second language. And the clearing, once quiet, now hummed—not with certainty, but with presence.

Adebola repositioned herself. “Well. I suppose I’m emotionally compromised.”

“Good,” said Chinelo.

Temi closed her eyes and whispered, “Now even the silence glows.”

And Ijeoma, old and rooted and deeply amused, felt the laughter of the stars ripple down through her bark—not loud, but knowing.

Above them, unseen, something enormous and gentle exhaled.

And so the clearing remained: a poem mid-stanza, a cathedral made of roots and snowlight, gently overturned and perfectly upside down.

There was no loud parting, no great cinematic goodbye.

It was more like forgetting to lock the door behind you. Or stepping into a quiet room and realizing you’ve been there all along. That's how she arrived here—not above, not beyond, but quietly interwoven with everything she'd once touched. The moment didn’t announce itself. It unfolded.

And now, in the softness of whatever existed beyond the boundary of breath, she watched.

She saw the clearing again, just as it had been—not as a memory but as a presence. The snow fell in asymmetrical spirals, never in straight lines. There were no corners to it, no edges. Just rhythm. A slow hush carrying the echo of something worth listening to.

She used to look for borders. Points of entry. Certainty wrapped in structure. But here—where nothing demanded arrival or departure—she began to understand that stillness was not stagnation. Stillness was revelation, offered quietly.

Her gaze drifted to them, those still held in the clearing's tender now.

Temi, fluttering her feathers just so when possibility stirred too loudly to ignore. Ijeoma, rooted in laughter older than language—her voice the sound of dusk making peace with the day. Adebola, ever-restless, offering unfiltered monologues to inanimate puddles as though each reflection owed her a debate. And Chinelo, folding logic into contradiction with the finesse of one who’d long learned the difference between understanding and knowing.

They were still becoming.

That truth unspooled itself inside her gently. Becoming doesn’t end. It doesn’t resolve, or tie itself up neatly in bows. It expands. Unfinished was not a flaw. It was evidence of breath.

She longed to reach out—not to interrupt, not to correct, but to offer something soft and true. And so she did, in the only way that this place allowed: she whispered into the pause between snowfall.

Silence is not absence, she said. It’s an invitation.

She imagined them pausing, perhaps unknowingly, when a branch creaked with no wind, or when their shadows stretched longer than the light should have allowed. She imagined one of them feeling a brush of warmth in the middle of winter, and for no reason at all, exhaling.

She had never been far. Not truly.

Sometimes shadows near their feet carried her shape—not sharply, not overtly, but like a gesture forgotten midair. She hoped they wouldn’t chase those echoes. She hoped instead they’d sit beside them. Because those silhouettes weren’t about her. They were remnants of who they had been, who they had loved, who they had almost become and could still reclaim.

And when—because it always came eventually—the sky tilted again and light wandered, unguided and uncertain through the trees, she hoped they would remember.

Remember that light is not always obedient.

Remember that beauty doesn’t require symmetry.

And above all, remember that belonging is never something one earns. Belonging, real belonging, doesn’t wear credentials. It isn’t granted.

It simply opens its arms and says:

Oh. There you are.

The first sign wasn't the cold. It was the light.

The snow had spoken already—quietly, insistently, in that way snow does when it wants to be listened to rather than noticed. But the light… the light was another matter. It didn’t descend gently, didn’t ease its way through the canopy or brush across frost-bitten branches. It arrived all at once, slicing through the sky with such clarity it felt like punctuation—something final and inevitable in a sentence nobody remembered starting.

Temi was the first to see it.

She had perched herself midway up Ijeoma’s trunk, her feathers dark as forgotten ink and just as ready to write something down. A shaft of brilliance, unfamiliar and impossible, struck the snow nearby—radiant, disobedient, almost conscious.

The colors it revealed weren’t ones Temi had words for. Not gold. Not silver. It was more like... recollection catching fire.

“That light has no source,” she whispered.

“And yet,” Ijeoma answered from below, “it has every intention.”

By the time the shadows began to move, nobody was surprised. They didn’t startle. They didn’t flicker with the drama of prophecy. They shifted with an odd patience—like dancers waiting for music to begin, even though the rhythm had always been in their bones.

Adebola emerged from the crease of two wide stones, pausing to assess the interruption. Her shell picked up pieces of the light and flung them into pattern—hard, sharp fragments turned sudden shimmer.

“This wasn’t scheduled,” she said flatly.

Temi chuckled. “Nothing real ever is.”

Then came more of it—light folding through the grey like clean sheets drawn from an old chest. It hit everything gently, but with purpose: bark, puddles, breath. It coaxed rather than demanded.

One particular puddle, only moments before just another patch of melt, began to hum. Not with sound, but with tone. Its surface trembled as though stirred by memory.

Then something lifted—not the water, but what it contained. It rose slowly, peeled off the puddle’s skin, unfurling into a window that hovered midair. Behind the glass: scenes. Lives. Familiar, though nobody said it aloud.

There was a grandmother with flour-dusted hands, singing in Yoruba as she pressed akara dough into sacred shapes. A boy in a red knit hat cupping his palms to whistle through winter wind. A woman on a rooftop, spinning laughter toward a full moon as though she’d just been let in on the secret of it all.

Chinelo stepped forward. Her logic was usually absolute. But here, in the flickering light, in the quiet hum of recollection, she said only, “This is not logical.”

Adebola, without turning, murmured, “Which is perhaps why it is flawless.”

The four of them gathered—not to study, but to witness. The way people do around fire. Or faith.

And then came the voice.

It didn’t belong to anyone and it didn’t speak in words. But they all understood. It said:

Everything you buried in silence is still glowing.

Temi felt it. Not as a shock, but as a remembering. The unfinished story still wedged under her mattress. The one she’d sworn she’d get to someday.

Ijeoma, slower than the others, cracked softly along the grain of her bark. A name, old and whispered once in longing, stirred in her rings.

Adebola exhaled. “I’ll allow one metaphor. Just one. This light feels like forgiveness with excellent posture.”

And just like that, the shadows changed again. Only this time they weren’t cast. They were self-born. Light’s sibling. Not a product of the moment but part of it. They moved differently now—not passively, but deliberately, as though finally comfortable in their roles. Each one approached its counterpart and wrapped around them—not to bind, but to see.

Yes, they seemed to say. Yes, I see you. All of you. And I love the version that made it through.

Something in the clearing shifted. Not above, not beneath, but in. The snow softened. The air loosened. Even words—yearning, waiting, hoping—lost their shape, as if they’d been replaced by simply... being.

Nobody spoke of the thaw. Nobody named the new warmth. Even Chinelo let it pass without analysis.

There was a sound—short, melodic. Possibly a giggle. Possibly the light itself. Possibly spring arriving early through a single stubborn leaf.

Eventually, as all wonder must, the moment folded. The sky resealed itself—not with resistance, but with understanding. The beams drew back—not abruptly, but with ceremony. They left behind residue. Not a mess. A shimmer.

And the puddles remained lit. And the snow? The snow didn’t stop falling. It just started to make sense.

Adebola adjusted her stance. “Well,” she said, deadpan as ever, “I suppose I’m emotionally compromised.”

Chinelo, for once, didn’t argue. “Good.”

Temi closed her eyes. “Now even the silence glows.”

And from somewhere in Ijeoma’s ancient spine, laughter rose—not loud, but certain.

Something vast and unseen—a presence larger than weather, smaller than breath—let out a long, slow exhale.

The clearing, without needing to be told, stayed exactly as it was: a poem stopped mid-stanza, a soft cathedral of snow and root, perfectly inverted, perfectly itself.

She had been there all along, watching.

Not from above. Not from beyond. Just slightly sideways. She hadn’t left. She had... woven. The boundary had never been real. It was just a fold in the fabric, another page turned halfway back. Her departure had been more suggestion than conclusion.

Still, she watched.

The clearing didn’t recall her as memory. It knew her as presence. As rhythm. As shape in snowfall. She noticed the spiral in the flakes, the endless descent with no final landing. How the world had no corners here. How every direction simply returned you to your own heartbeat.

She used to crave structure. Boundaries. The safety of cause and effect. But here, where becoming never stopped, she began to understand the truth: stillness isn’t waiting. It’s a form of revelation. One breath wide and infinite beneath.

Her beloveds remained below. Temi, scribbling in the dirt with a claw, breath fogging to punctuation. Ijeoma, holding time like a hymn. Adebola, orating to the puddles with the sharp pride of someone who’s never been wrong about something important. Chinelo, distilling paradox into peaceful theory.

They were still unfolding. Still arriving.

She wanted to offer something. A single gift. Not intervention—invitation.

And so she did. In the pause between snowflakes.

Silence is not absence, she whispered. It’s an opening.

She imagined how they might feel her: a shift in shadow that didn’t align with the sun. A warmth in the cold with no source. A stillness so specific, it had to be holding something kind.

She was never far.

Not even now.

Sometimes, if the light bent just right, her shape appeared—not vividly, not with fanfare. Just a trace. Just the outline of a thought.

She didn’t want them to chase her. She only wanted them to sit.

To remember that the shadows they see are only reminders of what still waits to be loved within them.

When the sky tilts again—and it always does—they’ll know. When the light chooses mischief over symmetry and meanders its way through their ordinary moments, she hopes they’ll pause.

They’ll remember: light doesn’t owe order. Beauty doesn’t require balance. And real belonging?

Real belonging just looks you in the eye and says,

Oh. There you are.

It began with a puddle dreaming.

Not in metaphor—the puddle dreamed visibly. Waves of color folded across its surface: matte pinks, copper smoke, green that smelled like wet brass. It inhaled light and exhaled fragments of half-remembered songs.

Temi watched it curl back into itself, then stretch outward as if trying to touch something not in this reality but running parallel beside it. The puddle reflected none of them now. Instead, it showed an hourglass tipping sideways. It leaked mist.

Adebola was mid-sentence—something about ill-mannered metaphors—when her shadow stepped out ahead of her. It wore her smile. Slightly crooked. Unrepentant.

“I think I’m being replaced,” she said.

“No,” Chinelo replied, calculating angles that changed every time she blinked. “You’re being echoed. But in reverse.”

Ijeoma’s trunk had split open—not as injury, but invitation. From within, a staircase coiled downward. Except it coiled up. Except there was no stair.

And the light, oh the light—

It was folding now, thick as silk and twice as sentient. It bent itself into new shapes: the outline of a house built from riddles, the silhouette of a woman with wings stitched from borrowed maps, a small animal made entirely of punctuation.

At one point, a beam of it passed through Temi’s wing. When it emerged on the other side, it whispered in four languages, none of which had names. One of the whispers said, we never left the moment we haven’t arrived in yet.

That made sense. At the time.

Above them, time was peeling.

It curled at the edges like paper left in sun. Numbers no longer moved forward. They rearranged themselves alphabetically, laughing.

“Someone,” Adebola announced flatly, “has clearly stirred the tea with a paintbrush again.”

Chinelo wasn’t listening. She was busy translating gravity into lullaby. Ijeoma had stopped moving altogether. Her leaves now hummed softly, their veins running backward into blossoms that hadn’t bloomed since the world was still thinking about being born.

Temi, kneeling by the puddle, held a reflection in her hands. It pulsed—something between breath and heartbeat. Inside it, she saw all of them standing exactly as they were. And also not.

“It’s not that the clearing changed,” she said at last. “It just remembered something we hadn’t dared imagine yet.”

And with that, the snow fell upward—slowly, gently, like forgiveness unlearning gravity.
