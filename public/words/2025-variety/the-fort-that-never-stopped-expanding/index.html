The Fort That Never Stopped Expanding

Words / 2025 Variety

Part One: The Inheritance

I inherited my grandfather Benjamin Weber's archive the same week Colombia announced it would close La Tramacúa Penitentiary—the prison he'd spent seventeen years documenting, built with $48 million in U.S. aid under agreements he'd helped expose. Standing in his Tampa study, I found 2,847 photographs, 341 recorded interviews, and one question written on index cards pinned to every wall: When did Fort Marion stop being a place and become a system?

The answer was in Box 47, labeled "Osceola's Cell / Shakur's Escape / My Father's Silence." Inside: a coquina stone fragment from Fort Marion's west wall, a 1979 newspaper clipping about Assata Shakur's prison break, and a letter my grandfather never sent to his own father, dated 1962, that began: "I finally understand why you never spoke about what you saw in the Philippines."

My grandfather had traced a single line from 1837 to today—from the moment Seminole Chief Osceola was imprisoned at Fort Marion in St. Augustine to the construction of supermax facilities in Colombia funded by the U.S. Bureau of Prisons. He called it "the fort that never stopped expanding."

I'm telling this story because I hold the evidence: contracts signed, testimonies recorded, connections mapped. Because last month, standing in Osceola's reconstructed cell at Fort Marion—now a tourist site—I watched a Colombian family on vacation taking selfies, unaware their taxes had funded an identical cell 2,000 miles south.

Part Two: St. Augustine, Florida, 1837 (From the Archive)

The limestone walls of Fort Marion sweat in the Florida humidity, each drop carrying microscopic fragments of shells compressed 400,000 years ago—older than any human claim to this land. On October 21, 1837, Seminole Chief Osceola enters under a white flag of truce. General Thomas Jesup's betrayal is swift: the conference table becomes an arrest scene, the peace negotiation a trap.

My grandfather's notes, compiled from military records:

*Cell dimensions: 8 x 10 feet
Limestone thickness: 9 feet
Window: 14 inches square, 11 feet from floor
Daily ration: 1 pound cornmeal, 1/2 pound salt pork
Visitors logged: 47 in three months (tourists, $0.25 admission)*

But Osceola transforms his cell into theater. He appears at the window in full regalia—silver gorgets, ostrich plumes, red leggings—refusing the prison uniform. Artist George Catlin sketches him January 17, 1838, writing: "He dies, but with the dignity of a king in exile."

Osceola's death on January 30, 1838, spreads through telegraph wires. The Charleston Courier reports: "The celebrated chief expired of acute tonsillitis." The prison doctor, Frederick Weedon, decapitates the corpse, keeping the head as a medical curiosity—displaying it to his children when they misbehave.

Part Four: La Tramacúa, Colombia, 2011 (From Interview #238)

[Recorded testimony of Miguel Santos, former prisoner, translated from Spanish]

"The American advisors came in 2002. Measured everything—ceiling height, bar spacing, sight lines. They brought diagrams. I was on the construction crew, $0.80 a day. The blueprints were in English. Someone translated the cover page: 'Based on Marion Model Correctional Methodology.'

"First thing they changed: windows. Made them smaller, higher. Fourteen inches square, they said, eleven feet up. Exact measurements. Said it was about security. But I knew better. When you can't see the horizon, time stops moving. Your mind starts eating itself.

"They brought a consultant, Thompson, from Florida. Taught our guards 'compliance techniques.' How to break someone without leaving marks. Sensory deprivation, they called it. Same thing Osceola faced, just with better terminology.

"My cellmate, Professor Herrera—political prisoner, wrote about land reform—he studied the architecture. Said these prisons were copies of something older. Traced it back to your Fort Marion. 'We're living in their prototype,' he said. 'The laboratory moved, but the experiment continues.'"

Part Five: Threading Backwards (1979)

Assata Shakur's escape route, reconstructed from my grandfather's interviews:

November 2, 1979, 3:47 AM: Two men enter Clinton Correctional Facility for Women, New Jersey. Guards subdued with minimal violence. Assata walks out the front door.

November 3: Safe house, East Orange. The FBI surrounds the wrong building.

November 10: Philadelphia. False trail planted toward California.

November 28: Miami. Boat waiting.

December 15: Havana. Press conference: "I am a 20th century escaped slave."

My grandfather interviewed her nephew in 2003. One quote circled three times: "She said the walls at Clinton were limestone, like Fort Marion. Said America's been building the same prison for 200 years, just changing the inmates."

Part Six: The Filipino Connection (From Letters Never Sent)

My great-grandfather William Weber, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, helped design Bilibid Prison in Manila, 1901. His letters to his wife:

March 3, 1901: "The Spanish left us a framework, but Washington wants improvements. They've sent plans from a fort in Florida—Marion, they call it. Strange to copy such an old design, but they insist on the window specifications."

June 15, 1901: "The Filipinos call it 'Bilangguan ng mga Bayani'—Prison of Heroes. They mean it as protest, but I fear they're right. We're imprisoning their best minds, their leaders. This isn't construction; it's architecture as warfare."

His son—my grandfather's father—never spoke about his own service in the Philippines, 1944-1946. But my grandfather found his journal in 1987, one entry bookmarked:

"Bilibid still stands. Recognize father's work in the walls. Japanese used it for POWs. We're using it for 'insurgents.' Same cells, different empire. Can't stop thinking about the men inside. Look like my neighbors in Tampa. Speak differently, but their faces when discussing family—identical. What are we doing here?"

Part Eight: Valledupar, Colombia (Three Months After Closure Announcement)

I stand outside La Tramacúa with Ricardo Méndez, whose brother died inside in 2018—suicide, the report said, though Ricardo has photos suggesting otherwise. The prison's concrete walls are already cracking, vegetation pushing through like the earth reclaiming its own.

"Your grandfather came here seven times," Ricardo says. "Always asked the same question: 'What did it feel like when you first saw it?' I'll tell you what I told him—it felt like looking at a photograph of a photograph. Like someone took Fort Marion, photocopied it, faxed it here, then built from the degraded image."

He pulls out a smartphone, shows me a video from inside before closure: 180 men in a space for 40, concrete floor slick with sewage, one toilet, no windows visible. "This is your export," he says, not unkindly. "This is what happens when imprisonment becomes technology transfer."

Behind us, former prisoners' families hold vigil—not for the prison's closure, but for the 47 men who died inside over two decades. Candles flicker in mason jars, each labeled with names and dates. The youngest: Tomás Rivera, 19, "acute tonsillitis" (the same official cause as Osceola's death).

Part Nine: The Return to Fort Marion (Present)

I return to St. Augustine with Box 47's contents. The tourist portion closes at 5 PM, but I've arranged with the National Park Service to access Osceola's actual cell after hours.

The limestone walls still sweat. My fingers find grooves—not from Osceola, but from the 278 Native Americans imprisoned here 1875-1878, the 461 Apache held 1886-1887, the unnamed others. Their marks layer like sediment, each generation of prisoners adding their testimony to the stone.

I place my grandfather's coquina fragment against the wall. Perfect match—he'd chipped it from this exact spot in 1987, the day he decided to map the expansion.

In my pocket: testimony from María Gonzalez, arrested in Bogotá for protesting water privatization, held in La Tramacúa 2009-2011. She describes her cell: "Eight by ten feet. Window fourteen inches, too high to reach. Limestone transferred to concrete, but the same tomb dimensions. I measured with my body—four steps long, three steps wide. Same as your Osceola. Same as your Assata. Same as every prisoner in this system they've spread like virus."

Part Ten: What the Archive Reveals (Present)

In the final box, #172, I find what my grandfather considered his proof:

Department of Defense Memo, 1967: "The Marion Model proves effective for indigenous resistance populations. Recommend implementation in strategic territories."

USAID Report, 1994: "Prison infrastructure development creates stability for economic investment. Colombian implementation shows 73% reduction in organized resistance within containment zones."

Private email, CCA executive, 2001: "The beauty of Marion specs is their universality. Same design works in Philippines, Colombia, Arizona. Humans break predictably in regulated spaces."

But the last document makes me set down the box:

Letter from my grandfather to me, dated one week before his death:

"Ben—By the time you read this, you'll have the archive. You'll see what I've seen: Fort Marion never stopped expanding. It just learned to reproduce. Every cell built to its specifications carries its DNA—the peculiar American fusion of efficiency and cruelty, the transformation of punishment into profit.

But here's what the documents won't show: resistance is older than any fort. Osceola's dignity, Assata's escape, the Colombian protestors singing through concrete walls—they're the same song. The fort expands, but so does the determination to tear it down.

Don't just document this. End it.

Your inheritance isn't the archive. It's the obligation.

—Benjamin"

Epilogue: The Question That Remains

Six months after Colombia announces La Tramacúa's closure, they break ground on a new facility fifteen miles away. The contractor: MTC (Management & Training Corporation), based in Utah. The blueprints, obtained through Ricardo's legal team, show familiar dimensions: 8 x 10 feet cells, windows 14 inches square, 11 feet up.

Fort Marion's methodology, perfected over 187 years, encoded in concrete and steel, exported through treaties and training programs, generating $70 billion annually in the U.S. alone—the fort that learned to franchise itself.

I stand again in Osceola's cell, running my fingers over marks in the limestone. A Japanese tourist asks what I'm doing. I tell her: "Measuring the prototype for the world's largest prison system."

She nods politely, not understanding. But her daughter, maybe twelve, studies the cell dimensions with serious eyes. "It's still expanding, isn't it?" she asks in perfect English.

Yes. The fort that never stopped expanding is still growing, still reproducing, still transforming bodies into revenue, resistance into reasons for more cells. But in La Tramacúa's abandoned halls, vegetation breaks through concrete. In New Jersey, murals of Assata multiply on walls. In Colombia, families of prisoners organize, demanding transformation, not reformation.

My grandfather's question—When did Fort Marion stop being a place and become a system?—has an answer: October 21, 1837, the moment Osceola was betrayed under a flag of truce. That betrayal became architecture, architecture became methodology, methodology became export commodity.

But there's another date worth remembering: November 2, 1979, when Assata Shakur walked out of a limestone prison, proving that no fort—no matter how far it expands—can contain the human impulse toward freedom.

The archive sits in my study now, 2,847 photographs of the same prison built again and again, each one believing itself unique. But I've added a new box, #173: testimonies from those organizing against the next iteration, those who understand that the fort's expansion can be stopped, must be stopped, will be stopped.

The work continues.
