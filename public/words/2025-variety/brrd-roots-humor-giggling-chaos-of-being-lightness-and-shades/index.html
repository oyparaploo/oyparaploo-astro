Brrd Roots ~ Humor ~ Giggling Chaos of Being ~ Lightness and Shades

Words / 2025 Variety

It starts the way most bad decisions start—with misplaced confidence and an overcaffeinated sense of control.

Somewhere between apocalyptic dread and sweetened almost-dead comedy, between laundry bags of foreign cash and booty texts that deserve an Oscar, we’re all doing the same thing—trying to figure out what kind of story we’re telling and whether or not we’ll ever stop talking forever and never stopping.

We want things under our noses—the closest comforts, the fastest ideas—but the world won’t let us just reach, grab, and be done with it. No, the world wiggles, the world contradicts, the world flinches when podcasts try to tell it what grief really is.

We love having conversations, but isn’t that just performing? We tell jokes, we spin stories, we lay our thoughts bare, but somewhere in the process of casting, branding, presenting—are we just optimizing ourselves into ghosts?

And then there’s temptation. The grand distraction. The thing we understand because it’s as human as grief and first drafts and ancient slavery. The thing that whispers surrender, tries to pull us under its soft pressure—colonial competition, commercial warfare, the slow march toward making everything a product.

If we quit—if we give in—what happens to our pet projects?

They love us more than we think they do.

They float when we think we’re sinking.

They make space for ridiculousness, for wild momentum, for the kind of giggle freedom that turns a performance into something true.

We follow our hearts from the beginning—but how do we stop second-guessing ourselves when writing new jokes? How do we let go of the pressure to make steady forward progress every minute of the day? How do we exist without shamefully chasing productivity like it’s an unruly dog?

The truth is that relationships don’t require that kind of attention.

Humorous dreams bring satisfaction even if we never achieve them.

We cannot be Zen and feel guilty at the same time.

We cannot optimize ourselves for the masses and expect to feel whole.

Instead, we trust kindred spirits—the ones who run with the child ball, who giggle with freedom, who perform card tricks before legal out-of-water zoo animals.

Instead, we follow funny momentum, even when it seems offbeat.

We look at ourselves in the mirror and accept that we’ll be ourselves, whether that means doing it this way or doing it that way.

We create. We make meaning. We fail. We laugh at failure like it’s a rigged game show.

And when expectations turn everything into dung, we find the magic hidden inside it anyway.

Because everything—every fast processing thought, every misplaced idea, every abandoned dream—is still a giggling chaos of being.

And that’s enough.

Spectacular Art of Falling Both Loudly and Quietly

Somewhere between apocalyptic dread and booty texts sent with suspicious confidence, between laundry bags stuffed with foreign cash and first drafts masquerading as finished brilliance, we are all stumbling through time with the grace of a flamingo that forgot how knees work.

But isn’t that just life?

Take a Wednesday morning. A perfectly neutral, forgettable Wednesday morning where the coffee spills, the phone rings, and someone somewhere declares that today is finally the day they get their life together. Maybe they do. Maybe they don’t. Maybe they get distracted halfway through and start wondering whether time itself is just running in circles for the sake of entertainment.

We crave control. We want things under our noses, within reach, clearly labeled. But the world doesn’t work like that. No, the world shuffles things when we aren’t looking, pushes the good cheese to the back, hides existential truths in the produce section, then stands back and watches, amused, as we frantically try to find what we came for.

And so we talk.

We talk about who quit, who gave in, who surrendered—to colonial competition, to the soft pressure of optimization, to the endless march toward turning ourselves into brands.

We talk about grief, about how hard it is to explain without making it sound like bad poetry.

We talk about temptation, because of course we understand it—it’s as natural as breathing, as inevitable as regret, as stupidly human as buying expensive candles and pretending it counts as self-care.

We talk, and we perform, and we laugh at ourselves, because the alternative is taking everything too seriously and collapsing under the weight of it.

And somewhere in the middle of all that talking, our pet projects whisper to us.

They remind us that they love us more than we think they do.

That even when we abandon them, they sit in the corner of the universe, wagging their tails, whispering, I still exist, idiot.

They float. Like parade floats. Like afterthoughts with glitter. Like dreams that refuse to be discarded, even when life demands practicality.

Meanwhile, hardcore readers see everything. They decode our deepest insecurities, analyze our streaming combination of dread and caffeine-fueled euphoria, quietly wondering whether we meant that metaphor exactly the way it sounded.

And honestly? Yes, there should be a book about how to achieve your most shallow mindset.

What’s the difference between ancient slavery and a bad first draft? Expectations, probably. Everything turns into dung when expectations are too high.

But that’s the magic.

Dung becomes fertilizer. Mistakes become momentum. Every failure is just a future absurd anecdote.

So we trust kindred spirits—the ones who run with child balls, who giggle with freedom, who perform card tricks for out-of-water zoo animals.

We cannot be Zen and guilty at the same time.

We cannot optimize ourselves for mass consumption and expect to feel whole.

Instead, we do it this way or that way.

We embrace funny momentum, even when it trips over itself.

We follow our hearts, from the beginning, through the chaos, through bad paperback novels and flinching podcasts, through expectations that are too high and apocalypses that aren’t happening fast enough.

Because at the end of everything—when the plot twists get ridiculous, when all the metaphors trip over their own feet—the only way forward is to fall louder, laugh harder, and keep moving.

It starts the way most bad decisions start—with misplaced confidence and an overcaffeinated sense of control.

Somewhere between apocalyptic dread and booty texts sent with suspicious enthusiasm, between laundry bags stuffed with foreign cash and first drafts masquerading as finished brilliance, we are all stumbling through time with the grace of a flamingo that forgot how knees work.

A perfectly neutral Wednesday morning. Coffee spills with deep malice, as if it has a personal vendetta. The phone rings at the worst possible moment—twice, just to be spiteful. Somewhere, someone announces—out loud, even—that today is finally the day they get their life together.

Maybe they do. Maybe they don’t. Maybe they just rearrange their furniture, call it a spiritual rebirth, and move on.

This is the illusion of control—this desperate little belief that if enough things are placed in the right order, life will gracefully cooperate. But life is a feral cat, and control is just shaking a bag of treats, hoping it comes back.

“Are you talking to yourself?”

The voice is coming from the couch.

Not from the couch, exactly, but from someone who has, for all intents and purposes, merged with it.

“Hypothetically,” I say.

They yawn, stretch like a housecat, and grab their phone.

“Good luck with the self-improvement. I, personally, have scheduled a crisis for later this afternoon, but only a small one. Just enough to keep things interesting.”

I consider my options. Respond? Ignore? Spill more coffee and let it decide the mood for me?

Instead, I sigh and continue narrating the day as if it has any logic at all.

The world is not a well-organized pantry. It refuses to be labeled, refuses to be easy, refuses to hand anyone certainty in a reusable tote bag. It pushes the good cheese to the back, hides existential truths in the produce section, then stands back, amused, watching people scramble to make sense of it all.

And so we talk.

We talk about who quit, who gave in, who surrendered—to colonial competition, to optimization, to the endless march toward turning ourselves into brands.

We talk about grief, and how it always starts as bad poetry before it turns into something real.

We talk about temptation, because of course we understand it—it’s as natural as impulse buys, as inevitable as regret, as stupidly human as convincing yourself that one more scented candle will fix your life.

We talk, we perform, we laugh at ourselves—because the alternative is taking everything too seriously and collapsing under the weight of it.

Meanwhile, hardcore readers see everything. They analyze our streaming combination of dread and caffeine-fueled euphoria, quietly wondering whether we meant that metaphor exactly the way it sounded.

"Who even writes like this?" Couch-Person mutters, still scrolling.

Me. Obviously.

And somewhere in the middle of all that talking, our pet projects whisper to us.

They remind us that they love us more than we think they do.

That even when abandoned, they sit in the corner of the universe, wagging their tails, whispering, Still here, idiot.

They float—like parade floats, like half-forgotten dreams with a glitter budget, like something stupidly beautiful refusing to be discarded just because life demands practicality.

"Maybe failure isn’t real," Couch-Person muses, clicking through social media.

"Maybe you're deflecting," I counter, but we both know neither of us actually care about winning this conversation.

Because failure turns into fertilizer. Mistakes become momentum. Every disaster is just a future absurd anecdote.

And honestly? Yes, there should be a book about how to achieve your most shallow mindset.

We trust kindred spirits—the ones who run with child balls, who giggle with freedom, who perform card tricks for out-of-water zoo animals.

We cannot be Zen and guilty at the same time.

We cannot optimize ourselves for mass consumption and expect to feel whole.

Instead, we do it this way or that way.

We embrace funny momentum, even when it trips over itself.

We follow our hearts, from the beginning, through the chaos, through bad paperback novels and flinching podcasts, through expectations that are too high and apocalypses that aren’t happening fast enough.

Because at the end of everything—when the plot twists get ridiculous, when all the metaphors trip over their own feet—the only way forward is to fall louder, laugh harder, and keep moving.

"...I think I want waffles," Couch-Person announces.

I glance at the coffee-stained countertop. At the barely-started day. At the ongoing absurdity of existence itself.

Waffles.

Yeah. That feels like progress.
