Giggling Chaos of Being ~ Lightness and Shades

← Words / 2025 Variety

It starts the way most bad decisions start—with misplaced confidence and an overcaffeinated sense of control.

Somewhere between apocalyptic dread and booty texts sent with suspicious enthusiasm, between laundry bags stuffed with foreign cash and first drafts masquerading as finished brilliance, we are all stumbling through time with the grace of a flamingo that forgot how knees work.

A perfectly neutral Wednesday morning. Coffee spills with deep malice, as if it has a personal vendetta. The phone rings at the worst possible moment—twice, just to be spiteful. Somewhere, someone announces—out loud, even—that today is finally the day they get their life together.

Maybe they do. Maybe they don’t. Maybe they just rearrange their furniture, call it a spiritual rebirth, and move on.

This is the illusion of control—this desperate little belief that if enough things are placed in the right order, life will gracefully cooperate. But life is a feral cat, and control is just shaking a bag of treats, hoping it comes back.

“Are you talking to yourself?”

The voice is coming from the couch.

Not from the couch, exactly, but from someone who has, for all intents and purposes, merged with it.

“Hypothetically,” I say.

They yawn, stretch like a housecat, and grab their phone.

“Good luck with the self-improvement. I, personally, have scheduled a crisis for later this afternoon, but only a small one. Just enough to keep things interesting.”

I consider my options. Respond? Ignore? Spill more coffee and let it decide the mood for me?

Instead, I sigh and continue narrating the day as if it has any logic at all.

The world is not a well-organized pantry. It refuses to be labeled, refuses to be easy, refuses to hand anyone certainty in a reusable tote bag. It pushes the good cheese to the back, hides existential truths in the produce section, then stands back, amused, watching people scramble to make sense of it all.

And so we talk.

We talk about who quit, who gave in, who surrendered—to colonial competition, to optimization, to the endless march toward turning ourselves into brands.

We talk about grief, and how it always starts as bad poetry before it turns into something real.

We talk about temptation, because of course we understand it—it’s as natural as impulse buys, as inevitable as regret, as stupidly human as convincing yourself that one more scented candle will fix your life.

We talk, we perform, we laugh at ourselves—because the alternative is taking everything too seriously and collapsing under the weight of it.

Meanwhile, hardcore readers see everything. They analyze our streaming combination of dread and caffeine-fueled euphoria, quietly wondering whether we meant that metaphor exactly the way it sounded.

"Who even writes like this?" Couch-Person mutters, still scrolling.

Me. Obviously.

And somewhere in the middle of all that talking, our pet projects whisper to us.

They remind us that they love us more than we think they do.

That even when abandoned, they sit in the corner of the universe, wagging their tails, whispering, Still here, idiot.

They float—like parade floats, like half-forgotten dreams with a glitter budget, like something stupidly beautiful refusing to be discarded just because life demands practicality.

"Maybe failure isn’t real," Couch-Person muses, clicking through social media.

"Maybe you're deflecting," I counter, but we both know neither of us actually care about winning this conversation.

Because failure turns into fertilizer. Mistakes become momentum. Every disaster is just a future absurd anecdote.

And honestly? Yes, there should be a book about how to achieve your most shallow mindset.

We trust kindred spirits—the ones who run with child balls, who giggle with freedom, who perform card tricks for out-of-water zoo animals.

We cannot be Zen and guilty at the same time.

We cannot optimize ourselves for mass consumption and expect to feel whole.

Instead, we do it this way or that way.

We embrace funny momentum, even when it trips over itself.

We follow our hearts, from the beginning, through the chaos, through bad paperback novels and flinching podcasts, through expectations that are too high and apocalypses that aren’t happening fast enough.

Because at the end of everything—when the plot twists get ridiculous, when all the metaphors trip over their own feet—the only way forward is to fall louder, laugh harder, and keep moving.

"...I think I want waffles," Couch-Person announces.

I glance at the coffee-stained countertop. At the barely-started day. At the ongoing absurdity of existence itself.

Waffles.

Yeah. That feels like progress.
