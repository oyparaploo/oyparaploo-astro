Pivotal Moments That Have No Endpoint

Words / 2025 Variety

---

In the moment between losing everything and starting over, a group of strangers in a bankruptcy court waiting room discover that saying "we are doing something" changes reality itself—not through magic but through the simple act of shifting from separate to together, from frozen to moving.

---

Marie Debris Johnson sat in the gray waiting room, papers everywhere—on her lap, falling off, she didn't care. Foreclosure on the house her grandmother had bought in 1962 for $11,000, now valued at $380,000, now gone. Medical bankruptcy from the miscarriage—$67,000 for losing a baby at twenty-three weeks, for the D&C, for the infection after, for the therapy that didn't help, for the Xanax that helped too much. The whole mess. Around her, six others, same boat, different leaks.

She started moving chairs. Just dragging them. Metal on linoleum. Making noise that sounded like screaming.

"What are you doing?" the clerk asked, alarmed. $32,000 a year to watch people lose everything.

Marie looked up. Almost said "nothing." Instead: "We are doing something."

"We?" The clerk glanced at the others—strangers staring at floors, walls, anywhere but each other.

"We," Marie repeated, and Pascal Cue looked up. Owned a restaurant until COVID. $900,000 in debt. Then Drumm Winters. Lost his trucking business when diesel hit $5.40. Then the woman in the corner—Silt, just Silt—tearing her forms into tiny pieces. Chapter 7. Chapter 13. Chapter who-gives-a-fuck.

"We are doing something," Pascal said, testing it. The words felt wrong. Right. Both. Like the taste of expensive wine when you can't afford water.

That was it. Pivotal. Everything lining up then falling apart then lining up different.

"What are we—" Drumm started.

"Something," Marie interrupted. "Just... something. Move that chair."

"Why?"

"Because moving is doing and doing is... just move it!"

They started rearranging everything. Not carefully. Chairs scraped. Papers flew. The woman in the corner (Silt, she'd whisper later, like sediment, like what's left after everything settles) started laughing, then crying, then both.

"This is insane," Silt said, but she was stacking forms into towers. Bankruptcy forms as architecture. As art. As fuck-you to the system.

"More insane than sitting here separately drowning?" Marie asked, knocking over a water cooler. Not on purpose. Or maybe on purpose. Hard to tell. The water spread across the floor like a map of somewhere better.

Present intensity captured them. This insistence on motion, never complete, refusing endpoints. The waiting room became chaos—chairs in circles, then lines, then piles. Papers everywhere. Someone's coffee spilled—$4.50 latte, last luxury before surrender. Nobody cleaned it.

"We're making a mess," Pascal said, delighted. First time he'd smiled since losing the restaurant. Since his wife left. Since his daughter stopped calling.

"We're doing something," Marie corrected.

"Same thing!"

The clerk called security. "They're destroying—"

"WE ARE DOING SOMETHING!" everyone yelled. Not angry. Just... loud. Present. Continuous. The grammar of survival.

Security arrived. Jorge. $14 an hour. Also in bankruptcy. Medical bills from his son's autism therapy that insurance called "unnecessary." He looked at them. At the chaos. At the water spreading across the floor.

"We are doing something," Marie said.

Jorge nodded. Moved a chair.

They started meeting every Tuesday. In the waiting room until banned. Then the parking lot where they'd all lived in their cars at some point. Then the park across the street where the homeless camps were. Always doing something. Building things from trash—Pascal made a dining table from foreclosure signs. Breaking things that needed breaking—Drumm took a sledgehammer to his truck, made it sculpture. Moving constantly because stillness meant thinking and thinking meant remembering what they'd lost.

"This is..." Drumm started one day, trying to explain to his ex-wife why he couldn't pick up the kids. Why he was covered in paint and dirt and something like hope.

"Revolutionary prayer space," Silt interrupted. She'd been a teacher. Master's degree. Let go in budget cuts. $47,000 in student loans for a job that no longer existed.

"Comically modest," Pascal added.

"We are doing something," Marie said, like that explained everything. It did.

It became their answer to everything:

"What's your case number?"
"We are doing something."

"Are you protesting?"
"We are doing something."

"Are you okay?"
"We are doing something."

They wrote it everywhere. Chalk on courthouse walls—$500 fine they'd never pay. Pencil on bathroom stalls—where people cried through proceedings. Lipstick on mirrors—seeing themselves becoming something else. WE ARE DOING SOMETHING. Not explaining. Just declaring.

"It's like," Pascal tried to tell the reporter from the local news, "when someone walks in on you making a mess—"

"A creative mess," Silt interrupted.

"Papers everywhere—" Drumm added. His hands still had motor oil under the nails. Permanent. Like grief.

"And instead of apologizing—" Marie continued.

"You just say—" Pascal again.

"WE ARE DOING SOMETHING!" all together, laughing like maniacs. Like people who'd lost everything. Like people who'd found something else.

The reporter wrote: "Local Group Does Something." Missed the point entirely.

They didn't frame it. They tore it into pieces. Made confetti. Threw it at the next meeting. Then ate some of it. Because why not. Because doing. Because the paper tasted like freedom.

More people came. Hundreds eventually. All broken by different specifics—medical bankruptcy (67% of cases), business failure (COVID, inflation, Amazon), divorce (lawyer fees averaging $15,000), all needing to shift from I to we, from stopped to moving, from alone to together to chaos to—

"What do you actually DO?" everyone asked.

"Everything," Marie would say.

"Nothing," Pascal would say at the same time.

"Something," Silt would correct them both.

"Specifically we... we don't know. Specifically everything? Nothing? The mess between?" Drumm would try.

"That makes no sense—"

"Perfect! Nothing makes sense. Bankruptcy for medical bills doesn't make sense. Losing your grandmother's house to Wells Fargo doesn't make sense. We are doing something with the no-sense."

The court got restraining orders. They ignored them. Got arrested. Jorge the security guard testified on their behalf—"They're doing something, your honor." Got released. Kept doing something. In jail cells—taught inmates to say it. In courtrooms—whispered it during proceedings. In the street—screamed it at the federal building. The doing wouldn't stop because stopping would mean—

"Endpoints," Marie explained to her lawyer. $350 an hour. Billed in six-minute increments. "We refuse endpoints."

"You have to stop sometime—"

"No. We are doing something. Present continuous. Always."

"But what—"

"SOMETHING!"

They became infamous. Then famous. Then infamous again. Then forgot about fame because fame was an endpoint and they were doing something.

Tuesday, 3 AM. Fifty people in the park. Moving benches. Building nothing. Building everything. Someone brought paint—stolen from Home Depot, self-checkout liberation. Someone brought food—dumpster diving behind Whole Foods, $100 worth of bread thrown out for being a day old. Someone brought their bankruptcy papers and burned them which was illegal but who cared they were doing something.

"We lined something up," Marie said, covered in paint and ash. The ashes of debt. The paint of refusal.

"The moment—" Pascal started.

"When everything—" Silt continued.

"Shifts—" Drumm added.

"We ARE shifting," they all corrected. "Always shifting. We are doing—"

A cop showed up. "What's going on here?"

"Something," they all said. Then laughed. Then cried. Then moved more benches. Then painted the cop who didn't seem to mind, who maybe needed to shift too, who made $52,000 a year to police people who made nothing, who maybe understood something about doing something when nothing made sense.

"I don't understand," the cop said, but he was helping move benches now.

"Good," Marie said. "Understanding is an endpoint. We are doing something."

The sun came up. They kept doing. The sun went down. Still doing. Weeks passed. Months. Still doing something. The bankruptcy court closed for renovations (their mess had spread somehow, infected the building with motion). They kept meeting. Kept moving. Kept refusing to stop, to explain, to make sense.

"Are we better?" Silt asked once. Six months in. Still teaching but under the table now. Cash only. No taxes. No system.

"We're doing," everyone answered.

"But are we—"

"WE ARE DOING SOMETHING!"

Not as answer. As action. As the thing that never ends because ending would mean accepting stillness again, separation again, the isolated disaster of medical bills and foreclosures and diesel prices and COVID and everything else that had broken them individually but couldn't break them collectively because they were doing something.

Someone suggested making t-shirts. They made them. With stolen supplies. Then tore them up. Then made new ones from the pieces. Then wore them. Then traded them. Then threw them in the air. Because doing. Because something. Because we.

The city offered them a grant. $10,000 to formalize. To become a 501(c)(3). To have endpoints.

They burned the application. Kept doing something. Are still doing something. Will always be doing something. Without endpoints. Without explanation. Without anything but the pivotal eternal moment of choosing to be we, choosing to do, choosing something over nothing over everything over something.

Always we.
Always are.
Always doing.
Always something.
Always.

---

We are all doing something when we shift from I to we, from frozen to chaos, from making sense to making mess, from separate disasters to collective becoming that never stops, never completes, never explains itself beyond the simple revolutionary insistence that we are doing something, always, forever, without endpoints, without understanding, just the pure pivotal eternal doing that emerges from bankruptcy and becomes something else, something unending, something that refuses to be nothing even when everything has been taken away.
