Any Heaven Worth the Name Must Be Green

Words / 2025 Variety

A Meditation on Whether Photosynthesis Continues After Death, and Other Questions That Bloom in the Strange Hours Between Grief and Wonder

( Introduction )

In this haunting theatrical meditation, a lone voice wanders through the purple hours before dawn, posing an impossible question: Does photosynthesis continue after we die? What begins as philosophical whimsy transforms into a breathtaking journey through humanity's garden of grief and hope, where wish-fulfilling trees grow branches for those who couldn't afford medicine, and pomegranates contain gems for every secret kindness. Through lyrical passages that blur the boundaries between essay and incantation, we traverse the afterlife visions of world traditions—from eternal gardens where fruit regenerates with each picking to lotus ponds that birth consciousness itself. The play's dreamscape suddenly fractures when a news reporter appears at a protest rally, screaming that the gardens are already growing inside our chests, her microphone transforming into a bird that flies toward hope. This is theater as séance, inviting audiences to sit in the strange blue light between sorrow and wonder, between the last breath and the first bloom, ultimately revealing that any heaven worth the name must be green.

( The Story )

So here's a strange question I've been mulling over in those purple hours before dawn, when the world feels thin as moth wings: Does photosynthesis keep going after we die?

I know, I know. It sounds like something you'd overhear in a college dorm at 2 AM, whispered between young souls who haven't yet learned how much of themselves they'll leave in gardens they'll never see bloom again. But stick with me here. This odd little question actually opens up something much bigger—it's where science bumps into spirituality, where our love for the natural world meets our hope for what comes next, where all the flowers we never picked wait for us in some impossible meadow.

The Great Redemption of Growing Things

Most of us were raised with some version of an afterlife that sounds, frankly, a bit boring. Clouds. Harps. Maybe some singing. But dig into the actual texts and traditions, and you find something wild: a vision where Earth doesn't end but transforms. Gets upgraded, if you like. As if every tear we've shed waters seeds we can't yet see.

The old poetry and sacred writings are absolutely drunk on nature imagery—maybe because the writers knew what we know: that everyone we've lost loved something green. Someone's grandmother tended roses. Someone's child collected autumn leaves. Rivers in these texts run clearer than anything we've got here, perhaps carrying away all the words we never said. Trees with fruit that actually satisfies—not just your stomach, but that deeper hunger we all carry around, the one that started the first time we realized summers end.

What gets me, sitting here in my kitchen while outside the rain taps messages on windows to no one in particular, is how these visions flip our everyday experience. Here, beauty fades (I've watched it happen in hospital rooms, in November gardens, in mirrors). There, it doesn't. Here, we watch things we love decay. There, decay just... isn't a thing anymore. The essence of what makes a rose smell sweet or a mountain take your breath away—that stays. But all the thorns and avalanches? Gone. Along with the arthritic hands that could no longer hold the pruning shears.

It's like someone took nature and ran it through the ultimate Instagram filter, except real. The sun gets replaced by something that illuminates without burning—a light that would never give melanoma to the uncle who loved fishing. The air carries praise instead of pollen. (Though I bet some of us would miss the pollen. We're weird that way. We miss everything, even the things that made us sneeze.)

Gardens of Infinite Delight

Then you've got traditions that go all-in on the sensory details, painted in colors that don't exist yet, for all the artists who died with paintbrushes in their hands. These aren't your minimalist, stark-white afterlives. We're talking full-on garden party paradise, where everyone you've lost is tending something beautiful.

The texts get specific. Really specific. Springs that bubble up from pure joy—which sounds impossible until you remember that one perfect spring morning when you felt exactly that, before you knew how much you'd lose. Palm trees swaying to music only they can hear, the same music your mother hummed while doing dishes. Pomegranates that somehow contain actual gems, one for every kindness done in secret, every anonymous donation, every time someone chose gentleness when rage would have been easier.

And the fruit thing—this always gets me—fruit that regenerates the instant you pick it. No scarcity. No guilt about taking the last apple. No children going hungry while orchards rot. It breaks every law of conservation we know, but maybe that's the point. Maybe paradise operates on abundance instead of economics, the way love should have worked all along.

The birds there (and why wouldn't there be birds? All those sparrows that flew into windows, all those migrations interrupted by city lights) sing songs that do double duty as entertainment and enlightenment. Efficient. The flowers never stop blooming, which should get monotonous but somehow doesn't—like how you never got tired of your daughter's laugh, even the ten thousandth time. The whole setup suggests that earthly pleasures and spiritual joy aren't opposites. They're dance partners, swaying in the strange blue light that comes just before thunderstorms.

The Quieter Symbolisms

Not everyone goes for the full Broadway production, though. Some traditions prefer their afterlife nature with the volume turned down, like libraries after closing time, when the books whisper their secrets to empty chairs. Gardens become metaphors within metaphors, dreams within dreams. Trees mean wisdom earned through rings of sorrow. Rivers mean understanding flowing toward those who thirst for meaning. Light means... well, light means everything we couldn't see through our tears.

But even the most abstract philosophies can't quite let go of gardens. It's like we're hardwired to understand bliss through leaves and petals—maybe because the first thing we lose is usually something that grew. Even when they're trying to describe the indescribable, they reach for roses, those blood-bright reminders of beauty's cost.

The mystics in these traditions see layers everywhere, like sedimentary rock made of compressed prayers. Multiple paradises, each with its own weird ecology, its own way of transforming grief into growth. And the end game? The whole universe becomes what our gardens and temples are trying to be—a place where sacred and natural aren't two things but one thing. Where dust is holy (containing as it does the remnants of stars and grandparents) and water is sacred (having passed through clouds and kidneys and baptismal fonts) and light carries blessings like a bee carries pollen, from soul to soul, across the strange geography of forever.

The Spectrum of Liberation

Eastern thought gives us the full menu, served on plates made of solidified sighs, from "transcend everything material" to "check out these celestial realms that make Earth look like a rough draft written by someone who'd just learned about suffering."

Wish-fulfilling trees. I love this concept. Trees that grow what you need, not what their DNA dictates. A branch for everyone who couldn't afford medicine. A leaf for every letter never sent. Lotus ponds that birth consciousness itself, their surfaces reflecting not what is but what could have been. (Try explaining that to a botanist. Try explaining any of this to anyone who hasn't sat beside a hospital bed at 3 AM, watching breath become more precious than diamonds.) Forests where all seasons happen simultaneously—cherry blossoms with autumn maples with winter pines, all thriving in impossible harmony, the way families should have worked.

The descriptions go full cosmic poetry, written in languages that taste like rain. Trees made of crystallized joy—the kind you felt holding your newborn before you knew about SIDS. Lakes of liquid light, where every ripple is a memory saved from forgetting. Birds whose songs teach while they sing, explaining why things had to hurt so much. It's like nature got a degree in multitasking and a PhD in healing what it first had to break.

But—and here's where it gets interesting, in that way funerals are interesting when someone breaks into inappropriate laughter—even these amazing realms are seen as temporary. Waypoints, not destinations.

Suddenly, as if tuning into a frequency from another dimension, I see a news reporter in a rumpled suit, microphone trembling in her grip like a divining rod detecting rage. She's at some protest rally that might be happening now or a thousand years ago or never, and she's screaming—really screaming—at the crowd: "DON'T YOU UNDERSTAND? THE GARDENS ARE ALREADY HERE! THEY'RE GROWING INSIDE YOUR CHEST! STOP LOOKING UP WHEN YOU SHOULD BE LOOKING IN!" Her cameraman has abandoned his equipment, which films only sky now, endless sky the color of forgiveness. The protesters hold signs that read things like "MORE CHLOROPHYLL FOR THE DEAD" and "MY GRANDMOTHER DESERVES ETERNAL PHOTOSYNTHESIS" and one strange one that just says "YES" in green letters that seem to be growing leaves. The reporter throws her microphone into the crowd where it transforms into a small bird that flies directly upward until it becomes indistinguishable from hope.

And then the vision fades, like all visions do, leaving only the scent of phantom jasmine and the feeling that somewhere, in some realm, that reporter is now tending the most beautiful garden, her anger transformed into the most careful kind of cultivation.

Like nature itself becomes a ladder, and eventually you don't need ladders. You just... are. The way we all were before we learned we could stop being.

The Dance of Temporary Eternity

This paradox breaks my brain in the best way, like ice cracking on a pond you're not sure will hold your weight. Eternal realms that aren't eternal. Permanent gardens that aren't permanent. Some souls get reborn into worlds where music has texture (rough like grief, smooth like acceptance) and lotuses grow as big as cars and everything is trying to teach you something about impermanence while being weirdly permanent itself.

Every flower whispers about change while never changing, like photographs of the dead. Every regenerating fruit talks about abundance while pointing beyond abundance, toward something that makes generosity look stingy. The whole environment becomes a very patient teacher, the kind who waits while you cry in the bathroom, using beauty as its curriculum and sorrow as its teaching assistant.

And then—if you follow this thread far enough, past all the funerals you've attended and the ones you've missed—you get to something that isn't a place or a state or anything language can wrap around. The lotus rising clean from mud becomes the perfect image: using nature to point beyond nature. Like a finger pointing at the moon, except the finger is made of flowers and the moon is... actually, I have no idea what the moon is in this metaphor. That's kind of the point. Like trying to explain the color blue to someone who's never had eyes.

The Eternal Nature Paradox Resolved

Here's where the rational mind starts sending error messages, like a computer trying to divide by zero or calculate the weight of a goodbye. Photosynthesis without death? Eternal blooming? Fruits that grow without depleting anything? Rivers flowing without gravity? It's enough to make a physicist weep—which, coincidentally, is the same thing poetry does.

But maybe—just maybe—that's exactly what we need to hear in our strange floating moment between what was and what might be. These visions aren't trying to satisfy the scientist in us. They're feeding something else, something that knows every act of kindness creates ripples in dimensions we can't see. They show us nature freed from everything that makes it "natural." Beauty without the expiration date we all carry. Growth without the compost heap where we bury our hopes.

Each tradition handles this impossible physics its own way, like jazz musicians riffing on the theme of "what if nothing beautiful ever really dies?" Some say heaven runs on different laws—grace instead of chemistry, joy instead of photons, the same energy that makes strangers share umbrellas. Others say consciousness eventually leaves all forms behind, finding a freedom that includes and surpasses every tree and river and the hands that planted and swam. Still others offer a choose-your-own-adventure afterlife where different souls find different destinations, like trains leaving a station built from compressed starlight and forgiveness.

The Great Continuity

What amazes me, here in this kitchen where I've had countless conversations with people who are now just photographs, is how all these different visions agree on the big stuff. Death changes nature's wonder; it doesn't delete it. The files get converted to a new format, but nothing is lost—not even the small kindnesses, the glasses of water offered to strangers, the flowers left on graves no one visits anymore.

So does photosynthesis continue after death? Depends on your ZIP code in eternity, apparently. In the paradise gardens, maybe it draws energy from joy instead of sunlight—the accumulated joy of every wedding dance, every child's first laugh, every moment someone chose love over fear. In the middle realms, nature keeps doing its thing through divine permit rather than natural law, signed by whatever bureaucracy handles resurrection permits. And for those who graduate beyond all realms? The question melts away like snow on a tongue, leaving only sweetness and the memory of cold.

These traditions are making us a promise wrapped in petals and tied with the ribbon of every goodbye that hurt: nothing beautiful truly disappears. The rose leaves the garden but doesn't stop being a rose. It becomes either the perfect rose in an eternal garden (where the gardener's knees never ache) or part of a consciousness that includes all possible roses and the hands that held them. Paradise photosynthesis might not need carbon dioxide, but it produces something better than oxygen—it makes hope, that strange gas we breathe when the regular air gets too thick with grief.

Maybe that's why we can't shake the feeling that nature continues past death's door, why we plant trees for the dead and bring flowers to stones. Not because we understand the mechanics, but because we can't imagine anywhere worth being that doesn't have rivers laughing over rocks (the way your brother laughed), trees offering shade (like your mother's arms), flowers showing off (proud as any grandchild), and yes, even the quiet miracle of leaves turning light into life, the way suffering sometimes turns into compassion if you wait long enough and water it with tears.

In our bones, in our cells, in the parts of us that remember being stardust and will become stardust again, in the spaces between heartbeats where eternity lives, we know—any heaven worth the name must be green. Green as spring. Green as hope. Green as the world before we learned it could end.
