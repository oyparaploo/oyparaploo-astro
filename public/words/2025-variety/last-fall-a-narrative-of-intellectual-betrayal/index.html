Last Fall ~ A Narrative of Intellectual Betrayal

Words / 2025 Variety

Part One: The Think Tank as Weather System

The Rangmanch Collective's Black Box Theatre wasn't a building anymore. It had become weather—betrayal manifesting as continuous atmospheric condition, each character generating their own climate of deception.

I am the Feminine Shaman, witnessing from shadows cast before light exists. I've been here since before the play began, will remain after it ends. This is the story of how intellectual homes become emotional prisons, how every pristine surface feeds on processed suffering.

The think tank floated seventeen floors above Mumbai, wrapped in smart glass that fogged with proximity to truth. Inside, furniture made of hollow acrylic filled with vapor, books suspended in lucite—readable but untouchable—a conference table of laminated air recording every touch as condensation.

Part Two: The Players Assemble

Jersey's Eternal Present

Jersey Mehta-Singhania arrived wearing ₹3 lakh jackets while "volunteering," speaking exclusively in present tense. Twenty-eight years old, heir to industrial fortune, they existed outside temporal causality—no past guilt, no future hope.

"I am not remembering," Jersey announced to the assembled staff. "I am not anticipating. I am only being."

Their presence immediately disrupted the facility's temporal flow. Clocks stopped agreeing. Minutes lasted different durations in different rooms.

Diop's Calculated Seductions

Diop Mehta, Jersey's uncle/aunt (pronouns shifting with intent), had fucked three board members—strategically, therapeutically, destroying each precisely. Gender-fluid predator oscillating between power modes, they left condensation on windows that never dried.

"All territory is dangerous," Diop explained to their latest conquest. "That's what makes it territory."

Louise's Humid Care

Louise D'Souza, 55, had abandoned law for elder care after her mother Campilla walked into monsoon and never returned. Her caregiving created steam that fogged her own reflection. She moved through the sterile think tank like humidity invading climate control.

"I'm the only one here who touches people without contracts," she said.

Part Three: The Wounds Think

Vingt's Scar Collection

Vingt chose their name at twenty when numbness became worse than pain. The think tank's youngest researcher, they collected emotional scars like credentials, pursuing whoever would damage them most exquisitely.

"My wounds write equations," Vingt explained, tracing patterns on their arms. "They're smarter than thoughts that wound."

They followed Felipe through corridors that shouldn't exist, wanting to learn dissolution.

Felipe's Liminal Architecture

Felipe, the Ambience Consultant, existed primarily in reflections. They mapped the building's psychological architecture through dream corridors, revealing that walls were also doors for those willing to exist in paradox.

"I am them. Was them. Will be them," Felipe said, using pronouns as temporal markers. "My pronoun isn't he or she or they—it's when."

Home E. Da's Death Harvest

Home E. Da, former patient who absorbed 4,000 deaths during empathy enhancement therapy, now harvested final moments. They distributed breadcrumb pills containing the last thoughts of dying patients.

"These aren't drugs," they explained at wellness seminars. "They're concentrated enlightenment."

Their acid-scarred body had transformed into production sites for crystalline weapons—memory shards, empathy spines, clarity fangs growing from wounds that wouldn't heal.

Part Four: The Stillness Incident

Quirky Theo's Explosion

Theo, the Stillness Consultant, had been sitting unmoving for three hours, hadn't blinked in 37 minutes. Their job: reduce motion to increase productivity.

"Motion is anxiety made visible," they said without moving their lips.

Then the shoulder twitched. The head rolled opposite the shoulders. Something inside trying to escape. A kinetic seizure—accumulated motion releasing catastrophically, destroying the conference room in seconds before returning to perfect stillness.

"I was holding the building's scream," Theo explained to the wreckage.

Part Five: The Pronoun Wars

Grammar as Violence

The confrontation erupted in the corridor:

"We need to—" Diop began.

"We?" Vingt challenged. "Or you hiding behind plurality?"

"They say—"

"They?" Felipe materialized from three directions. "Or those you've othered?"

Silence stretched—15 seconds, 30, 45, until Jersey spoke:

"I am—"

But couldn't finish. None of them could complete "I am" without their elaborate constructions collapsing.

Part Six: Campilla's Ghost

The Mother Who Walked Into Monsoon

Campilla D'Souza appeared only to Louise and mysteriously to Adadie, the chaos mathematician. She'd abandoned her family for a radical commune, her betrayal the wound that created Louise's compulsive caregiving.

"You became a saint to punish me," Campilla said in Malayalam that Louise pretended not to understand.

"You became absent to avoid watching," Louise replied.

The ghost mother revealed the ultimate transgression: Louise's law career funded by death benefits, her entire moral foundation built on abandonment's insurance payout.

Part Seven: Mrs. Fernandes's Wisdom

Innocence as Choice

At 93, Mrs. Fernandes carried a broken music box that she wound anyway. When offered Home E. Da's clarity pills, she laughed:

"I'm quite unclear and prefer it that way."

She traced Vingt's scars calling them "punctuation," "little bells." Her pockets held collected wonders—stones, buttons, candies transformed by attention.

"Innocence isn't not knowing," she explained. "It's knowing and choosing to remain tender anyway. Cynicism is gravity—it takes no effort to fall. Innocence is flight."

Part Eight: The Climate Breaks

When Betrayal Reaches Saturation

All accumulated lies reached critical mass. Indoor rain began—not water but every suppressed human fluid returning. Diop's designer clothes dissolved. Equations ran like mascara down walls. Jersey's climate bubble cracked, flooding them with all the time they'd postponed.

"I am denying while confessing," Jersey screamed in ecstasy. "I am desperate while satisfied. This is obscene!"

The revelation: their charity was buying indulgences for ongoing crimes. Their cruelty was forcing others to exist in time while they floated above it.

Part Nine: Adadie's Calculations

The Mathematics of Collapse

Adadie Verma, chaos mathematician, had been hired to stabilize through controlled destruction. They revealed the terrible math:

- Louise and Diop were entangled particles—goodness creating vacuum demanding evil
- Every character's wound was ancient, discoveries mere rediscoveries
- The building itself was wound made architecture

"I calculated the probability of my brother agreeing to die," Adadie confessed. "Made continuing seem selfish, dying seem generous. He died at probability 1.0. Certainty."

Part Ten: The Revelation

What Everyone Knew

The Feminine Shaman emerged from shadows:

"You think this is about individual guilt, but watch closer. The building breathes. The furniture sweats tears. The walls develop stigmata. This think tank is built from crystallized suffering, refined into commodity."

Every beautiful surface—the preserved butterflies in conference tables, the moss walls spelling confessions in chlorophyll—was decay transformed into aesthetics.

"Some of you literally cannot leave," I revealed. "You've been incorporated into the architecture."

Part Eleven: Integration

The Question Transformed

Jersey's question evolved throughout the play:
- "Can one betray a role they never chose?"
- "Is one betraying a role one isn't choosing?"
- Finally: "Is choosing itself the betrayal?"

Each character faced their truth:
- Louise was choosing sainthood to avoid being
- Diop was choosing predation to avoid being
- Vingt chose accumulating wounds to feel real
- Felipe chose existing in all states to avoid choosing
- Home E. Da chose harvesting death to avoid living

Part Twelve: The Ending That Doesn't End

Eternal Rehearsal

The play concluded but the witnessing continued. The audience remained in chairs, growing the same crystalline wounds. The building settled into new weather patterns—shame as humidity, guilt as pressure systems, betrayal as the air everyone breathed.

Mrs. Fernandes wound her broken music box. It played through human voices humming together—the only sound that could escape the think tank's climate control.

"Facts are just the beginning of the story," she said to the water damage. "Innocence is what we write in the margins."

The clearing opened briefly—that space where light bends sideways, where all poisons converge. Through it, glimpses:
- Maya drawing buildings with lungs
- Chijioke flailing in calculated conference rooms
- Jonas documenting which betrayals were environmental, which pharmaceutical
- The laundromat breathing its memories

But the think tank remained, seventeen floors above Mumbai, generating weather from wounds, crystallizing suffering into intellectual property, each character trapped in their chosen betrayal, forever falling toward their own reflection in laminated air.

I watch still from shadows. The play ends nightly. The patterns repeat eternally. Every audience member becomes Jersey, existing outside consequence until their bubble bursts into glorious, guilty time.

The question remains: Is the betrayal in what we didn't choose, or in pretending we're not choosing every moment?

The building knows. It's always known. We're just the transport system for damage seeking damage, pain seeking pain, clarity seeking hosts to corrupt with truth.

---

LAST FALL plays eternally at The Rangmanch Collective, where furniture remembers who sat in it, walls collect condensation from lies, and every exit is also an entrance to the same room. Tickets available in exchange for one authentic betrayal, crystallized and mounted.
