Secrets in the Archive

The Physics of Taking Up Space

The MRI technician asks me to hold perfectly still. "Any movement ruins the imaging," she says. Inside the machine, my mother's brain tumor takes up 47 cubic centimeters—the volume of a fig. It's been doubling every six weeks. At this rate, she has four months before it presses against the speech center.

I'm a sound engineer. I measure volume for a living—decibels, frequencies, the space sound occupies in a mix. But I never understood volume as survival until now.

SCENE 1: The Recording Session

Thursday, 2:47 PM, Studio B

"Say it again, but louder," I tell my mother through the booth glass.

She's reading her mother's fig cake recipe, the one from Calabria. Her voice wavers. Not from emotion—from the pressure of tissue against tissue, growth against boundary.

"Three pounds of figs," she reads. "Never less. The volume matters."

I adjust the levels. Her voice peaks at 73 decibels, down from 89 last month. I do the math: she's losing 4 decibels per week. At this rate, she'll be whispering by October, silent by December.

"Should I read it again?" she asks.

"Perfect," I lie, saving the file as "Mom_Recipe_Take_17_FINAL_REALLY_FINAL.wav"

Dr. Kowalski explained it yesterday: "The tumor isn't just growing. It's displacing. Every cubic centimeter it gains, something else loses."

SCENE 2: The Neurosurgeon's Office

"Think of it like sound in a room," Dr. Kowalski says, pulling up the 3D imaging. "There's only so much space. When one frequency dominates, others get compressed."

The tumor rotates on screen, rendered in blue. It looks like a fig.

"Can you remove it?"

"We can debulk—reduce the volume by 60%. But the margins..." He traces the edges where tumor meets memory, tumor meets language, tumor meets whatever makes my mother herself. "It's not about removal. It's about buying time."

"How much time?"

"With surgery and radiation? Maybe a year of good volume." He means voice. He means presence. He means taking up space in the world.

RESEARCH AS FLESH

Fact: Glioblastomas double in volume every 49.6 days on average.

Transformed: I measure the doubling in mother's margins. Her handwriting, once filling entire pages, shrinks to fit index cards. Her phone calls, once hour-long performances, compress to three-minute check-ins. Even her physical presence seems to contract—she takes up less of the couch, less of the kitchen, as if rehearsing for absence.

Study: MIT research shows the human voice contains 4,000 measurable characteristics unique as fingerprints.

Transformed: I isolate each characteristic in Pro Tools—her glottal fry at 87 Hz, the way she clips consonants when tired, the specific resonance at 2,100 Hz that appears only when she says family names. I save each frequency separately, building an archive of acoustic DNA.

My hands shake as I compress the final mix. Twenty-seven hours of recording condensed to twelve tracks. Her voice reading recipes, singing hymns, telling the story about the time she sang backup for Tony Bennett (a lie, but beautiful). The waveforms look like mountain ranges. I realize I'm looking at the topography of my mother—every peak a laugh, every valley a pause for breath she increasingly struggles to find.

"You're stealing her," my brother says, standing in the studio doorway. "Reducing her to files."

He's not wrong. There's something grotesque about measuring my mother in gigabytes, compressing her to fit on a hard drive.

"At least I'm keeping something," I say.

"You're keeping sound. Not her."

But then Mom's voice comes through the monitors—reading the fig recipe again—and we both stop. Because it sounds exactly like her. Because it sounds nothing like her. Because volume is both presence and absence, taking up space and leaving it empty.

ENDING TURN

The tumor was removed Tuesday. 62% debulked, the surgeon said, proud of the percentage. Mom woke up speaking in volumes—too loud, then too soft, her brain recalibrating space.

At home, I play her the recordings. She doesn't recognize her own voice at first.

"I sound so... full," she says.

The fig tree in her yard is producing. The fruit swells beyond its usual size this year, each one taking up more space than it should. She picks one, holds it to the light.

"Three pounds," she says, remembering the recipe. Her voice is different now—raspier, quieter, but still hers. "Never less."

I hit record on my phone. Because volume isn't just space occupied. It's space insisted upon. It's my mother, reduced by 62%, still filling the kitchen with the story of figs, the weight of fruit in her palm, the sound of a voice that refuses to be compressed to nothing.

The tumor will return. It always does. Doubling every 49.6 days, pressing against boundaries. But for now, there's this: my mother, taking up space. The fig, taking up space. The voice, recorded at 71 decibels, taking up exactly 47 megabytes on a drive labeled "Everything."

Never less.
