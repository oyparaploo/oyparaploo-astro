WE GARDEN (2)

Fig Grove Knows Labor and Speaks Blooming Promise Beneath the Soil’s Top Layers

← Words / 2025 Variety

The fig trees hum—not loudly, not in words, but in a vibration deep enough to settle into the soles of Maélys’ bare feet. The ground stirs beneath her as though something is shifting inside the roots, threading itself upward, unfurling a message too tangled for language.

She presses her palm flat against the dirt.
It breathes beneath her touch.

The fig grove is not patient today.
It does not whisper. It insists.

Sap thickens in its veins. The branches quiver slightly, not from wind but from remembering. The trees know something she does not yet understand—something that pulses beneath the soil in braille she cannot read but can feel.

A ripe fig drops without warning, thudding softly into the earth beside her knee. She does not pick it up. She watches. She waits.

The fig splits itself open.

The scent swells—fermented, sweet, insistent.

And then, beneath the fig’s thick perfume, another smell rises. Something older. Something unearthed.

She exhales.

Somewhere deep beneath her fingertips, the roots shift again.
And this time—
they speak.

The fig trees hum—not loudly, not in words, but in a vibration deep enough to scramble Maélys' balance. She wobbles for a moment, because apparently trees can have opinions, and today they are making theirs known through seismic suggestion.

The ground beneath her is feeling dramatic.

She presses her palm flat against the dirt. It breathes beneath her touch—but in a way that feels just a little passive-aggressive.

The fig grove does not have time for subtlety today. It does not whisper. It insists.

Sap thickens in its veins, branches shiver slightly—not from wind, but from some deep-rooted existential crisis that has nothing to do with weather. The trees know something she does not yet understand, something that pulses beneath the soil like a prophecy spoken through chewing gum and citrus rinds.

A ripe fig drops without warning, colliding with the earth like it meant business. Or like it had a speech prepared but forgot how to start.

She does not pick it up. She watches. She waits.

The fig splits itself open, very dramatically, very much an act of unnecessary theater.

Its scent floods outward, fermented, sweet, reckless—like someone who meant to toast their own brilliance but spilled half the wine down their shirt.

And then, beneath the fig’s thick perfume, another smell rises, uncertain, old, a little frayed at the edges, like an ancient scripture written in crayon and smuggled inside a lunchbox.

She exhales.

Somewhere deep beneath her fingertips, the roots shuffle, reorganize, sigh deeply.

And this time—

they speak.

Not like wisdom handed down in perfect clarity—but more like someone yelling directions from another room while halfway distracted.

The fig trees hum—not loudly, not in words, but in a vibration tangled in the back of Maélys’ throat. It catches there, like a name she should remember but doesn’t, like a scent from a childhood she’s no longer sure belonged to her.

She presses her palm flat against the dirt.

It breathes beneath her touch—but there’s hesitation in it, like something trying to recall whether it still exists in the same way it once did.

The fig grove is not patient today. It does not whisper. It insists.

Sap thickens in its veins, branches twitch slightly—not from wind, but from the exhaustion of trying to hold onto everything all at once. The trees know something she does not yet understand, something that pulses beneath the soil like a document misplaced too many times to still be considered real.

A ripe fig drops without warning, landing with a soft, unimpressed thud, as if announcing its own resignation from the cycle of being known.

She does not pick it up. She watches. She waits.

The fig splits itself open, hesitantly, like it’s trying to remember if it had any choice in the matter.

Its scent spills outward, thick, fermented, clinging—not simply sweet, but desperate, like the last breath of something that does not want to be forgotten.

And then, beneath the fig’s perfume, another smell rises—something old, something faded at the edges, something that might have once been a promise but is now only residue.

She exhales.

Somewhere deep beneath her fingertips, the roots shiver slightly, uncertain whether they are holding onto something real or only the memory of it.

And this time—

they speak.

But the voice catches. Fumbles. Sounds more like a breath let out too late. Sounds more like something that almost existed.

The fig trees hum—not loudly, not in words, but in the kind of vibration you don’t hear so much as recognize in your bones. A warmth. A weight. A presence that says, you’re standing exactly where you need to be.

Maélys presses her palm flat against the dirt.

It breathes beneath her touch—not whispering, not warning, just existing, solid and steady, like a friend showing up without needing an invitation.

The fig grove is not patient today. It does not whisper. It insists.

Sap thickens in its veins. The branches quiver—not in fear, not in uncertainty, but in something closer to laughter, closer to the undeniable pulse of knowing you belong somewhere.

A ripe fig drops without warning, landing beside her knee as if it had been waiting for precisely this moment to arrive.

She does not pick it up. She watches. She waits.

The fig splits itself open—unfolding, expanding, generous, as if to say, see? There’s always more to give.

Its scent floods outward, fermented, sweet, boundless—the kind of perfume that reminds you the world is still full of sweetness, no matter how many times it’s been bruised.

And then, beneath the fig’s thick perfume, another smell rises—older, sturdier, carrying the undeniable truth that even the most forgotten things can still be found.

She exhales.

Somewhere deep beneath her fingertips, the roots shift again—not hesitantly, not reluctantly, but like something stepping forward, like something ready to grow.

And this time—

they speak.

Not in riddles. Not in mysteries. Just in the quiet certainty that something, somewhere, is still waiting to bloom.

The fig trees hum—not loudly, not in words, but in a syrupy vibration, slow and golden, something that glides into the bones like summer heat curling through the window before noon. It is not rushed. It is thick with intention.

Maélys presses her palm flat against the dirt.

It breathes beneath her touch—exhaling like ripened fruit, like warm loam, like something waiting to bloom but perfectly content in the meantime.

The fig grove is not patient today. It does not whisper. It insists.

Sap thickens in its veins. The branches shiver—not with urgency, but with pleasure, with the decadent knowing of something utterly unbothered by time. The trees know something she does not yet understand, something pulsing beneath the soil like a promise written in sun-drenched decay, fragrant with rot, full of generous ruin.

A ripe fig drops without warning, landing beside her knee like the happiest mistake, like a love letter flung from a balcony in the wrong century.

She does not pick it up. She watches. She waits.

The fig splits itself open, its skin giving way like warm laughter, like indulgence, like the slow delight of knowing when to surrender.

Its scent floods outward, rich, fermented, sweet—the undeniable perfume of something having lived well enough to be worth the rot.

And then, beneath the fig’s thick perfume, another smell rises—darker, deeper, full-bodied, unapologetic.

Not ruin. Foundation.

Not waste. Fuel.

Not something discarded. Something returning to where it belongs.

She exhales.

Somewhere deep beneath her fingertips, the roots stretch, swell, sigh—welcoming it all, making room for everything, soaking in the abundance of both bloom and breakdown.

And this time—

they speak.

Not cautiously. Not carefully. Just with the easy certainty of something that knows the nectar is always sweeter where the soil has been willing to feast.

The fig trees hum—not softly, not gently, but like knuckles cracking before a long shift, like hands dunked into cold water before they can blister, like steel worn down by work but never broken. The vibration doesn’t settle into Maélys’ feet—it pushes against them, grinds itself into the soles like calloused wisdom, reminding her that patience is not the absence of force, but the decision to endure it.

She presses her palm flat against the dirt.

It heaves beneath her touch, not in welcome, not in relief, but like something that knows the weight of labor and does not flinch under it.

The fig grove is not patient today. It does not whisper. It insists.

Sap thickens in its veins. The branches clench, crack, reset themselves—not from wind, but from decades of bending under their own weight, knowing exactly how far they can be pushed before they break, and how to keep producing anyway. The trees know something she does not yet understand, something stitched into the rhythm of knees pressing into the earth, the swing of a scythe, the ache of a back that has carried more than it should.

A ripe fig drops without warning, landing beside her knee like a hammer hitting the dirt after too many swings—not thrown, not discarded, just spent.

She does not pick it up. She watches. She waits.

The fig splits itself open, slow, sticky—like hands raw from kneading dough, like fingers cracked from frost, like something that knows how to give even when it is too tired to keep holding.

Its scent pours outward, fermented, sweet, sharp—not delicate, but earned, like sweat dried into cotton, like bread pulled from an oven seconds before burning, like the moment before a muscle finally releases after weeks of strain.

And then, beneath the fig’s thick perfume, another smell rises—deep, loamy, streaked with effort and time, the smell of something that has been worked, buried, reborn, worked again.

She exhales.

Somewhere deep beneath her fingertips, the roots shift again—not in hesitation, but in the certainty of something that has been dug up and put back and dug up again, and does not resist it.

And this time—

they speak.

Not softly. Not gently. But with the voice of something that understands toil is not suffering—it is survival.
