The Last Puppet Show

Words / 2025 Variety

Vashon Island, Washington
August 2024

The community theater floods. Salt water, three feet deep, during king tide.

Eight antique marionettes float face-up in the wings. Their strings tangle in kelp.

---

Day One

MAYA (72, puppet theater director) wades through her drowned theater. Her wife JUN (68) follows with garbage bags.

MAYA: Forty years of shows. Gone.

She lifts a sodden puppet—OLIVIER, the sailor. Hand-carved by her grandmother in 1952. The wood splits.

JUN: Insurance will cover—

MAYA: Insurance doesn't cover "king tide combined with storm surge." Act of God.

JUN: Then we rebuild.

MAYA: With what? I'm seventy-two. My hands shake. Look.

She tries to untangle Olivier's strings. Can't. Arthritis.

JUN: The summer kids need this theater.

MAYA: The summer kids have YouTube.

---

Day Two

Eight teenagers arrive with pumps. Led by LIZZ (17), purple hair, island native.

LIZZ: We're saving the puppets.

MAYA: They're ruined. Wood doesn't survive salt water.

LIZZ: My grandfather was Japanese. You know what they do with broken pottery? Kintsugi. Gold in the cracks.

MAYA: These aren't bowls.

LIZZ: No. They're better.

The kids lay out eight puppets on tarps:
- Olivier (sailor)
- Maud (gardener)
- Privat (merchant)
- Keren (weather woman)
- Peder (skeptic)
- Alela (dreamer)
- Jun (painter)
- Lizz (trickster)

TEEN BOY: Why does the trickster have my sister's name?

LIZZ: Your grandmother made that one. 1987. The year I was born. She knew.

---

Day Three

The teenagers work. They shouldn't know how to restore marionettes, but they do. Like memory in their hands.

MAYA: Where did you learn to restring?

LIZZ: Dreams.

MAYA: Be serious.

LIZZ: I am. We all dreamed the same thing. The puppets teaching us.

Jun brings coffee, watches the kids work.

JUN (to Maya): Remember our first show?

MAYA: "The Inland Island." You painted sets.

JUN: You made me fall in love with you. Through puppets.

MAYA: Puppets are easier than people.

JUN: No. They're the same. Just more honest about their strings.

---

Day Four

The puppets are drying. Changed. Salt-bleached. Cracked but somehow more beautiful.

LIZZ: We need a show.

MAYA: The theater's destroyed.

LIZZ: Outside then. Beach. Full moon tomorrow.

MAYA: I don't have a script.

LIZZ: The island does.

She hands Maya pages. Handwritten. Eight different teenage hands.

MAYA (reading): "The Last Puppet Show: A Ritual for What Survives."

---

The Beach Performance

Full moon. Low tide. Hundred islanders with lanterns.

The teenagers manipulate the puppets. Maya narrates, Jun plays accordion.

MAYA: Eight puppets survived the flood. Salt-changed. Storm-blessed. They remember when this island was actually inland, before the ice melted, before the seas rose.

OLIVIER (through Lizz): I sailed here when there was no here. Just trees touching water.

MAUD (through another teen): I planted the first garden. Salt killed it. I planted again.

PRIVAT: I came for fortune. Found only driftwood. Sold it as art. Got rich anyway.

KEREN: I predicted this storm. Fifty years ago. No one listened.

PEDER: I doubted we'd survive. I was right. We didn't. We transformed.

ALELA: I dreamed of the island before it was island. I dream of it after it's gone.

JUN (the puppet): I painted what I couldn't save.

LIZZ (the puppet): I laughed when the water came. What else could I do?

The puppets dance. Strings catch moonlight.

MAYA: Every ending is a beginning. Every flood is a baptism. Every loss teaches floating.

The teenagers make the puppets bow. The audience doesn't applaud—they hum. Low, wordless, like the sound under the island.

---

After

LIZZ: Will you teach us?

MAYA: Teach you what? You already know.

LIZZ: Not the puppets. The stories. Your grandmother's stories.

MAYA: Those drowned too.

LIZZ: No. They just got salt-cured. Preserved.

Maya looks at the puppets, drying in moonlight. Their cracked faces more expressive than before.

MAYA: Tuesday afternoons. After school.

LIZZ: The theater's gone.

MAYA: The beach isn't.

Jun takes Maya's arthritic hand.

JUN: The underground network survived.

MAYA: What?

JUN: The mycelium under the island. Connects every tree. The flood just made it visible. Look.

In the moonlight, phosphorescence traces root patterns in the wet sand. A map of connection.

MAYA: My grandmother used to say the island was alive.

LIZZ: She was right. It just needed salt water to wake up.

The teenagers pack the puppets carefully. Each puppet wrapped in kelp—not to protect them, but to honor them.

---

Three Months Later

The theater's rebuilt. Smaller. On stilts.

Opening night: "The Inland Island: Remembered."

Maya watches from the back. Her hands too shaky now to work the strings. But Lizz and the others move the puppets like they were born to it.

The story's different now. Not about an island that was. About an island becoming. About things that survive by changing. About puppet strings that become lifelines.

OLIVIER (through Lizz): We paid the price to live here. The price was transformation.

The audience—tourists and locals—lean forward.

In the rebuilt theater, you can still smell salt.

You can still hear water.

The island remembers.

End
