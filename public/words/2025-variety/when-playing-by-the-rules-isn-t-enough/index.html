When Playing by the Rules Isn't Enough

Words / 2025 Variety

Maria Torres works two jobs, sixty hours a week, and still had to choose between fixing her car and buying her daughter's insulin last month. She chose the insulin and took three buses to work until her next paycheck. She's never missed a tax payment, never broken a law, never stopped believing that hard work would eventually pay off. But after fifteen years of playing by every rule, she's further from financial stability than when she started.

Maria's story isn't unique—it's the reality for millions of Americans who've discovered a harsh truth: our laws create a straight path to success, but only for those already near the destination. For everyone else, that same legal framework becomes a maze where working harder somehow leaves you with less. The minimum wage that hasn't kept pace with rent. The tax codes that favor investment income over wages. The healthcare system that bankrupts the very workers who keep it running.

These aren't accidents or oversights. They're laws carefully crafted by those with the resources to shape policy, creating a system that protects wealth rather than rewards work. A purposeful and noble cause for our time is to question and change these laws, to make them more just and suitable for our entire nation. But this transformation demands more than angry posts or passive hope—it requires what every meaningful change in our democracy has always required: real people collecting in real spaces, organizing with exhausted bodies after exhausting workdays, refusing to accept that this is the best we can do.

The powerful count on our fatigue, on Maria being too tired after her double shift to attend city council meetings, on all of us being too overwhelmed to organize. But history shows us something they hope we forget: every law that now protects workers, every right we take for granted, was won by ordinary people who decided that unfairness wasn't permanent. Our democracy has always been a work in progress, shaped by those brave enough to insist it live up to its promises.

Maria's daughter deserves better than choosing between medicine and transportation. We all do. The question isn't whether the system is unfair—we know it is. The question is whether we're ready to do the unglamorous, tiring work of changing it, together.
