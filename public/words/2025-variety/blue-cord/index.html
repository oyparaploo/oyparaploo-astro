Blue Cord (2)

← Words / 2025 Variety

Marion threads the blue cord through the doorknob at 4 AM, when sleep refuses to come. The doorknob that sticks at twenty-three degrees, that everyone in the house learned to turn without discussing it. Her mother's doorknob, removed from the house on Wolseley before the estate sale.

Through the hole where the spindle was, the cord emerges clean. Electric blue against tarnished brass.

She works at her kitchen table, 1247 Pembina Highway, apartment 4B. The cord cost $2.99 at Value Village on Ellice, purchased at exactly 2:17 PM last Tuesday while Gerald was on break. Gerald, who has worked there eleven years and maintains aggressive enthusiasm about other people's purchases. "Oh, this'll be perfect for macramé!" he'd say, and Marion cannot bear it, so she times her visits to his absence.

Next, the cup handle. Broke from its cup December 3rd, a clean break she heard from the living room. The cup went into recycling but the handle stayed on the counter. Seventy-three days before she drilled the hole. The ceramic dust made her sneeze, a violent sound in the empty apartment. Mrs. Chen knocked on the wall to check. Marion didn't answer. Mrs. Chen was watching Jeopardy! in Portuguese that night—Marion could tell by the rhythm. Tuesday is Portuguese, Thursday is Mandarin, Sunday is always French.

The soil comes third. Her mother's last planting, May 15, 2019, three days before the stroke. Marion watched from the doorway as her mother pressed fingers deep into the earth, making wells for morning glory seeds. The soil dried holding those shapes—five finger-width depressions, the pinkie one shallow because of arthritis that her mother never complained about, just worked around, the way she worked around everything difficult.

Marion packs the soil carefully around the cord. Some falls onto the table. She sweeps it into margarine container number 248. She has 247 others in the freezer, subcategorized by cafeteria shift. Containers 1 through 89 from Rhonda who drew smiley faces on the lids. Containers 90 through 178 from the Tuesday volunteer who hummed The Tragically Hip. Containers 179 through 247 from Marcus who always said "There you go, sweetheart" in a way that wasn't creepy, just tired.

The construction debris—a square of tar paper from the Meadows site—still smells of diesel and fresh lumber. Someone wrote "TUES LAST DAY" in Sharpie across one corner. Tuesday they stopped building the condos that would have blocked her view of the river. Not her victory, just economics. The company went under. The machines went silent. She took the tar paper on her evening walk, not theft exactly, not anything exactly.

At 5 AM she adds the garden fragments. Three morning glory seeds that could still grow. She tested one in wet paper towel last week—root emerged after six days, white and searching. Tested another in leftover Tim Hortons coffee because this is Winnipeg and everything runs on Tim Hortons, even seeds apparently. That one died. The surviving seed grows in margarine container 248 now, reaching for the kitchen window.

The cord pierces each object at its weakest point. This matters. Not the strongest point where they could bear the weight, but the place where they're already broken, already vulnerable. She learned this from watching her mother work—always finding the flaw in the wood, the weak spot in the fabric, and working with it rather than against it.

By 6 AM, Marion has created something that shouldn't exist. Objects that were never meant to touch now touching. The doorknob hangs lowest, 0.9 pounds of brass and memory. The cup handle, 0.1 pounds, spins slowly when the heat comes on. The soil, 0.4 pounds, compressed around the cord. The tar paper, 0.2 pounds, with its prophetic message. All suspended from a cuphook rated for five pounds. Total weight: 1.7 pounds. She has weighed everything, keeps the measurements in a notebook from the dollar store.

The toilet runs. The landlord promised to fix it "after spring flooding season." Then "once Mercury's out of retrograde." Then "when my guy gets back from ice fishing." Marion has stopped asking. She's developed an elaborate route through the apartment that avoids the creaky floorboard near the bathroom, the one that sounds exactly like her mother clearing her throat, preparing to say something difficult.

Mrs. Chen from 4C stops by at 9 AM with tea. Looks at the assemblage hanging in the window. "Like jewelry," she says in English, though Marion knows she speaks seven languages. "Like my aunt's earrings from the casino gift shop in Kenora. Beautiful but you're not sure why."

Marion doesn't correct her. Doesn't explain that it's not decoration but something else—evidence maybe, or argument, or just the physical fact that things can be held together even when they want to come apart. The blue cord bright between them like a vein, like the IV line that fed her mother those last weeks, like looking up through water toward light you can never quite reach.

Her daughter texts from Vancouver: "Saw someone here hanging crystals in their window! Is that what you're doing? Should I send crystals? There's a shop here that sells ones for grief specifically???"

Marion sets the phone face-down. Through the west window, she can see the river, and beyond it, the abandoned construction site where she sometimes walks, collecting pieces, timing her visits to avoid the security guard who takes his dinner break at 6:47 PM exactly, who eats the same submarine sandwich from the same place, who reads paperback westerns with broken spines.

In seven hours, at 3 PM exactly, the sun will hit the cord and transform it, make it translucent, make it sing almost, though she would never use that word aloud. The assemblage will shift when trucks pass on Pembina. Three grains of soil will fall. She will sweep them into container 249, which will go in the freezer between container 248 (Marcus, no smiley face) and the bag of frozen peas she's been meaning to throw out since 2018.

For now, she sits with what she has made. Not art exactly. Not memorial exactly. Just things that weren't meant to touch now forced to reckon with each other's weight. The doorknob remembers twenty-three degrees. The soil holds the shape of fingers that knew exactly how deep to plant. The cord holds them all at the precise tension where breaking becomes holding, where holding becomes possible.

Tomorrow she will adjust the tension. Thursday at 9 AM, like always. Tighter and the soil crumbles. Looser and the doorknob drops. She has found the exact point where everything barely continues. Like her mother's last month. Like her own days now. Like the morning glory in container 248, growing toward light it recognizes but has never directly known, next to the seed she killed with coffee in a moment of exhausted experimentation.

The assemblage turns slowly in the morning sun. Blue cord, brass doorknob, ceramic handle, packed earth, tar paper prophecy. Each thing itself but also part of something else now, something that has no name but exists anyway, hanging in a fourth-floor window in Winnipeg, visible to anyone who looks up, though no one does.
