When the Sky Remembers the Breaths We Tried to Hide

← Words / 2025 Variety

LOOP(S)

(Encounter in a laundromat, in the shadow of smog)

The machines churned with a patient hum inside the small-town laundromat—lavender and dryer sheets drifting through vents that strained to hold back the worsening air beyond. A yellowed poster by the front window read “Breathe Easy,” its letters faded, ironic.

Outside, the air was officially toxic. The kind of day when the government recommended indoor sheltering. “Smoke Day,” the locals had started calling it—like a snow day, but without wonder.

Skylar checked the AQI on her phone again: Hazardous. 494. “Basically Chernobyl in a blender,” she muttered to herself. Twenty-six, idealistic, strapped into her pastel N95, and perched atop an old Maytag like it was a hill she’d die on for the environment.

Across the room, Donny plodded in. Fifty-two, HVAC technician, buzzcut under a faded NASCAR cap, one shoulder patched with duct tape. He clutched a Snickers bar and a six-pack of Gatorade that looked like it had been warming in his truck all morning.

“You see the sky out there?” he said, not really looking at her.

“It’s a war crime,” Skylar replied. “The sky shouldn’t be gray at noon.”

Donny gave a dry chuckle, cracking open a Gatorade like it was the key to continued existence.

“I survived three summers tearing apart moldy ductwork with nothin’ but my nostrils and a stubborn attitude. Ain’t scared of a little ash in the wind.”

Skylar blinked. “Donny, that ‘little ash’ is PM2.5—ultrafine particles that get past your lungs, into your bloodstream, into your brain. They cross the blood-brain barrier and mess with your cognition. You might think you’re just forgetful, but that foggy feeling in your head? The one you’ve been ignoring? That’s pollution. That’s cellular distress. Oxidative stress, to be specific.”

Donny paused. “So you’re saying smog made me forget what I came in here for?”

Skylar shrugged. “Basically.”

She hopped off the dryer and pulled out a second mask from her canvas tote.

“Try this—it’s an N95. Not that fabric bandana you’ve been dragging around like it’s your emotional support rag.”

Donny took it, thumbed the edges thoughtfully. “Feels like stealin’ hospital gear.”

“It’s survival gear now,” Skylar said. “You think I wear this thing to look cute?”

“You do look like a stormtrooper who shops organic,” he said.

“Thanks, I think.”

She rattled off tips with the breathless intensity of someone who had read every EPA bulletin twice: stay indoors if you can, drink water to flush toxins, eat berries and greens to fight oxidative aging, use HEPA filters in the house and the car—even a box fan with a furnace filter taped to it could help.

Donny listened, nodding slowly. “Huh. You know all this just from the internet?”

Skylar gave a soft laugh. “From living in this crisis. And also, yeah—from the EPA, research journals, and my friend Javi who sells air purifiers out of his garage.”

He cracked a smile. “Well. Guess there’s more to this apocalypse than duct tape and prayer.”

The conversation drifted—toward trees, weather, clean water, the future. There was no breakthrough moment, no sweeping montage. Just a young activist and an older tradesman sharing filtered air and half a bag of trail mix, joined by something quietly sacred: the belief that breath matters.

They didn’t agree on much.

But in that suffocating afternoon, in a laundromat humming like a tired heart, they decided to take care of the same breath.

The lights went out.

Not all at once—but in stages. One fluorescent tube near the dryer buzzed erratically, its flicker like a trapped fly. Then it blinked off, leaving a faint scent of burned plastic. Around the room, machines staggered into silence, their rhythms cut mid-cycle. The purifier was the last to go. Its gentle hum sputtered once before the quiet settled in.

In that silence, the fog outside felt louder.

Skylar slid down from the dryer, the metal cool beneath her palms. Her breath hissed softly behind her mask. Every muscle tensed, attuned now to what wasn’t there: the electric hum, the airflow, the low drone of the outside world. All of it gone.

Donny stood still beside the vending machine, his hand paused mid-pocket, fingers grazing coins that never made it into the slot.

“Well,” he muttered, his voice dampened by the dark, “either the circuit blew… or something pulled the plug.”

Skylar didn't respond. There was no thunder. No wind. Just a stillness that felt stale, practiced. Like the room had been waiting to be empty.

Then came the knock. Soft. Deliberate.

Three taps.

Then again.

From the front door.

They turned together. Through the glass: nothing. Just fog—dense, unmoving, swallowing the outside parking lot. Not even the orange glow from the bank sign across the street made it through.

A second knock. But not from the door this time. From behind them.

Skylar froze.

The wall on that side held no exit. Only a vent, shoulder-high, covered in aluminum mesh. Barely wide enough for a hand. But now it rattled—lightly, purposefully.

Like breath.

Or worse: like thought.

Donny stepped forward, instinct kicking past logic. Skylar reached out, fingers catching his sleeve. “Don’t.”

He didn’t stop. He leaned toward the vent, gaze narrowing.

“There’s something…” he murmured. His voice drifted off.

Then softly, “Something… saying my name.”

Behind them, the purifier sparked once—just a flicker in the dark. Static cracked against metal. Then the emergency light above the folding table blinked on—low, jaundiced orange. Just enough to paint their reflections onto the glass of the front door.

And in that faint windowlight: a third figure.

Standing between them. Head slightly bowed. Expression obscured.

The light clicked off.

Neither moved.

Skylar’s pulse thumped against her ribs. Not fear exactly. More like familiarity. The wrong kind.

Like whatever was in the room with them wasn’t new. Wasn’t foreign.

It had always been here.

Just… waiting.

In the air.

In the walls.

In the breath they stopped taking for granted.

The fog at the windows shifted—subtle, but closer now. Pressed like hands to glass.

They weren’t alone anymore.

The laundromat hummed with the exhausted rhythm of small machines doing quiet labor. Warm air smelled faintly of lavender and lint. Skylar perched barefoot atop a dryer with chipped enamel, elbows on her knees, legs crossed like a prophet waiting for the end to blow in on schedule. Her N95—powder pink and slightly creased—dampened her breath into soft static.

Outside, the sky had gone the color of boiled asphalt. The kind of light that made dogs whimper and birds disappear. An AQI of 494. Not air anymore, really—more like something leftover. A memory of air. Skylar whispered to no one, “Chernobyl in a blender.”

Then Donny arrived—boots heavy on the scuffed tile, Gatorade clinking like a miner returning from the edge of the mine. He moved like someone who had long ago decided his body could take it. Whatever it was. In his mind, exposure was a paycheck. A cough was just overtime with phlegm.

“You see the sky out there?” he asked, peeling open a Snickers bar like it owed him an apology.

Skylar barely looked up. “It’s a war crime,” she said flatly.

He laughed the kind of laugh that lives somewhere between disbelief and muscle memory. “I’ve inhaled worse.”

She slid off the dryer. “You’ve inhaled dumb, Donny. PM2.5 is fine enough to tango with your bloodstream. Those ‘clouds’ out there? They’re trafficking particles into your neurons. It’s not just smog. It’s strategy.”

He blinked. “So you’re sayin’ the air’s makin’ me stupid?”

“No,” she said. “I'm saying you’ve got help.”

It was banter, but underneath it, something older pulsed—an ache neither of them had words for. A knowing that whatever this air was carrying, it wasn’t just poison. It was memory. It was consequence.

She gave him a proper mask. He tried it on like a borrowed helmet. It made him look like a retired welder hiding from ghosts. Still, he kept it on.

Their conversation wandered into strange little havens: blueberries, filtered box fans, electrolyte balance. They disagreed about almost everything, and still, a softness held between them like shared breath.

And then the lights went out.

Not like a blink, not like an accident. More like something was being allowed. The fluorescents died in a stuttering hush. The machines groaned, then quit. The purifier sighed once and surrendered.

Stillness spread wide across the tiled floor.

Skylar felt her ribs tighten. That kind of silence didn’t happen unless something else was listening.

Donny froze mid-pocket, fingers brushed against coins that no longer had utility.

“Well,” he said, slow and dry, “either the power’s out, or we just got unplugged from the map.”

There was no thunder. No wind. Just fog—pressing thick against the windows like a held breath. And then—

Three knocks. Soft. Measured.

From the door.

They turned at the same time.

No one there. Just smeared glass and haze. The world outside gone ash-grey and hollow.

Another knock. This time behind them.

Skylar’s spine locked.

That wall had no door. Only a vent, lined in mesh. Nothing should’ve been able to knock there. But the grate jittered. Lightly. Like breath. Like intention.

Donny moved before she could stop him.

“There’s something,” he whispered, his mouth close to the vent. “Something… saying my name.”

A flash—white-hot and brief—from the dead purifier.

The emergency light kicked in, dim and sickly orange. It shivered on. Enough to show their shapes in the front window.

And one more.

A third reflection. Standing between them. Still.

Skylar felt a sudden hum beneath her skin, like memory being unearthed.

Then the light went out.

The laundromat held its breath.

Whatever this was, it wasn’t new.

It wasn’t alien.

It was something they'd lived with long before they learned to mask it.

Something in the fog remembered them.

And now, it wanted to be known.

Yes … the laundromat begins to breathe … and what it breathes is memory …

The temperature shifted imperceptibly—less a drop in degrees, more a change in mood, as if the walls had inhaled.

Skylar noticed it first. The smell.

Not lavender.

Not detergent.

Something else.

Something like mothballs and wet insulation, chased by the faint sweetness of long-extinguished incense. It wasn’t drifting in from outside. It was coming up through the floor.

“The hell is that?” Donny asked, pulling his mask tighter, eyes narrowing at the vents.

Skylar didn’t answer.

Instead, she stepped slowly toward the wall of dryers. One of them—number 17, the one with the chipped coin slot and the permanent out of order sticker—was rattling. Just barely. Not enough to hear unless you were already listening.

She was listening.

As her hand grazed the front of the machine, she flinched. The surface was warm.

But the power was out.

“It’s remembering something,” she whispered.

Donny tilted his head like she was speaking in parables. “The dryer?”

“No,” she said softly. “The building.”

The ceiling groaned—a long, low sound that felt structural, but didn’t echo like settling pipes. It was closer. More organic.

More like throat-clearing.

Skylar stepped backward instinctively as a stain began blooming across one of the ceiling tiles—circular, pale brown, oddly geometric. Not quite mold. Not quite water.

“Looks like an old record,” Donny said, voice lower now.

“Or a fingerprint,” she whispered.

There were whispers now. Not clear words—just the sensation of syllables brushing past the edges of thought. Enough to make Skylar remember the stories she’d only half believed. About the fire twenty years ago in the apartments above the laundromat. About the woman who used to bring cinnamon incense with her laundry and fold everything twice to the hum of the spin cycle. About the girl who used to hum lullabies by the change machine.

About how the air didn’t just carry breath—it kept it.

Held it. Let it settle. Waited.

Donny stepped toward the folding table. Then stopped. Slowly, reverently, he reached out and wiped a layer of dust from its surface with his callused thumb.

Beneath it: a handprint.

Tiny. Child-sized. Too fresh.

There hadn’t been a kid in here all day.

The vents exhaled again. Not loudly. Just enough for the edges of their hair to lift.

And in that breath, Skylar didn’t just feel humidity or warmth. She felt reminder.

A presence pressing gently—You breathed here once. You forgot. But I didn’t.

The air was thick with the memory of what went unnoticed.

Of breath unspoken.

Of grief unopened.

And now—slowly, without aggression or malice—the building was beginning to give it back.

Piece by piece.
