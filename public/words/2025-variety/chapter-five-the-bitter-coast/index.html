Chapter Five ~ The Bitter Coast

Words / 2025 Variety

The train curved along the coast like it was tracing a scar. On one side: vineyards clinging to hills in disciplined rows, stone terraces stepping the slopes like patient handwriting. On the other: the Mediterranean, darker than Collioure’s postcard blue, its surface corrugated by a wind that seemed to come from far inland carrying dust and rumor.

Banyuls-sur-Mer gathered itself tightly around a small harbor. Shutters were bleached and bowed; roof tiles held the day’s heat like a memory they didn’t trust anyone else to keep. The air braided two scents—fermenting wine somewhere below street level, and iodine from the sea.

Sound, here, came in singles. Collioure had been an orchestra; Banyuls was a metronome. A halyard knocked a mast, once. A winch turned, once—crrrrk—and rested. Hammers struck wood, measured. Hooves clopped up a stairpath between vines, each step hollow and contained like water dripped through a still.

Libaux Torrini adjusted his collar as if the town itself made him want to sit straighter. “Feels like it’s waiting to be asked the correct question,” he said.

“Or like it knows we’ll get the question wrong,” Troll Tearsen answered.

They crossed the quay toward a café where fishermen were bent over cards. The game made its own small soundscape: the fff of shuffling, the flat tap of a played card, a cough that didn’t quite leave a throat. The owner approached with the steady gait of someone for whom spilled coffee would be a moral failure. He put down two cups. Porcelain met saucer with a clean tik that seemed to measure the room’s temperature.

“You knew Miller Corbal,” Troll said.

The owner’s eyes lifted with the slowness of someone unentitled to surprise. “She came,” he said. “She asked for the caves.” He tilted his chin toward the cliffs that shouldered the town. “She wasn’t afraid of dark places. She wrote in a little book while listening.”

“Listening to what?” Libaux asked.

“To drips. To drips and to herself.” The owner’s mouth turned like he was almost smiling at a child’s determination. “She had a companion the second time. Foreign voice. Shoes that didn’t scuff when they should. He bought sardines and didn’t touch them. He left three coins stacked neat.”

“Name?” Troll asked.

The owner lifted his shoulders. “Men who stack coins do not leave names, Madame. They take them.”

The saucer under Troll’s cup shivered against the table when she set it down. tik-tik. She thought—uninvited—of Nunito Volker’s rigid fingers in Fontanella, the green marble that had refused to be only glass.

They thanked the owner and went toward the cliff path.

---

Terraces climbed in steps of dry stone, the mortarless seams holding centuries together by sheer stubborn geometry. Vines ran in lines that made the hills look disciplined, but the wind gave them away; leaves jittered, insisted. Between two terraces a narrow stairpath rose and then turned out of sight. A mule clopped upward with baskets that clinked from time to time as if the animal were carrying a box of carefully-disordered cutlery.

At the cliff’s lip the path narrowed, and then the first cave took the light from them.

The interior air was colder—not winter-cold, but archive-cold. Sound changed. A single drop fell from the ceiling, and the echo took the room, multiplied itself into a congregation of falling, and then stopped as if embarrassed by its own enthusiasm. Their footfalls on damp rock rolled beneath them. Libaux said “Stremler,” just to see what might happen to the word here. It came back slower, heavy with the cave’s vowel. Untrustworthy.

“Rooms that reshape sound are inconvenient,” Troll said. “And honest.”

They passed crates softened to slumps, rusted tins that had given up their labels long ago, graffiti whose letters had become weather rather than language. In the deepest chamber, Troll’s flashlight found what at first looked like a knot of weed. She pushed the beam closer.

“Net,” Libaux said.

“Marbles,” Troll corrected.

A rotted mesh bag lay tangled among stones, half-calcified with salt. Dozens of glass spheres filled it—clear, green, a red or two, some cracked with white veins like lightning trapped mid-blink. Troll lifted the net; the marbles knocked together with a close, dry choir—tch-tch-tch—muted by cloth and salt.

“Planted,” Libaux said.

“Or returned,” Troll said, the word small in her throat.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket, discreet and indecent in this amplified room. Miyake Coscia: Your mother asked for you again.

She did not reply. The silence after not replying took up more space in the cave than the message had.

They set the net down. Troll watched the smallest crack in one marble catch her light and release it in a thin, weak red. She remembered Meuller’s warning—the marbles are not evidence; they are performance—before she had even seen the sender.

Outside the cave mouth, a figure waited in rubber boots, a bucket forgotten in the crook of his elbow. His face seemed carved by a knife not interested in symmetry. When Troll and Libaux stepped into the gray daylight, he spoke without greeting.

“You’re too late,” he said.

“For what?” Troll asked.

“For the woman who asked before you.” He paused as if counting days. “The teacher. She wrote in her book while she listened. She said a word, like a place that had no sea. Pigalle.”

Libaux glanced at Troll. “Paris,” he said, mostly to the cliff.

The fisherman nodded, once. “She left this place, and a man followed. Shoes that did not like rocks. Voices do not like competition with rocks.” He looked down at his bucket like he had meant to put fish in it at some point. “He had the voice of a man who explains. He explained nothing.”

“Fredda Stremler,” Troll said. Not a question.

The fisherman neither agreed nor disagreed, and somehow made both stances visible with the same small arrangement of muscles near his eyes.

They thanked him and descended.

---

Their hotel room overlooked a lane where voices went past in pairs—people on their way to elsewhere, carrying conversations that sounded like the part of a song that only exists between verses. Troll opened her laptop and saw Mage Meuller’s name in her inbox.

> Fontanella marble—fine scratches inconsistent with incidental wear. Depths vary like a nervous hand. “RIVAGE” is too perfect a breadcrumb to have been found by accident. If you follow the letters, he is leading you, not leaving you anything. Remember: performance.

She read the email twice, as if a second reading might invent a helpful line at the end. Outside, a barrel rolled in some cellar, a hollow thud that found the lane and made a slow line of itself.

A little later, by the harbor, kids played with a flat-faced ball that hopped wrong and made a wooden thunk each time it hit the memorial wall. Names carved into stone took the evening light and refused to let go of it. Libaux sat on the bench and rubbed his thumb along a scar near the base of his ring finger, a gesture Troll had come to recognize as him thinking.

“Miller was trying to hear a thing not meant to be heard,” he said. “Files are the quietest rooms in the world. Maybe she thought the caves would be louder.”

“She wrote Pigalle,” Troll said. She could see the pencil marks in Miller’s precise hand, the way the ‘g’ had a habit of lowering its tail beyond what was necessary. “She knew where the voices collect, even if they don’t belong to who’s speaking.”

A gull clacked its beak twice and pretended to have said something.

Troll’s phone buzzed again. Voicemail—Uma Arouje. She put the phone to her ear.

Uma’s voice was clipped even through the network’s thinning. “Tearsen, this has gone far enough. Vienna is under scrutiny, and you are walking straight through the center of a lens. We need something demonstrable or we need you on a train north. Do not—”

There was an interruption, small, not real—just a sound near the microphone like glass touching wood. Then the message ended without farewell.

Libaux lifted an eyebrow. “Do not…?”

“Finish the sentence,” Troll said.

“Or finish the performance,” he answered, and let the corner of his mouth pretend to smile without quite doing it.

Evening made the harbor violet and then some other color no one admits to liking in public. Nets slapped boats with an offbeat whup. Halyards tried being bells and failed but did it beautifully. A man wheeled two empty barrels down a side lane and they spoke hollow syllables to the ground beneath them.

Troll took the marble net from her bag and held it low so the spheres inside were close to the waterline. The sea fidgeted them; the glass knocked softly in a way that made the sound vibrate along the stone—tch… tch-tch—and then settle. She imagined that the vibrations could be read like braille if the wall had fingers.

“We follow her pencil,” Libaux said.

“We follow his performance,” Troll said.

“Same theater.”

They stayed until the water decided that sound was unnecessary and only breathing mattered. When they rose, the bench made a small complaint like an old man getting up too quickly.

On the walk back, a poster peeled itself off a wall with a dry little sigh and flapped twice before surrendering. Two men at a doorway laughed in a language Troll didn’t understand; the laughter did not need a translation. Somewhere a radio played two adjacent stations at once. The overlap made a third song that had not agreed to be written.

At the hotel door, Troll paused and looked back. The harbor’s lights stippled the water; the water shook them into punctuation. She took out Meuller’s email again as if the bluntness of his sentences could be used like a level to check if the world had been installed correctly.

On the screen, the cursor blinked after the last word—performance—like a metronome that had lost interest in time but continued out of duty. Troll closed the laptop, slid the marble net inside the suitcase, and pressed her palm to the lid as though it might feel like a pulse.

Libaux stood with one hand on the corridor’s railing, listening to the building whisper itself into sleep—pipes contracting, a hinge clearing its throat, a floorboard deciding whether to creak now or later.

“Paris,” he said.

“Pigalle,” she said.

And the town—metronome-tight, barrel-hollowed, mule-stepped—kept time for a few beats more and then, courteously, let them go.
