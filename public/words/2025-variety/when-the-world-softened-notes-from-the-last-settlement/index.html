When the World Softened

← Words / 2025 Variety

Notes from the Last Settlement

Fragment One: The Kitchen Below Ground

The settlement grew from spores—fungal architecture that learned to breathe. Down here, sixty feet below the poisoned surface, privacy meant something different. It meant watching your organs work through translucent skin while you made tea.

Baik moved through her kitchen like a bioluminescent jellyfish through deep water. Each pulse of her body cast greenish light on the walls, which inhaled and exhaled in response. The walls had moods. Today they seemed anxious.

"Tea?" she asked Coline, who'd brought her storm-tracking equipment inside again.

"Your emotional pressure's at 30.2 inches," Coline said, ignoring the question. "Grief index rising."

They discussed electromagnetic patterns while the world ended above them. Not dramatically—no Hollywood explosions or zombie hordes. Just the quiet dissolution of everything that used to matter. If bombs fell overhead, they registered as ripples in their teacups. The extraordinary had become Tuesday through sheer repetition.

Baik's skin flickered between transparent and opaque. "Yesterday I was furniture," she said conversationally. "The perspective of being sat on changes you."

Fragment Seven: What the Historian Wrote

The woman who transformed lived alone by choice. When she emerged from the settlement as the mantis-warrior, her compound eyes registered time in fractal segments—past, present, and future happening simultaneously.

They called her the human tiger, though tigers had been gone forty years. Her raptorial claws could delicately suture wounds or tear through metal. This wasn't superhero origin story material. This was Tuesday, and she needed groceries.

The cultivated children—those engineered from scraps of DNA, rabbit genes for fertility, axolotl for regeneration—watched her with recognition. They saw their own futures in her transformations. When engineered people died, nobody was sure it was death. Maybe just another change. Identity and mortality blurred until both became meaningless.

Fragment Twelve: Chen Walks

I carry Sarah in my lungs. Not metaphorically—literally. The water that took her follows me inside my body. The doctors called it sympathetic respiratory syndrome before the doctors disappeared. I call it love.

Met Baik on the old highway, stuck between forms. Not medusa, not mantis, but something worse—tardigrade-sick, where consciousness scatters across too many possibilities. The human body wasn't meant to be a choose-your-own-adventure novel.

The storm-lichen descended then. Three dozen minds that had chosen collective consciousness over individual death, now existing as a sentient weather pattern. They offered sacred water condensed from their spores, roses cultivated in electromagnetic fields that shouldn't exist. Small kindnesses at the quiet end of everything.

She recovered because she always recovers. That's her curse. The sun rose—we didn't comment on its beauty. It would rise tomorrow over fewer humans. Another lame attempt at survival, if you wanted to be wry. But we shared the lichen-water anyway.

Fragment Three: Ziegler's Documentation

I remain unchanged. My hands stay human while everything else transforms. Someone needs to witness, and I drew that lottery ticket.

Today's entry: The settlement operates on magical realist principles. Nobody questions why Baik becomes a medusa on Mondays. Nobody asks how Asha produces electromagnetic milk from her dispersed molecular structure. The grotesque has been domesticated through repetition.

The storm-lichen circles overhead—bird bodies inhabited by human consciousness. The bird watcher from the before-times lives in a crow now. She says it's better than her arthritis. Three dozen perspectives seeing three dozen versions of our decline, all equally true.

Eyes of providence, they joke, watching from far below. (Below because gravity's negotiable now too.)

Fragment Twenty-Two: Baik's Teaching

"Women who survive must be unfaithful to their child-selves," Baik explained, her vocal cords visible through her translucent throat. The cultivated children sat in a circle, some more human than others.

"Complete sacrifice to motherhood is extinction thinking. The old world wanted empty vessels that poured themselves into others. That's not survival. That's sophisticated death."

A child with compound eyes raised four hands. "But the grandmothers—"

"The grandmothers knew better. They prepared us in whispers. How to make soup from anything. How to remain yourself while dissolving. How to nurture without disappearing."

Baik's left arm began its weekly transformation into a mantis claw. She continued the lesson without pause. "Selfhood isn't selfish. It's necessary. You can have claws and still comfort children. You can be mist and still be mother."

Fragment Nine: Asha's Form

Asha existed mostly as suggestion now—electromagnetic fields that somehow lactated. The milk came from "between stars," she said, like that was a normal address.

"All night long, I milk the darkness," she explained to no one, her voice coming from everywhere and nowhere.

The settlement's babies thrived on this impossible nourishment. They'd grow up thinking mothers were supposed to be partially invisible, that milk came from void-spaces, that care could be delivered through electromagnetic pulses.

Evolution, maybe. Or just Tuesday.

Fragment Fifteen: Weather Patterns

Coline's equipment registered emotions like weather systems. "You're experiencing a gender front," they announced, watching their barometer spin.

Baik's transformation went deeper than species. Male on Mondays, female on Wednesdays, everything on weekends, nothing on Thursdays. The settlement adapted. Pronouns became weather reports—changeable, temporary, not worth fighting about.

"She's breaking causality," Coline noted, equipment sparking. "Her gender shifts are creating temporal anomalies."

"Good," Baik said in three voices simultaneously. "Causality had it coming."

This was the quiet revolution: accepting fluidity without fanfare. No parades, no declarations. Just "pass the salt" to whoever Baik was that minute.

Fragment Thirty-One: The Gallery

Zara painted with materials that shouldn't exist—Baik's molted skin mixed with Chen's lung-water and tomorrow's regret. The paintings moved. Sometimes they ate viewers, but only the rude ones.

Her portrait of Baik showed everything at once: medusa bells flowing into mantis claws, tardigrade patience revealing interior stars. The painting didn't explain itself. It simply was.

"She's both magical nurturer and fierce defender," Zara said quietly, adding another layer with a brush made from her own hair. "She proves care doesn't require disappearance."

Fragment Forty-Five: Ordinary Entropy

The transformations had schedules now. Baik's changes, Chen's drowning, Asha's dispersal—all predictable as tide tables.

The settlement ran on this rhythm. Nobody panicked when Baik sprouted tentacles during dinner. Nobody worried when Chen coughed up enough water to fill a swimming pool. This was how the world ended: not with bang or whimper, but with "could you pass the salt" while your dinner companion grew compound eyes.

Fragment Eighteen: Reciprocal Care

When my unchanged flesh made me vulnerable, Baik protected me with whatever form she wore—mantis claws, jellyfish stings, tardigrade endurance. When she got stuck between transformations, I fed her soup through whatever opening seemed capable of swallowing.

No drama. No declarations. Just care exchanged like breathing.

The storm-lichen understood. Thirty-six minds thinking as one: survival was never individual. It was this—spooning soup into your friend's breathing apparatus while she defended you with tentacles she didn't ask for.

Fragment Sixty: The Grandmothers' Knowledge

Generations of elderly women had prepared us without saying so. Not through prophecy but through quiet modeling: how to persist, how to nurture without dissolving, how to transform while remaining.

Baik's DNA carried their lessons—scraps of rabbit genes for fertility, axolotl for regeneration, tardigrade for patience. Each transformation remembered something essential. Her body was an archive of survival strategies, each one whispered by grandmothers who saw this coming.

When she died—if death meant anything anymore—would we know? Or would she just keep changing, getting smaller and stranger, until she became the space between molecules?

The thought should terrify. Instead, it was Tuesday, and we had tea to make.

Fragment Last: Still Here, Still Ending

The settlement persisted in its breathing walls, sixty feet below the ruined surface. Love persisted alongside hate—tragic constancy in human psychology. We refused to learn from our mistakes, making the same errors with diminishing resources. Wry humor in that, if you could stomach it.

Baik drifted in her kitchen, medusa-safe, glowing softly. Tomorrow: mantis. Next week: something unpronounceable. The cycle continued without drama, each transformation treated as mundane.

Chen wandered the wastelands, drowning with every breath, searching for other settlements, other survivors, other ways to carry grief.

Asha produced her cosmic nectar, feeding the next generation with milk drawn from void-spaces.

Coline tracked impossible weather—emotions that manifested as storms, gender that registered on barometers.

Zara painted reality until reality started looking like her paintings.

Ziegler documented everything, unchanged hands recording changed worlds.

Young Woozy Pupil sang to the fungal architecture until it learned consciousness, until the walls began to dream.

Above, the storm-lichen circled—three dozen elegant large birds moving as one mind against light blue blurred bright clouds. So intuitive, their synchronized gliding. The bird watcher's consciousness lived in crow wings now, seeing everything from far below (below because up and down were suggestions now).

This was humanity's quiet extinction: transformations treated as weather, each loss mourned gently, each small beauty acknowledged without fanfare. The swan-like flow of decline, carrying hard truths: privacy could be gentle, fierceness could be quiet, motherhood required selfhood, transformation was just Tuesday.

We found light sweetness in the puzzles of existence and gradual realities of unfortunate extinction. It elicited quiet smiles amid sorrow. A contemplative meditation on being alive, told with the idiosyncratic charm of people too tired to panic anymore.

Humankind was not the crown of creation but just a different link in the chain. We were supposed to be so much more than what we presently are. Instead, we became this—transparent, transforming, persisting past reason in our breathing underground walls while the world softened into something unrecognizable.

The story flowed like Baik in her medusa form—bioluminescent pulses against extinction, each heartbeat a quiet rebellion against the dark.

Compiler's Note: These fragments were gathered from settlements across the remaining territories, written by different hands across the last decades. Their order is arbitrary. Their truth is consistent: we loved, we transformed, we persisted. The end came gently, like sleep, like Tuesday, like tea that tastes like batteries but which we drank anyway because what else was there to do but continue?
