Night Shifts and Trade Wars

Night Shift

The Basquiat wasn't a Basquiat. Just some art student's copy. Still better than most of what hung in Gallery F, which nobody visited anyway because it was too far from the bathrooms.

Wrack had been cleaning the Contemporary Museum for seven years. Seven years of the same route: Contemporary to Modern to Ancient, then back. Eleven fifty an hour. Her daughter Luna was nine now. Wrack saw her every other weekend because night shifts meant "unstable environment" in the custody arrangement.

Into the cart it went. The fake Basquiat. Not stealing—it was trash. Just... keeping.

"What're you doing?"

Hinge. Security guard. Night shift, like her. He had an MFA from RISD, told everyone about it like it was a wound.

"Nothing."

"That's from the education studio."

"It's trash."

"So?"

"So it's trash."

He looked in her cart. Broken frames. Paint tubes twisted like dead things. Those wooden mannequins artists use, missing limbs. Weeks of collecting she hadn't meant to do.

"You building something?"

"No." Then: "I don't know."

Gallery C was supposed to be under renovation. Since 2019. But there was no money, just signs saying "Pardon Our Appearance While We Improve Yours!" Wrack had been putting things there. First just overnight—I'll throw it away tomorrow. Then tomorrow became next week. Then next week became this.

"Jesus Christ," Hinge said when she showed him.

The room was. It was something. Broken sculptures leaning on cafeteria napkins. Torn canvases under donor pamphlets nobody read. A pyramid of wine cups from openings, lipstick stains like tiny screams.

"I know it's—"

"It's fucking beautiful," he said.

It wasn't. But also it was. But mostly it wasn't. But—

"Help me move this." She was dragging something heavy. Part of an installation that sold for $400,000 last year. The part that broke during shipping. Insurance paid out. Artist didn't want it back.

They worked until 5 AM. Not talking much. Moving things. Piling things. Making something that wasn't art but wasn't not art but wasn't anything really except what happens when you save everything everyone else throws away.

Peal found them Tuesday. Or Wednesday. Days blurred on night shift. She did data entry, tracking donations from people who wrote off more than she made in a year.

"What... what is this?"

"Nothing," Wrack said. Then: "Everything nobody wants."

Peal laughed. Bad laugh. The kind when you've been up since 4:30 to get your kid to before-school care that costs extra. "Can I—can I add something?"

Three of them. Then more. Gift shop workers who sold forty-five dollar t-shirts that cost three dollars to make. Shipping guys who handled art worth more than they'd make in ten lifetimes. All of them invisible. All of them there.

They'd meet after midnight. Not planned. Just happened. Moving trash to Gallery C, then D, then E. The museum had forty-seven galleries. Six were theirs now. Nobody checked those galleries. Too far from the entrance where the donors gathered.

"My kid," Peal said one night. She was arranging receipt papers on the floor. Maybe a pattern. Maybe just tired. "My kid told her class I'm an artist because I work at a museum."

Nobody answered. What was there to say?

The pink slips started appearing in week five. Budget cuts. Positions eliminated. But the director's assistant's assistant still made six figures.

"Put it in," Wrack said when Mélodie brought hers.

"Where?"

"Anywhere."

They made something from the pink slips. Looked like a flower. Or a wound. Or nothing.

Dr. Pierce found them eventually. The director. Yale PhD. Nobody said her salary out loud but everyone knew.

"What is this?"

Gallery C didn't look like a gallery anymore. It looked like what happened when you saved everything a museum threw away for eight weeks. Or eight months. Time was weird on night shift.

"It's what we do," Wrack said. She was tired. Luna had asked on the phone why Mommy was never home for bedtime.

"You're janitors."

"Yeah."

"This has to stop."

"No," everyone said. Not together. Just overlapping. Messy.

Pierce brought lawyers next time. Or administrators. Same difference.

"You're trespassing."

"We work here."

"Not in these galleries."

"We work in all the galleries. Every night. Cleaning your—"

"You're fired."

"All of us?"

Silence.

"Who's gonna clean the toilets for the gala next week?" Hinge asked.

More silence.

Channel 7 came. Pretty reporter. Clean clothes. Called it "guerrilla art" and "protest installation."

"It's not art," Wrack told her. "It's just garbage we didn't throw away."

"But what's your message?"

Wrack thought about Luna. About her knees that needed surgery. About eleven fifty an hour in a building full of millions.

"We has—" She stopped. Started again. "We have—" No. "We has recognized."

"I don't understand."

"Yeah."

They're still there. Still adding to it. Twelve galleries now. Or fifteen. Nobody counts. The museum pretends it isn't happening. Closes those galleries for "maintenance" during visitor hours. But at night, they're there. Adding garbage. Adding pink slips. Adding themselves.

Wrack was in Gallery C last Tuesday. Or Wednesday. Days blur.

"We did something," she said to nobody.

"We is something," Hinge said. He was arranging broken frames into something that wasn't anything.

"We has," Peal added. She was crying. Or laughing. Both.

The fake Basquiat is still there. Pinned to the wall with a hundred other fake masterpieces. Or real ones. Who could tell anymore? Who cared?

Eleven fifty an hour.

Seven years.

Every night.

Still there.
