What You Say, How You Say It

Finding Your Authentic Voice

← Words / 2025 Variety

The Cereal Box Delivery

Artists with incredible work die on stage reciting memorized text like reading ingredients. "This. Piece. Represents. My. Journey. Through. Grief." Every word carries identical weight and pace while their painting screams with emotion. Somewhere between heart and mouth, feeling dies.

This happens when people focus on getting words right—hitting all their points—forgetting that delivery moves people. Nobody remembers exact words about your art. They remember how you made them feel discussing it.

The Voice Catch

I watched a sculptor whose voice caught when discussing clay, as if still touching it while speaking. Couldn't tell you specifics she said, but that involuntary moment where emotion broke through made me want everything she'd made.

You can't fake these moments. Our brains detect authenticity in voices—evolutionary wiring to identify trust. When someone performs their artist statement, our primitive brain knows something's off. They're saying words about passion while their voice says "I've memorized this please let me get through it."

Rediscovering, Not Repeating

Artists at fairs explaining the same piece repeatedly face actors' challenge—keeping it fresh doing the same show nightly. The secret: find something different each time. Focus on texture this telling, color next, the memory of making it after that.

Your voice naturally changes when thinking versus reciting. Micro-pauses appear when considering something. Little tone lifts emerge with genuine excitement. People hear this difference instantly, even unnamed.

Weight Becomes Weight

Sometimes overwhelming emotion serves the work. A ceramicist broke down explaining bowls made while her mother died. She barely spoke, but everyone bought one—not from pity but because her voice's weight became the objects' weight.

Rhythm matters too. Speech pace can mirror work—fast and choppy for anxious pieces, slow and flowing for meditative ones. A painter naturally sped up discussing chaotic abstracts, slowed for landscapes. Her voice synced unconsciously with the work.

The Polite Wall

Your voice should have opinions about your art. Get excited about certain parts, protective about others. When someone misinterprets work, an edge enters—not aggressive but firm. "Actually, that's not what this piece is about" spoken kindly but with tone that doesn't invite argument sets boundaries.

Code-switching between gallery voice, studio visit voice, artist talk voice isn't fake—it's adaptive. As long as they're versions of you, not characters played. Gallery voice might be polished but should sound like you on a good day, not pretending to be someone else.

The Considering Voice

Presence beats perfection. Being actually there in conversation, not delivering pre-loaded content. Start by really listening to questions before answering—actually listen, don't await cues for prepared responses.

The considering voice has texture, variation, discovery moments. The reciting voice is smooth but dead. Better alive and rough than dead and smooth.

When what you say and how you say it sync—that's when people don't just hear you but feel you. That's when art actually lands.
