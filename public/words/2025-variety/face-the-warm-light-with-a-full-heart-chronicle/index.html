Face the Warm Light With a Full Heart ~ Chronicle

Words / 2025 Variety

Part I: The Weight of Morning

Every morning, somewhere, a hand reaches for a phone before feet touch the floor. The screen lights up with numbers—how many hurt, how many lost, how many angry. The heart speeds up. The day hasn't even started, but already it feels heavy.

This is the particular exhaustion of our moment—watching cycles of violence become normalized through repetition, where weaponized chaotic and organized human on human violence becomes not an aberration but a predictable pattern. The distinction matters: both the random eruptions of harm and the systematic architectures of oppression that enable them. Each morning brings the question of which sources to trust, which voices deserve amplification, which narratives serve power rather than truth.

But this morning, something small happens. The hand pauses. Sets the phone aside. Feet find the floor first instead.

Thousands of people make this same promise to themselves—they won't check their phones right away. Studies show most last about four minutes. The ones who make it longer usually cheat by checking the time, which means opening the phone, which means seeing the notifications anyway. Those who actually make it to breakfast first act like they've climbed Everest. They'll feel superior for exactly three minutes, then lose two hours reading about something that upset them in 1997.

Someone wakes up at 6:30. Their phone is charging on the nightstand. This morning they decide to wait until after breakfast. They make it four minutes before checking the time. So much for that plan. The notifications are already there, glowing. But they close it again. Small victory.

Part II: The Kitchen Negotiations

In kitchens everywhere, someone is discovering they're out of milk right after pouring coffee. In another kitchen, water boils for tea. Outside the window, a bird builds a nest, one twig at a time. The bird doesn't read the news. It just builds.

Someone else is scrubbing at a mark on a fork that they'll eventually realize is a permanent scratch. This same fork has been confusing people in this household since 2019. For about five minutes, they're not thinking about anything except trying to scrub off something stuck to a fork that turns out to be just a scratch in the metal they've been trying to clean for months. Yet no one throws it away. It's become part of the landscape.

The warm water from washing dishes feels good. During these five minutes, there's nothing except the dishes. This simple act—looking at something real, doing something concrete—changes the rhythm of breathing. Slower now. Deeper. The meditation teachers charge hundreds for what dishwater provides free.

While the coffee brews (forgotten milk, drunk black), or while the tea steeps, hands pick up a pencil and paper. Not to write important thoughts or make big plans. The notebook industry stays in business partly because of morning list-makers. Common entries include appointments (often with the wrong time that gets corrected later), relatives' birthdays (frequently requiring a quick Google search because they can never remember if it's the 14th or 15th—it's the 14th), and "buy milk" or "buy almond milk" (which appears on the same list three days running).

By Thursday, the handwriting has degraded into hieroglyphics. "Dental appt" could mean this Tuesday or next Tuesday. But the act of writing, even badly, matters.

Some spend ten minutes sketching their coffee mug. They're not artists. The drawing doesn't look particularly good. The handle looks like a weird ear. They keep going anyway. But focusing on getting the proportions right requires concentration that pulls attention into the present. This isn't art—it's attention. Looking at something real, making something new.

Part III: The Information Reckoning

At 7:30, they properly check their phone. There are seventeen new emails and multiple news alerts. Five emails are from the same company about a sale they don't care about. They unsubscribe, though they did this last month too and somehow they're back. Like the scratched fork, some irritations are permanent.

They read through the emails systematically, deleting what doesn't need response, flagging what does. One news article is about local budget cuts. Another is about a conflict overseas. The headlines shout, but shouting doesn't make something more true. They start reading comments, catch themselves, close the app. Not today.

Later, when it's time to learn what's happening in the world, the approach is different. Like a doctor checking symptoms—careful, purposeful, not panicked. We're drowning in content while thirsting for clarity. The challenge isn't just identifying outright falsehoods, but navigating the more insidious distortions: the selective framings, the manufactured controversies, the way legitimate concerns get amplified into panic or minimized into irrelevance.

Sometimes the most important stories speak quietly. A community garden that started in an empty lot. Students teaching grandparents to use computers. Someone who changed their mind about something they'd believed for years. These stories don't trend. They don't get clicks. But they're happening everywhere, all the time.

Part IV: The Walking Meditation

Doctors say take a walk for your mental health. This is recommended by doctors for both physical and mental health. What they don't mention: roughly one in ten people doing this will realize partway through they're wearing mismatched shoes—one gray sneaker, one black. They're similar enough that probably no one notices. The mismatched shoes have never mattered to anyone except the person wearing them.

You'll spend half the walk composing arguments against people you'll never actually confront. On the walk, they see the same houses they always see. A neighbor is putting out trash bins. They nod at each other. Both of them do that polite smile-grimace that isn't quite either. Neither of you knows if you're supposed to do this every single time or if once a week covers it. This is the extent of their interaction, but it registers as human contact. This counts as social interaction.

The polite grimace-smile exchanged between neighbors who don't know each other's names happens approximately 3 million times per day in suburbs worldwide. Some version of this happens whether the shoes are sneakers or sandals, whether it's a walk or a bicycle ride, in apartments, houses, different countries, across economic levels.

Someone opens a window. Fresh air moves through a room that had grown stale. A neighbor walks by with their dog. They wave. This too is news—that people still wave, that dogs still wag their tails, that morning still comes no matter what happened yesterday.

Part V: The Work Hours

Back home, they work. During lunch, instead of reading news on their phone, they eat while looking out the window. Someone is eating leftover pasta while watching a utility crew fixing something across the street. At any given moment during work hours, someone is eating lunch while watching a utility crew where one person digs while three others supervise. One worker is standing around while three others dig. This seems about right for most group projects. This ratio has been consistent across all cultures and time periods where utilities have existed. This isn't inefficiency. This is universal law, like gravity or losing one sock.

This is also what's happening in the world—people maintaining infrastructure, doing their jobs. The world runs on people doing ordinary things ordinarily.

Group texts are 30% "did you see that article about—" followed immediately by "can we please talk about anything else." Text conversations regularly include the phrase "let's talk about literally anything else."

Their friend texts: "Did you see that article about—"
They reply: "Let's talk about literally anything else."
The friend sends back: "Fair. I found a recipe for banana bread that uses mayo."

They discuss this for ten minutes. Neither of them bakes. The topic that follows is often baking, even between people who don't bake. No one knows why banana bread specifically comes up so often. Or there's a debate about whether that actor was in that other thing. (They weren't.)

Part VI: The Evening Rituals

That evening, they call a friend. They talk for fifteen minutes about normal things—a recipe that worked well, a problem with a leaking faucet, plans for the weekend. Neither of them mentions the news. Both of them have read it. This conversation is a deliberate choice to talk about something else.

They water a houseplant they've been neglecting. It's somehow both dying and growing a new leaf. Plants are confusing. Every houseplant is both dying and sprouting new growth. Houseplants in various stages of decline are simultaneously growing new leaves in millions of homes. Ask a plant person to explain this and they'll say something about nitrogen. Botanists can explain this. Plant owners cannot. They don't know either. They're just guessing. The plant survival rate hovers around 60%. Whether it's a succulent or that unkillable pothos everyone swears by, whether it's a cactus instead of a houseplant—the confusion remains universal.

In the evening, hands that scrolled through tragedy now hold a pen, a paintbrush, a musical instrument, another person's hand. They make something—maybe just dinner, maybe just a joke that makes someone laugh. Creation is the antidote to destruction, even in the smallest doses.

Part VII: The Death Obsessions and Living Insistences

There's a particular modern malady in becoming so fixated on cataloging decay that we lose sight of what we're trying to preserve or build. The "death obsessions" that don't solve problems and are not at all useful—how crisis can become addictive, how we can mistake doom-scrolling for activism, confusing awareness of problems with working toward solutions.

For all news station reported crimes, there are the perceivably honest individuals who lie to everyone and themselves. This cuts to the heart of our epistemic crisis—not just that people lie, but that they've convinced themselves their lies serve some higher truth. The self-deception may be more dangerous than conscious manipulation because it's so much harder to combat. How do you reach someone who genuinely believes their distortions are clarity?

But drawing with full concentration emphasizes life over any growing death obsessions. The True Facts podcast episodes, the concentrated drawing—these aren't mere distractions but acts of resistance. They're ways of asserting that engagement with reality doesn't require surrendering to its darkest currents. It's choosing creation over consumption, specificity over abstraction.

Part VIII: The Night Negotiations

Before bed, they try to write one thing that went well. This is a practice suggested by psychologists. Research shows it can improve sleep quality and general mood when done consistently. That journaling thing therapists recommend—write one good thing from your day.

Today they write: "Finished the project report" then cross it out because they actually only finished most of it. They write "Remembered to eat lunch." This seems to count. Common entries include "the car started," "found matching socks," "answered that email from last week." Someone writes "Plant grew new leaf." Apparently this counts. Researchers say this still counts. The bar is exactly where it needs to be.

They charge their phone in the kitchen instead of the bedroom. This is a small decision that sleep specialists recommend. It means tomorrow morning, they'll have to get up to check it, creating a natural buffer between waking and consuming information. Then they remember they use it as an alarm clock. They bring it back to the bedroom but put it across the room on the dresser. Compromise.

The old "charge your phone in another room" sleep trick fails the moment you remember you need it for your alarm. The phone-in-another-room sleep strategy has a 50% failure rate due to alarm clock needs. The compromise—phone across the room—has a 100% rate of requiring at least one midnight check to confirm the alarm is set.

At 10:45, they get up to check that they actually set the alarm. They did. It always is. The person checks anyway. While they're there, they quickly check—no, they close it. Maybe just the weather for tomorrow? Okay, just the weather. Weather apps receive their highest traffic at 10:45 PM from people who said they weren't going to look at their phones before bed. The weather hasn't changed since they checked at dinner. They check anyway.

Part IX: The Patterns and Variations

These aren't failures of willpower. This is just what happens when regular people navigate regular days. These are ordinary actions interrupted by ordinary failures. The person is trying. Sometimes it works, sometimes it doesn't. They know about the world's problems but also forgot to buy milk again. Both of these things are true at the same time.

These patterns repeat everywhere. In apartments, houses, different countries, across economic levels. The details vary—tea instead of coffee, bicycle instead of walk, cactus instead of houseplant—but the basic shape remains: people trying to manage their relationship with information, succeeding partially, continuing anyway.

Tomorrow will bring tiny variations. Someone new will discover they've been using the wrong coffee maker setting. They've been pressing the wrong button on the coffee maker since they bought it. Someone discovers they've been using the wrong setting on the coffee maker for three years. Someone else will successfully wait until after breakfast to check their phone, feel proud, then immediately fall into a two-hour scroll.

Tomorrow will be similar. Small choices. Regular tasks. Small intentions, partial successes. Moments of attention to immediate surroundings. The discovery that they've been using the wrong setting on the coffee maker for three years. This is what sustaining yourself looks like. It's messy and imperfect and includes a lot of "good enough."

Part X: The Sum of Light

"Begin Again" suggests both exhaustion and determination—the need to repeatedly reset, to find fresh reserves of hope despite accumulated disappointments. Each morning becomes a choice: whether to approach the day with curiosity or dread, whether to seek connection or retreat into cynicism, whether to trust that collective action can still bend the arc toward justice even when individual actions feel impossibly small.

When the difficult news comes—and it always comes—it lands differently now. Not on an empty stomach and a racing heart, but on a foundation of something steadier. The problems are still real. People still hurt each other. Systems still fail. But alongside these truths live other truths: People also help each other. New solutions get discovered. Someone who fell down yesterday stands up today.

This is how most people actually cope. Not through grand gestures or life-changing revelations, but through small, deliberate choices about where to put their attention. They stay informed without being overwhelmed. They participate in life while knowing about its difficulties.

The world needs people who stay informed. But it also needs people who stay whole. Who remember that despair is not a requirement for caring. Who know that taking care of their one small corner helps the whole picture, even when they can't see how.

Every morning is a chance to begin again. Not because yesterday didn't matter, but because today still does. The hand reaches out—but this time, it reaches for something that builds rather than breaks. One choice. One morning. One person remembering that they're not just a witness to the world, but a participant in it.

And in that participation—small, ordinary, barely noticed—lives the quiet revolution of people who refuse to be paralyzed, who insist on adding their own light, however modest, to the sum of light.

Part XI: What Continues

This is what maintaining equilibrium looks like at scale. Not perfection, not failure, just millions of small negotiations between intention and reality, repeated daily, getting incrementally better or at least not notably worse.

The milk gets purchased eventually. The plant survival rate hovers around 60%. It'll get bought when it gets bought. The fork still has that scratch. The mismatched shoes have never mattered to anyone except the person wearing them. The plant has grown another confusing leaf. The bird completes another row of twigs. The tea is ready. The coffee is drunk black.

This is the part no one posts about, writes about, makes documentaries about. But it's most of what actually happens. Neither person is doing it wrong. Both outcomes are normal. The world continues.

Tomorrow morning, the same choice will appear. The phone will wait by the bedside, full of urgent messages about things that can't be fixed before breakfast. But there will also be the window, the pencil, the tea, the bird still building its nest.

Looking ahead towards days where, when more peace is a reality—where we live and overseas—what matters most is enabling more truthful journalism to somehow prevent weaponized chaotic and organized human on human violence. Immediately after rolling out of bed, researching important online sources, taking notes while listening to True Facts podcast episodes, drawing with full concentration to emphasize life over any growing death obsessions.

The fragments between thoughts, those pauses in the text—they're where possibility lives, where the future hasn't yet crystallized into inevitability. That space between thoughts holds what can't quite be articulated—the grief for what's being lost, the fear of what's emerging, but also the stubborn insistence that another way remains possible.

The milk is still on the list. The almond milk is still on the list. Tomorrow it might finally get bought. Or the day after. The plant grows its new leaf anyway.

And somehow, it's enough.
