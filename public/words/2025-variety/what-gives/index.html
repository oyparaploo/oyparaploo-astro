Ziigwan

The ice remembers being water.

The water remembers being ice.

Maang watches the edge of the lake where one becomes the other. Patient. The way Maang has always been patient. The way patience is not a thing you have but a thing you are.

Soon the sap.

Soon the sturgeon.

Soon everything that was still will move again.

○

Mato walks to the sugar bush before dawn.

Her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother walked this same path. The maples don't know the difference. The maples know: now the nights freeze and the days thaw and it is time.

She carries the birch bark makuks. She carries the cedar spiles. She carries the knowledge of what forty gallons becomes.

One gallon.

Sweet enough to last until the next sweetness.

Ma'iingan returns from the muskrat grounds.

Fur for trading. Meat for eating. Nothing wasted.

The spring muskrat — fat from winter, slow from waking. Easy to find if you know where to look.

Ma'iingan knows where to look.

His father knew. His father's father knew.

The muskrat don't move. The knowledge of where they live doesn't move either.

Miskwaadesi, on the sturgeon run:

This is the largest gathering of the year.

Larger than ricing. Larger than anything.

The sturgeon come to the river mouths to spawn. Three weeks. Maybe four. They come and they come and they come.

Everyone gathers. Every family. Every band. People you haven't seen since last year's sturgeon run.

The nets go out. The spears. The stories around the fires at night.

Children running between camps.

Young people finding each other.

Old people remembering when they were young people finding each other.

The sturgeon feed everyone.

But the gathering feeds something else.

The Four Sacred Medicines

Tobacco — asemaa

The first medicine.

Carries prayers to the spirit world.

Precedes all ceremonies.

Given first. Given always.

---

Cedar — giizhik

Purification. Protection.

The tea for fever.

The smoke for cleansing.

The branches for the floor of the sweat lodge.

---

Sage — bashkodejiibik

Cleansing.

Releases negative energy.

Burned before ceremony.

Carried for protection.

---

Sweetgrass — wiingashk

The sacred hair of Mother Earth.

Braided in twenty-one strands.

Attracts positive energy.

Burned after sage.

Sage pushes away. Sweetgrass invites in.

Miskwaadesi, final teaching

You ask what we owe the ancestors.

Wrong question.

The ancestors gave us everything. Every technique. Every timing. Every prayer. Every prohibition. Every story that teaches without lecturing.

We don't owe them.

We ARE them.

My hands are my grandmother's hands.

My grandmother's hands are her grandmother's hands.

The rice doesn't know the difference. The timpsila doesn't know the difference. The buffalo doesn't know the difference.

They know hands that know what to do.

When I dig timpsila, my grandmother digs timpsila.

When I bind the rice stalks, my grandmother binds the rice stalks.

When I stir the wasna, my grandmother stirs the wasna.

When I offer tobacco, my grandmother offers tobacco.

She is not gone.

None of them are gone.

They are in the rice. They are in the sugar. They are in the smoke rising from the parching fire. They are in the sound of the knocker sticks on the gunwale. They are in the blue flowers of the timpsila across the prairie. They are in the fat rendering over the slow fire. They are in the cedar tea steeping in the wooden bowl.

You want to honor them?

Do what they taught us.

That's all.

That's everything.
