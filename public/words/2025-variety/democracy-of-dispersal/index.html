Democracy of Dispersal

Words / 2025 Variety

Objects outlive their purposes. Purple tissue flowers bloom eternally beside a wheelbarrow that will never carry another load. A rubber duck, decades past its last bath. The ironing board, tagged 0106, still bears the ghost-marks of pressed clothes. Coral chunks that someone, somewhere, once thought looked nice on a shelf. Bamboo poles lean at angles that suggest collapsed structures, their purpose now purely compositional. That plastic lobster—who can say why anyone kept it this long?

Now they rest on concrete, each wearing a number like a name tag at the world's strangest reunion. Between the catalogued lots, green leaves scatter as if a garden exploded in slow motion. The photographer has turned someone's accumulation into evidence, though evidence of what remains unclear. Those wooden swords, tagged in the 0600s, might have been theater props or children's playthings. Not disaster, necessarily. The woven baskets still hold their shapes, defying whatever brought them here. Maybe just the slow accumulation that fills garages and basements until someone—an heir, a landlord, an artist—decides to sort it all out.

The auction dream works both ways. Blue and black ropes coil through the composition like the dreams' tangled logic. For sellers: that these remnants might fund something new. A lampshade skeleton stands without its skin, all structure and no shelter. For buyers: that they're acquiring more than someone else's discards. The yellow garden hose threads through everything, connecting nothing, a bright vein in a circulation system that no longer pumps. The numbered tags transform everything they touch. Shells and marine specimens cluster together, as if the ocean itself were being liquidated. That chipped candlestick becomes "Lot 0030," suddenly formal, suddenly valuable because someone bothered to count it.

What strikes hardest is the gaps. Traditional patterned fabrics drape over industrial materials, cultures colliding in the democracy of dispersal. For every salvaged object, ten others didn't make the cut—too broken, too ordinary, too hard to explain. Hand-carved pieces share equal billing with mass-produced plastics. These survivors won the lottery of preservation, earning their place in this careful grid not through beauty or worth but through sheer persistence. Even the burnt bark sections earned their numbers, their damage becoming provenance. They simply refused to disappear.

The photographs catch that exact moment when personal becomes impersonal, when "ours" becomes "anyone's." Concrete blocks anchor the composition, literal foundations for this temporary museum. Tomorrow, people will scroll through these images online, bidding on stories they'll never know. Those architectural wooden frames might rebuild someone else's dream. The rubber duck will join someone's "vintage collection." Multiple decorative flowers, purple and blue, will scatter to different homes, their artificial brotherhood dissolved. The coral will become "authentic beach house décor."

This is what remains: objects waiting for their next life, documented like crime scene evidence, though the only crime is time itself.
