Essay · The Divide · 2 minutes

Far From Home · Across the Divide

Unveiling Harm, Memory, and Agency

Colonial Violence: A Character’s Perspective
A historian, weathered with experience, explains:
“Colonial violence doesn’t begin or end with the blade—it stretches into structures, systems, and mindsets. It is the act of imposing power by fragmenting a land, erasing its identity, and replacing it with exploitation. It depletes resources and dismantles traditions while warping the natural rhythm of life itself. The checkpoints, the barriers—they are remnants of this violence, reminders of how control fractures communities. Colonial violence is both physical and psychological. It plants seeds of doubt, fear, and division, weaving itself into the fabric of the everyday."

The Actor on Occupation and Cycles of Violence
An actor breaks character to reflect, their voice heavy with emotional weight:
“Occupation dehumanizes—it strips people of autonomy and reduces them to checkpoints, searches, and waiting. It’s a system that teaches power through control. You see faces hardened by exhaustion, families fractured by displacement, and individuals reduced to numbers. Cycles of violence don’t end when someone passes through the checkpoint; they are carried forward—in memories of humiliation, in mistrust, and in anger. That anger feeds desperation, and desperation becomes the fuel for repeating the cycle. It’s not just about walls or borders; it’s about the invisible lines drawn inside people’s hearts.”

Contested Memory: Artistic Highlight
Memory doesn’t settle—it is contested, reshaped, and often erased under frameworks of power. Imagine a mural painted on a crumbling wall in a divided city—each brushstroke tells a story of joy, pain, loss, and resistance. But one day, the wall is painted over, the history smothered beneath layers of political messaging. Even as the mural disappears, the echoes of its meaning persist in the hearts of those who saw it. Memory, under power, shifts—it becomes a battlefield. What is remembered, what is silenced, and what is retold—it reflects who holds the pen.

Voicelessness vs. Determined, Limited Agency
Voicelessness is the absence of power to speak, a silence imposed by structures that crush words before they leave the mouth. Yet, even within silence, there can be agency—limited, deliberate, but defiant. The shelterless woman speaks through the roughness in her voice; it is a voice shaped by struggle, but unyielding. “Every act that tries to erase me just pushes me deeper into myself,” she says. Her agency exists in carving her existence into spaces ignored—a bag clasped tighter, a hand held higher, a walk that refuses to falter.

Physical and Psychological Fragmentation
An actor reflects on their role as a character caught between borders:
“Barriers don’t just divide land—they fragment identities. A physical wall slices through a family’s land, creating borders where none existed before. But it’s the psychological barriers that cut deeper—questions of belonging, of worth, of identity itself. The constant need for validation, for proving existence, becomes a weight people carry daily. Fragmentation isn’t just displacement—it’s the breaking of the self, piecing life into sections that don’t always fit.”

Closing Reflection
Through the lens of these characters, the symphony of divided existence gains depth—a mixture of harm, resilience, and quiet agency. The checkpoints, murals, fragments of memory, and voices weave together to tell a story not of division, but of determination.

(Holy Spirit) “Let your melodies rise even from broken divides—each note carries the weight of creation’s healing.”
