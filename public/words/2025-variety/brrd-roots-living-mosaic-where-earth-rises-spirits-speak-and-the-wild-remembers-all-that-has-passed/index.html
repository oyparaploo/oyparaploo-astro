Poem · 7 minutes

Living Mosaic

Where Earth Rises, Spirits Speak, and the Wild Remembers All That Has Passed

← Words / 2025 Variety

The hush before the performance is different.
The air is thick with voices, movement, and the hum of unseen change.
The audience leans in, sensing the shift—
the quiet is no longer empty; it is charged.

Beneath the stage, something stirs.

A network of roots, unseen but alive,
stretching beneath the grand Palais Garnier,
threading through the marble, the velvet, the gilded balconies.

They are not passive.
They are not silent.
They are not waiting.

They are rising.

And in the shadows—something flickers.

A moth’s wings, catching the dim glow of the footlights.
A fox’s silhouette, slipping through the edges of the dream.
The orchestra hums, but beneath it—
the whisper of wild things, moving unseen.

The wheat sways in slow waves, golden under the shifting light.
God meets the fields, but this time, the land does not simply exhale—
it speaks.

Colette watches from the balcony,
her fingers tracing the worn velvet of the railing.
She remembers the fields of her childhood—
the hush before dawn, the bending wheat, the quiet communion of earth and sky.

But now, the wheat moves differently.

It leans toward the people,
toward the workers,
toward the hands that have tilled the soil for generations.

The orchestra hums, deep and resonant.

Then—a twist.

The roots respond.

They stretch,
threading through the wheat,
weaving into the dancers’ steps,
learning the rhythm of movement.

A single branch lifts,
curving toward the light,
as if reaching for something unseen.

But this time, it does not reach for the divine.
It reaches for justice.

And in the fields—something stirs.

A hare, frozen in the golden light, ears twitching.
A shadow, slipping between the stalks, watching, waiting.
The wheat breathes, and the creatures move with it.

The wheat fades into rising foothills,
where paths twist toward the unknown.
Jesus meets the foothills, but this time, He does not walk alone.

Sabine sits beside Colette,
watching the dancers climb, pause, turn back.
The movement feels familiar—
the tension between ascent and hesitation, between faith and doubt.

Then—a twist.

The roots adapt.

They split,
branching into finer threads,
learning the shape of the foothills,
the weight of footsteps,
the hesitation of those who climb.

But this time, they do not simply mirror the doubt.
They push forward,
urging the dancers to keep moving,
to rise,
to demand more.

The opera house fills with the hum of cicadas,
their rhythmic pulse merging with the strings.
The dancers falter, adjusting to the sound.
The audience listens differently now—
leaning in, sensing the movement of wings, the brush of air displaced by flight.

And then—a presence.

Not just wisdom.
Not just humility.
But defiance.

And in the foothills—something watches.

A wolf, silent, eyes gleaming in the half-light.
A hawk, circling above, waiting for the right moment to dive.
The land listens, and the creatures listen with it.

The foothills dissolve into a dense forest garden,
where light filters through in shifting patterns.
Buddha meets the forest gardens, but this time, the trees do not simply stand in silence.

Colette closes her eyes.
She remembers the forests of her youth—
**the hush beneath the canopy, the way light fractured through the leaves,
the quiet unfolding of thought**.

Then—a twist.

The roots expand,
spreading through the forest,
learning the language of trees,
the hush of leaves,
the stillness of meditation.

But this time, they do not simply listen.
They speak.

A single branch wraps around a dancer’s wrist,
not constricting,
but holding,
as if offering comfort.

And then—a pulse.

The trees move,
not just in response to the wind,
but in response to the people.

The forest is not just alive—
it is aware.
It is watching.
It is waiting.

And in the shadows—something shifts.

A stag, stepping forward, antlers catching the moonlight.
A fox, slipping between the roots, unseen but present.
The forest breathes, and the creatures breathe with it.

The forest fades into a vast shoreline,
waves rolling in rhythms older than memory.
Great Indigenous Spirits meet the ocean shoreline, but this time, the water does not simply carry prayers—it carries demands.

Sabine grips Colette’s hand.
The scent of salt fills the opera house.
The waves echo through the balconies,
rolling in unseen currents.

Then—a twist.

The roots hesitate.

They have never touched the ocean.

They pause,
listening,
learning the rhythm of the tide,
the pull of the moon,
the weight of water.

But this time, they do not simply adapt.
They rise.

A single branch dips into the waves,
testing,
tasting,
discovering something new.

And then—a surge.

The water lifts,
not just in beauty,
but in protest.

The ocean does not wait.
It moves.
It demands.
It remembers.

And in the tide—something emerges.

A whale, rising, its song threading through the air.
A heron, standing at the edge, watching, waiting.
The ocean breathes, and the creatures rise with it.

All landscapes merge—
the field, foothills, forest, and ocean intertwining in a breathtaking crescendo.
The voices of God, Jesus, Buddha, and the Great Indigenous Spirits rise together,
not in opposition but in solidarity.

The opera house breathes,
alive with the pulse of nature.

The roots pulse,
fully conscious,
fully aware,
woven into the fabric of the performance,
the music,
the movement,
the breath of the audience.

But this time, they do not simply exist.
They act.
They push.
They demand.

Colette and Sabine step forward,
their hands outstretched.

The curtain falls.
The hush returns.

But now, the quiet is different.

It breathes.

It remembers.

It rises.

Beneath the surface, the roots whisper, their voices threading through the soil like ancient murmurs.

“Careful,” one hums, curling around a pocket of rich earth.
“There is life here.”

Another root pauses, sensing the delicate movement of unseen creatures—the slow pulse of earthworms, the quiet shift of buried seeds, the microscopic dance of fungi weaving through the dark.

“We do not disturb,” the roots agree.
“We listen.”

They remember the hands that once tilled this soil, the voices that spoke of its fertility, its depth, its rare and sacred stories.

“They told of the first harvests,” one whispers.
“Of the soil that held the weight of footsteps before it held the weight of crops.”

The earth hums beneath them, carrying the echoes of ancient farmers, forgotten prayers, and the quiet reverence of those who understood that soil is not just ground—it is memory.

Above, Colette kneels, pressing her fingers into the damp earth.

Sabine watches her, arms folded, the scent of soil thick in the air.

“You’re being careful,” she says.

Colette nods. “I don’t want to disturb anything.”

Sabine crouches beside her, running her fingers through the loose dirt.

“You know,” she murmurs, “there are stories about this soil. About how it remembers.”

Colette tilts her head. “Remembers what?”

Sabine smiles, brushing away a stray leaf.

“Everything.”
