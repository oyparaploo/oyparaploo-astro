What We Wear to Survive

Words / 2025 Variety

## I. Shibuya

Yuki paints her face before leaving for work, and not makeup in any conventional sense but actual face paint—neon pink swirled with electric blue, geometric patterns across her forehead, wide circles around her eyes that make her look like a carnival performer or a child's drawing of terror or both, which is exactly the point, though she couldn't articulate the point if anyone asked, and people do ask, constantly, strangers on the train saying "what's that for?" as if decoration requires justification, as if existing in public demands explanation.

She's twenty-seven, works at a convenience store in Shibuya, the one across from the crossing where a thousand people walk simultaneously eight times an hour, where every surface is a screen announcing something urgent—earthquake preparedness, missing persons, new phone plans, K-pop concerts, sale at Uniqlo, smile for the camera, consume this, fear that, be alert, stay safe, buy now—and Yuki paints her face because when the world is screaming at you in neon, you have to scream back.

Her roommate Hana says: "You look insane."

"That's the idea."

"What idea?"

And Yuki doesn't know how to explain that terror looks cartoonish if you frame it right, that if you make yourself look crazy enough, people look away, and isn't that a kind of invisibility? Isn't that its own form of safety?

Today she does purple swirls with yellow dots, exaggerated and childlike and unsettling, and she looks in the mirror and thinks: good, yes, this is it, this is the face of someone who is processing too much and has decided to make it visible instead of swallowing it down where it ferments into something worse.

The walk to work is three blocks of screens. Her phone buzzes constantly—news alerts, group chat, mother texting, sister texting, Twitter, Instagram, ten unread emails, twenty notifications, the world demanding she process it all, keep up with it all, respond to it all, and she doesn't look, just walks with her painted face through the bombardment, and people stare but they also move away, which is what she wants, this small perimeter of space her face buys her.

At the convenience store, her manager says: "Can you not do that?"

"Do what?"

"The face. It's unprofessional."

"I'm wearing the uniform."

"But—"

"I'll wear a mask if it helps."

She does. Covers the paint with a white surgical mask. Now she's just eyes—wide, terrified, exaggerated—and her manager stops complaining because eyes are harder to police than whole faces.

The shift is eight hours of scanning items while screens above her head play everything simultaneously—news, ads, music videos, weather, more ads—a woman buys twenty energy drinks, a student falls asleep at the table, a businessman cries quietly near the magazines, a child screams for ten minutes while the mother stares at her phone, and Yuki scans and smiles under her mask and feels the paint on her face like armor, like the only thing between her and complete dissolution into static.

## II. Manila

Nina wears her grandmother's dress to the grocery store, the one with the enormous pink flowers and bright green leaves and yellow centers, and it's basically wallpaper, the pattern so loud it's almost violent, the kind of dress that makes people look twice and then look away because they don't know how to process this much visual information in human form.

She's thirty-one, works from home doing data entry for a company in Singapore, eight hours daily of inputting numbers she doesn't understand about things she'll never see, and she wears her grandmother's dresses because when everything is chaos, you wear the chaos, you become the loudest thing in the room so nothing else can get louder than you.

Her aunt says: "Why do you wear that? It's for old ladies."

"I like it."

"It makes you look crazy."

"Good."

Manila is always too much—too loud, too crowded, too hot, too bright, too everything. The jeepneys are painted like explosions of color and pattern, the streets packed with people and motorcycles and vendors and dogs and children, every surface covered with posters, graffiti, advertisements, warnings. Her phone never stops buzzing. The TV is always on. The neighbors are always shouting. The construction never stops. The traffic never moves. The rain comes and floods everything and then stops and everything steams.

Nina walks to the grocery in her grandmother's dress feeling like she's wearing the room she grew up in, like domestic comfort turned inside out and made monstrous, recognizable but wrong, which is how she feels most days anyway, so why not make it visible?

People stare. An old woman laughs. A child points. Nina doesn't care. The dress is loud enough that she doesn't have to be.

At the grocery, fluorescent lights make everything look dead. The speakers play three songs simultaneously. Everyone's on their phones, processing information from elsewhere while standing here trying to buy eggs. Nina's phone buzzes—mother calling, doesn't answer, sister, doesn't answer, group chat about cousin's wedding, work email about deadline, news alert about typhoon, Facebook notification, Instagram notification, Twitter, WhatsApp from her aunt asking why she never responds—and Nina puts the phone in her bag, pays for her groceries, walks home in her grandmother's dress feeling like she's wearing armor made of flowers.

Her apartment is one room covered in things—posters, photos, fabric she bought and never used, newspapers she meant to read, mail she meant to open. It's too much. She knows it's too much. Can't stop adding to it.

Her therapist says: "Maybe start clearing some space."

Nina nods. Doesn't clear anything. Because the clutter is honest, at least. It's what her mind looks like externalized. Why pretend otherwise?

## III. Messages

Yuki's mother sees her photo online—someone took it on the train, posted it with hashtag #TokyoWeird—and calls immediately.

"What's wrong with your face?"

"Nothing. Just paint."

"Why?"

"I don't know. Because."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Her mother is quiet, then: "Are you okay?"

"Yeah."

"Really?"

"I don't know, Mom. Are any of us?"

Her mother doesn't have an answer for that, so they talk about other things—her sister's new job, the weather, normal things that require normal responses—and after they hang up, Yuki sits in her apartment with her painted face and thinks about how her mother asked if she was okay as if okay is a binary state, as if you're either fine or not fine, as if there's no middle ground where you're functioning and drowning simultaneously.

She paints her face again, different colors this time—green and orange and pink all bleeding together, and she takes a photo and posts it with no caption, and within minutes someone comments: "You good?" and she doesn't respond because how do you answer that? How do you explain that you're good and not good, that you're surviving by making visible the chaos you're processing, that decoration is survival strategy when you're drowning in input?

In Manila, Nina's sister visits, sees the dress, sees the apartment covered in layers.

"This is bad," her sister says.

"What is?"

"All of this. You. The dress. The apartment. It's too much."

"I know."

"So fix it."

"Can't."

"Why not?"

And Nina thinks about how to explain that decoration is survival, that when you're drowning you have to become the water, that wearing grandma's wallpaper dress is the only way to walk through a city that screams at you constantly, that the chaos on her walls is the chaos in her head externalized and at least it's honest, at least it's visible, at least she's not pretending everything is fine when it's not.

"Because it helps," Nina says.

"How?"

"I don't know. It just does."

Her sister leaves, tells their mother Nina is "getting worse," and their mother calls, and Nina doesn't answer, just sits at her desk in the floral dress and thinks about how everyone wants you to process everything quietly, wants you to manage the overload without showing it, wants you to consume the constant crisis without any visible sign of what it's doing to you.

## IV. Finding Each Other

Yuki finds Nina through Instagram, or Nina finds Yuki, or the algorithm decides these two women processing too much should know about each other. The algorithm is occasionally correct about human need.

Nina posts a photo: herself in the floral dress, eyes wide, looking terrified and cartoonish. Caption: "What we wear when the world is too loud."

Yuki comments: "I paint my face for the same reason."

They message. Schedule a video call. It's 9 PM in Tokyo, 8 PM in Manila.

"Show me," Nina says.

Yuki turns her camera on. Tonight's face paint is red and blue, swirled together, eyes exaggerated.

"Oh my god," Nina says. "You look amazing."

"I look insane."

"You look honest."

They talk for two hours. About mothers who don't understand, about therapists who suggest clearing space, about how the world demands you process everything—every crisis, every notification, every piece of information—and still show up to work looking normal, acting normal, being normal when normal is impossible because normal people aren't asked to consume this much chaos.

"The dress thing," Yuki says. "Does it help?"

"Sort of. Not really. But I can't stop."

"The paint thing doesn't help either. But I keep doing it."

"Why?"

"Because if I'm going to drown, I want people to see it happening. I want it visible. I want—"

"To not disappear quietly."

"Yes. Exactly that."

They talk about their cities—Tokyo's screens, Manila's noise, the constant assault of input—and about decoration as survival strategy, about making the overwhelm visible instead of swallowing it, about screaming back at a world that won't stop screaming.

Before they hang up, Nina says: "I'm glad you exist. With your painted face."

"I'm glad you exist. With your grandmother's dresses."

"We should show each other. Every day. So we know we're not alone in this."

"Okay."

They start a group chat. Just the two of them. Every morning, they post photos. Yuki's painted faces. Nina's loud dresses. No captions. Just evidence that they're still here, still processing, still surviving by making it visible.

## V. What Helps

Three months later, Yuki still paints her face. Her manager stopped commenting. Her mother stopped calling about it. Her sister texts occasionally—"you still doing the weird face thing?"—and Yuki responds "yeah" and that's the end of it.

At work, someone asks: "Why do you paint your face?"

And instead of saying "I don't know," Yuki says: "Because there's too much. Everywhere. All the time. Screens and news and messages and noise and I can't process it all quietly. So I don't. I paint it. Make it visible."

The person nods like this makes sense. Maybe it does. Maybe other people are drowning too and just better at hiding it.

Nina still wears the dresses. Her family stopped asking. Her sister visits once a month, doesn't mention the apartment anymore, just steps carefully through the layers of accumulated things.

At the grocery, a young girl asks: "Why is your dress so loud?"

"Because I'm loud," Nina says.

"Are you?"

"I'm trying to be."

The girl nods, satisfied, and Nina thinks: children understand things adults have forgotten, like how decoration can be armor, like how sometimes you have to wear the chaos to survive it.

That evening, Nina videos calls Yuki. It's 7 PM in Manila, 8 PM in Tokyo. They eat dinner together across time zones—Nina's adobo, Yuki's convenience store bento—and don't talk much. Just exist on screen together, Nina in her floral dress, Yuki with painted face, two women who've decided that if the world demands they process too much, they'll do it loudly, visibly, honestly.

"My therapist asked if the dress thing was getting better," Nina says.

"What'd you say?"

"That it's not better or worse. It just is."

"My manager asked if I'd consider toning down the paint."

"And?"

"I painted it brighter the next day."

They laugh. Tired laugh. Recognition laugh.

Yuki says: "Do you think we're okay?"

"I think we're surviving."

"Is that the same thing?"

"I don't know. Maybe it's better. Maybe surviving loudly is better than being okay quietly."

After they hang up, Yuki washes her face—the paint swirls down the drain in neon rivers—and looks at herself without it. Ordinary. Tired. Drowning in all the normal ways.

She picks up the paint. Does tomorrow's face tonight. Pink and yellow and green, wide eyes, exaggerated mouth, cartoonish terror that people can dismiss or not, she doesn't care, she's making it visible, she's refusing to disappear quietly into the static.

In Manila, Nina takes off the floral dress, hangs it with the others. Twenty dresses now. All her grandmother's. All loud. All saying: I'm here, I'm processing too much, I'm not pretending otherwise.

She puts on a different one for tomorrow. Orange and red and purple. Louder than today's. Always louder.

Her phone buzzes. Yuki sent a photo. Tomorrow's face paint. Green and orange swirls. Wide terrified eyes.

Nina sends back a photo of tomorrow's dress. The loudest one. Flowers like explosions.

No captions. Just: this is what we're wearing to survive. This is how we're processing. This is our armor against drowning.

It doesn't fix anything. The world doesn't get quieter. The screens don't stop. The information doesn't stop bombarding. The crisis doesn't end.

But they're still here. Still painting. Still wearing. Still making the overwhelm visible instead of swallowing it.

Still surviving loudly.

And maybe that's not okay. Maybe that's not fine. Maybe that's not even healthy by any normal metric.

But it's honest.

And honest is something.

And something is better than nothing.

And nothing is what happens when you try to process everything quietly until you disappear completely into the static that's always playing on every screen in every city where people are drowning together in too much, too much, always too much, and never enough space to not be processing, never enough silence, never enough anything except input and demand and crisis and noise.

So they paint. They wear. They survive.

Loudly.

Together.

Across continents and time zones and algorithms that occasionally guess correctly about what human connection looks like when you're both drowning in neon chaos.

They're still here.

Still loud.

Still visible.

That's everything.
