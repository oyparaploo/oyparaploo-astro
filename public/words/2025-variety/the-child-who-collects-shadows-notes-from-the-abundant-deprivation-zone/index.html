The Child Who Collects Shadows (2)

Words / 2025 Variety

Notes from the Abundant Deprivation Zone

A child in a hospital ward asks if clouds are edible, and suddenly we understand everything about what humans do to each other. This collection begins with testimony from a hunger ward where mothers dissolve themselves gram by gram to keep their babies alive one more day. It becomes a play where actors fold paper cranes from medical forms because beauty insists on happening anyway. Then it fractures into fevered fragments—the way a mind actually sounds when it's witnessing the unthinkable. This isn't writing about trauma; this is writing from inside trauma's own grammar.

Right now, as we watch genocide through phone screens, arguing about words while children starve, this work does what journalism can't: it makes us feel the weight of a disappearing child, the taste of remembered bread, the sound of a nurse crying while she puts on lipstick because that small act of dignity is all she has left. The fragments don't cohere because reality itself has stopped cohering. The repetitions circle like someone in shock. The questions—"Are clouds edible?" "How did we arrive here?"—hang unanswered because some questions are accusations dressed as curiosity.

This is necessary literature, the kind that changes how you breathe after reading it. It refuses to make atrocity poetic or bearable. Instead, it shows us exactly how love continues in conditions designed to kill it: a man guards a seed in his pocket, nurses share a single piece of gum, someone waters plastic plants every day. These aren't metaphors. This is what survival looks like when even survival seems obscene.
