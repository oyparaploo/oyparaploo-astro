Disco Balls for Fish

← Words / 2025 Variety

Part One: Putumayo River, Colombia, 2018

Carmen hung her dead husband's net between two guava trees. Wrong. All wrong. The knots faced inside out.

Three months since Miguel died of a thumb infection. Stupid death. Now the net caught air where water used to be.

The river: down four meters since the Chinese dam. Government said temporary. The mud said different.

Lucia found her tying washers to the net at dawn. "You're making it ugly."

"Good."

Other widows started bringing nets. Forty now. Some still had scales stuck—little silver scabs.

Swiss woman came. Or German. Who could tell. White linen in the jungle, stayed clean somehow. "These pieces engage with indigenous knowledge systems—"

"They're just nets."

"The way traditional craft encodes environmental—"

"Dead nets."

The woman offered fifteen thousand euros. Then twenty.

"Already sold," Carmen said.

"To who?"

Pointed at the brown ditch that used to be river.

That night Carmen tied compact mirrors where the floats went. Lucia helped.

"Now they're disco balls."

"Disco balls for fish."

"Fish can't dance."

"Dead ones can."

Part Two: Venice, 2019

The nets went first. Carmen followed. Threw up on the plane—first time flying. Translator from Bogotá kept calling her Señora like she was his grandmother.

White cube gallery. Lights that cost more than boats. She hung the nets wrong—tangled, sideways, inside-out. Real fishermen would weep.

Opening: black clothes, little wines, serious faces.

Man with small glasses: "This speaks to extractive capitalism and—"

"It speaks to fish."

"But surely the metaphor—"

"Fish don't live in metaphors. Also they're dead."

Woman found her by the emergency exit. Pearls. Crying. "My grandmother. Poland. After the camps, she made quilts from uniforms."

"Uniforms?"

"Prisoner uniforms. Cut them up. Made beautiful things."

Stood there. Two women who understood: you make pretty things from what used to work, just to prove something. What? Who knows.

Hotel: 400 euros a night. Carmen hung Miguel's first net in the bathroom. Twelve years old when he made it. In morning light it threw shadows like water. Almost.

Called Lucia: "Venice is sinking."

"Into what?"

"More Venice."

"The nets?"

"Famous now."

"Papá would hate it."

"He hated everything. My rice. My hair. The way I laughed."

"You do laugh wrong."

"I know."

Through the window: real water in canals. Rising, newspaper said. City drowning upward into sky.

The nets would stay after she left. Catching nothing between white walls. But tonight—shadow-water on the bathroom tiles where she'd hung Miguel's child-net wrong, so wrong he couldn't even complain from death.

She thought about the Polish woman's grandmother. Cutting up uniforms. Making something. After.

Carmen had cut nothing. Just hung things. Wrong.

In Colombia the river kept shrinking. In Venice the water kept rising. Somewhere between, her nets hung in gallery light, not catching fish or tourists or meaning. Just hanging.

The translator knocked. "Señora, tomorrow's interview—"

"Tell them about the disco balls."

"What?"

"Tell them dead fish dance better than live ones."

"I don't understand."

She touched Miguel's net. The knots were perfect actually. She'd lied about them being wrong.
