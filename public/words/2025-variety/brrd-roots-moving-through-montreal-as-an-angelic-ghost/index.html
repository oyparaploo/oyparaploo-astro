Moving Through Montreal as an Angelic Ghost

← Words / 2025 Variety

The Whisper That Never Fades

January 2021. Mile End. The eviction notice arrives in French only, though my landlord knows I struggle with the subjunctive. Reprise de logement—taking back the dwelling. Even the language makes dispossession sound like reclamation.

There are two kinds of presence in Montreal.

The kind that demands recognition—speaking loudly at Café Olympico, taking up two seats on the 80 bus during rush hour, insisting the dep owner speak English.

And the kind that lingers—learning to pronounce Beaubien without flinching, leaving exact change on the counter at Wilensky's, knowing which metro car stops nearest the escalator at each station.

After the eviction, I stopped trying to prove I belonged here. Started learning how to haunt instead.

You Are Felt Before You Are Seen

Marché Jean-Talon, 7 AM, February cold that makes breathing hurt. I move between the Vietnamese vendors and Québécois farmers, understanding both French and English but claiming neither. A woman drops her wallet near the blood oranges. I retrieve it, hand it back. She says "Merci"—then, studying my face—"Thank you." I nod, give nothing back, let her wonder which language lives in my mouth.

This is the first lesson of becoming ghostlike: let others fill in your silences with their assumptions.

At the STM office on Berri, I renew my Opus card. The agent asks, "Quelle zone?" I slide exact change under the glass partition. She prints the card without another word. Later, reviewing security footage for an unrelated incident, they'll notice how I appear in frame but never quite come into focus, like the city's CCTV can't decide if I'm worth recording.

There is power in being noticed before you are acknowledged—the way Mount Royal appears suddenly between buildings, always there but constantly surprising tourists who didn't realize Montreal had a mountain at its heart.

You Do Not Demand Space—You Shape It

The Notre-Dame Basilica charges tourists $12 to enter. Locals know you can attend morning mass for free, sitting in the back while golden light streams through Moment Factory's projections onto stone that remembers New France.

I discovered this three months after the eviction, sleeping in the 24-hour Tim Hortons on Sainte-Catherine until the security guard's shift change at 6 AM, then walking to the basilica to sit in warmth that cost nothing but the performance of prayer.

Ghosts do not knock on doors. We find the ones left ajar.

In the underground city—RÉSO, they officially call it, though nobody does—32 kilometers of tunnels connect 1,600 shops without ever surfacing into winter. I mapped every entrance during those months of displacement. From Atwater to Berri-UQAM, you can travel the length of downtown without showing your face to daylight. Security cameras see bodies, not stories. Store owners watch for theft, not for someone learning the precise art of existing without existing.

The Complexe Desjardins food court, Level 2. Table by the fountain that masks conversation. This becomes my office. Free WiFi, no password. Bathroom code that hasn't changed since 1987: 1967, the year of Expo. I conduct Zoom interviews for remote jobs, my background a careful blur, my location always "Montreal" but never more specific.

The Memory That Haunts Gently

McGill Ghetto, where students pay $2,000 for apartments I once rented for $400. The Portuguese families who sold me pastéis de nata have been replaced by third-wave coffee shops selling cortados for $6. But António's daughter still runs the corner dep, though she doesn't remember me until I ask for the Brazilian cigarettes her father kept behind the counter for homesick exchange students.

"You used to live on Hutchison," she says. Not a question.

"I used to live everywhere," I answer.

True impact is not immediate—it accumulates like snow on fire escapes, unnoticed until the weight reshapes the metal.

The Distance That Invites Curiosity

Parc La Fontaine, where I learned to feed the raccoons that everyone pretends don't exist. 3 AM is their hour, when the only humans awake are the ones who've lost something—sleep, homes, the ability to be seen during daylight.

A woman approaches, speaking rapid French about her son, missing three days. I don't tell her I don't fully understand. I show her the warm grate near the pond where people sometimes sleep, the community center that doesn't check IDs, the McDonald's on Ontario that lets you stay if you buy coffee every two hours.

She doesn't ask how I know these things. Ghosts don't have to explain their expertise in disappearance.

The Role of the Angelic Ghost in a Restless World

July 1st, Moving Day, when 115,000 Montreal leases expire simultaneously. The streets fill with abandoned furniture, whole lives left curbside. I watch from the balcony I no longer have, from the window of the apartment I was forced to leave, except the new tenants don't see me because I'm not really there—I'm across the street, in the shadow of the church, wearing the same gray as the stone.

Most people fight for visibility in this city. They join the language debates, pick sides between Habs and Nordiques (even though the Nordiques left in 1995), argue about whether St-Viateur or Fairmount makes better bagels.

I've learned something different in my displacement: Montreal is a city built on layers—French over English over Mohawk, Catholic over Jewish over sacred Haudenosaunee ground. The real power isn't in claiming a layer but in moving between them, like water through limestone, leaving minerals behind that slowly change the composition of everything.

The Whisper Returns

Winter 2024. I apply for an apartment on Rue Marquette, in Villeray, where the rents haven't caught up to the renovation. The landlord speaks French. I respond in the careful, accented French of someone who learned it from necessity, not birth. He asks for references. I give him the Portuguese dep owner's daughter, the security guard from the Tim Hortons who eventually stopped asking me to leave, the priest who noticed I never took communion but always dropped foreign coins in the collection plate.

"How long have you lived in Montreal?" he asks.

"I'm still arriving," I answer in French, then correct myself in English: "I never left."

He gives me the keys without checking the references.

This is what I've become: not invisible but essential, like the smell of depanneurs—cigarettes and slush and overpriced milk—that every Montrealer carries in their memory. I don't demand space anymore. I've learned to live in the space between demanding and disappearing, in the pause between "Français?" and "English?", in the moment before the Metro doors close when you can slip through without anyone noticing you never paid.

An angelic ghost moves through Montreal differently—with the grace of someone who's been evicted from visibility but not from presence, who's learned that the city remembers you not by how loudly you insisted on belonging but by the traces you left in its peripheral vision, the moments you shifted the air in rooms you were never quite invited to enter.

I am the whisper in Montreal's ear, speaking neither French nor English but the third language of those who've learned to survive between solitudes.

Forever. Effortlessly. Beautifully.

Even if the lease expires again.
