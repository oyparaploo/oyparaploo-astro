Echoes of Glass Stones

Chapter Ten

The Glass Trap

Night was a net cast wide over the city. Paris glimmered beneath it, every light a bead strung too tight. From the roof of the half-finished office tower, Troll Tearsen could see the river drag neon downstream, slow and exhausted. She felt the hum of the cranes even when they were still—steel resonances stored in bone.

Libaux Torrini climbed the last ladder behind her, boots on rung, metal on metal, each step a percussion that wanted to be more important than it was. He pulled himself onto the platform with a grunt that came back in doubled echoes, the kind of sound that made a man sound older than his years.

Below, the construction site was a cathedral of absence: scaffolding rattled faintly in wind, sheets of tarpaulin slapped themselves like anxious metronomes. The city’s noise was caught here, refracted, bent back upward—sirens warped into bleats, car horns into hiccupped vowels.

“Perfect stage,” Libaux muttered.

“For him,” Troll said.

She touched the small cloth bag in her pocket. The marbles shifted, making their private dry tch-tch. Each one had a tone, Mage Meuller had written. Each was a signature in glass. Tonight she expected Stremler’s next note.

A floodlight flicked on at ground level without warning. Concrete pillars glared pale; rebar threw spidery shadows onto tarpaulin. A loudspeaker somewhere—impossible to locate in the skeleton’s lattice—cracked with static.

“…you climb well.”

Stremler’s voice. Intimate, not amplified. Yet the loudspeaker carried it anyway, bent it until it arrived beside their ears like a friend whispering too close.

Libaux scanned the scaffolding. Nothing. The sound had been engineered to arrive from everywhere.

“…you follow because you think following is choice.”

The floodlight swung upward, pinned them for a breath, then swiveled away again. The afterimage burned the roof silver in their eyes.

Troll crouched, palms flat to the concrete. The floor hummed faintly, as if more machinery turned below than the site accounted for. “He’s using the building,” she said. “Glass ribs. Steel lungs. We’re inside his body.”

Another light ignited, this one across the river. It blinked twice. A deliberate rhythm. tik—tik.

Then a marble rolled. Somewhere in the dark interior of the tower, it clinked once against steel, then again, then spun itself out against silence. The reverb took it and multiplied, until the building seemed full of rolling glass, each echo chasing the next.

Troll’s stomach tightened. She thought of Fontanella’s shelter, Miller Corbal’s fractured hand, the Pigalle club bass vibrating inside the pristine sphere. The echo now was different—directed, manipulated. A trap disguised as music.

They descended two levels, boots sending percussion through hollow floorplates. The air grew heavier. Dust inhaled and exhaled around their headlamps.

A shape moved in the unfinished hallway. Not Stremler. A courier—different from the one they had found before—stood unsteadily, eyes wide. A marble in his palm reflected their lights, blood-bright red.

“He said—” the courier rasped. His voice doubled in the metal, repeating back at him as if mocking. “He said if you hear this, it means you chose to arrive.”

Libaux’s hand went to his shoulder. The man flinched as though sound itself were an impact. The marble slipped from his palm. It hit the floor with a tik that detonated into corridors and stairwells. The sound didn’t fade. It repeated, mechanical, recorded, rebroadcast through hidden speakers, until the whole floor sang with one tiny collision.

“Loop,” Troll breathed.

Stremler’s voice threaded through the repetition. “…echo is proof. Proof that you were here, proof that you obeyed. Glass remembers when flesh cannot.”

The courier sagged into Libaux’s arms, pulse fluttering, breath bitter with whatever had numbed him.

Troll’s phone buzzed. She didn’t want to look but did. Miyake Coscia: She is still here, Troll. Not for long. If you can, speak her name. The room will carry it.

She wanted to. She wanted the tower’s echo to carry her mother’s name as far as physics allowed. But she held her tongue. Stremler was listening, always listening. He would not have her mother’s name to rehearse.

They pressed deeper. A stairwell dropped three flights where the walls had been left in raw concrete. Their footsteps made cavernous tok-tok-tok sounds, magnified into ritual.

At the bottom, a circle of marbles had been arranged on the bare floor. Dozens—clear, red, green, veined white—each balanced on small chalk marks. A pattern.

“It’s a staff,” Libaux said after a beat. “Music without notes.”

The loudspeaker crackled again, Stremler’s tone warm and damning. “…audiences are loyal when the stage never ends. Every marble is a syllable. Every syllable is a stone. Together they build your testimony.”

Troll crouched at the circle’s edge. Her breath stirred one marble. It rolled, clinked against another. The room bloomed with the sound, feedback drawn long, distorted until it was no longer glass but thunder.

She covered her ears. The marbles kept clinking, one to another, dominoes of sound, each strike lifted by hidden mics and rebroadcast as if the floor itself had become an orchestra pit.

Above the storm of noise, Stremler’s voice: “…you are not investigators. You are the instruments.”

The floodlights cut out at once.

Darkness swelled. Only their headlamps gave weak cones of honesty. The marbles still knocked against one another, momentum endless, until Troll realized—no, it wasn’t momentum. They were being nudged. Air forced across the floor by hidden vents. Directed into motion.

“They’re moving us,” Libaux growled. “Herding.”

“Trap,” Troll said.

“The Glass Trap,” Libaux echoed grimly.

The circle closed. The sound intensified. A frequency deepened until her teeth ached. One marble spun directly against her boot, fast and frantic, as if trying to bore through leather. She kicked it aside. It skittered against the wall with a ringing note that reverberated until the concrete itself seemed to sing.

Then silence.

Not real silence, but engineered absence. Fans cut, speakers muted, marbles arrested. Their own breathing grew violent in the void.

A final voice, closer now, unmediated by wire or wall.

“…next time, you won’t leave.”

It was Stremler’s voice without echo.

They emerged hours later into dawn light grayed by drizzle. The river muttered against its quays, the city preparing its normal day as if nothing subterranean had happened.

Troll’s coat pocket felt heavier than it was. She still held the bubble-seeded marble from the Metro, the cracked one from the archive, the pristine one from Pigalle. Each hummed in her memory with its own pitch.

On her phone, two unheard voicemails from Miyake. She did not open them. She wasn’t ready for the sound of goodbye layered into the city’s chorus.

Libaux lit his last cigarette, then crushed it half-burned between his fingers. “He’s not showing us proof,” he said. “He’s showing us rehearsal.”

“And rehearsal never ends,” Troll said.

Paris gave no answer except the usual: pigeons clattering wings, taxis coughing exhaust, shutters clicking open like tired eyelids.

The city was awake. And so was the trap.
