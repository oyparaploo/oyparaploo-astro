**Echoes of Glass Stones**

#**Chapter One – The Marble in the Hand**

Anders pulled his phone out. Third time. 6:14. Shit—sixteen minutes gone already. Hauser would be standing at his desk with that little notebook, marking down every missing bastard minute like they were gold coins.

The shortcut through Fontanella would save four minutes. Maybe five if he really hoofed it. Twenty-eight years at the plant, he knew these calculations by heart. Main road: twenty-three minutes walking fast, twenty-seven if those morning trucks from Bludenz backed everything up at the crossing. But this path, cutting diagonal across the valley—nineteen minutes even when it was pissing rain like today.

You could taste the diesel in that fog. Like licking the back of a truck. Mixed with that chemical smell from the plant—they'd started the morning batch already. Wet wool and industrial cleaning shit. Made his throat tight.

His boots hit a slick patch where the gravel went to mud. Grabbed the railing—thing was so cold it bit right through his work gloves. Rust flakes came off on his palm. Twenty-eight years of this walk. Twenty-eight years of Hauser's bullshit, but the money was steady and Greta needed help with university.

The shelter looked smaller in the fog. Like it was shrinking back into the valley. Inside the glass, the bus timetable had gone wavy with damp. Useless thing. Nobody'd updated it since they killed the route six months back. Company promised shuttles for workers. Three weeks those shuttles ran, then—nothing. Budget cuts.

But someone sat on the bench.

Woman. Coat hanging open, which was wrong—cold as a bastard this morning. One boot on her foot, black leather, scuffed to hell at the toe. The other boot was upside down in the corner, laces spread out like... like he didn't know what. Spider legs maybe.

Her chin was tucked down. That angle. He'd seen that angle before, when Opa—no. Not thinking about that.

"Miss?" His voice came out weird, echoey in the shelter.

Nothing. He stepped closer. Work boots scraping, making that sound wet gravel makes.

Her hand. Jesus, her hand was holding something. Green glass. Old marble, the hand-blown kind with bubbles trapped inside. White swirl in the middle like frozen smoke.

Anders fumbled for his phone. Fingers too thick, too scarred from conveyor work. Hit wrong numbers twice. Finally got through.

"There's a woman." Voice cracked like he was some kid. "She's not moving. Fontanella shelter—the old one, you know? Below where the chemical plant turns off. She's holding—there's a marble. Green marble in her hand."

"Sir, is she breathing?"

He looked at her chest. Nothing. That gray-white color, like old wax. "No. Don't think so."

"Don't touch anything. Units coming."

He wanted to run. Every minute here was another mark in Hauser's book, another euro gone. But he couldn't leave her. Not with the fog pressing in. Not with her holding that green glass thing like it was—like it was the last solid thing in the world.

---

Detective Troll Tearsen had been up since 3. Not insomnia—she knew insomnia, and this wasn't it. This was that specific gut-twist that came with hospice texts. Latest one at 3:47: *Your mother was asking for you. Morning clarity window around 7 AM - Nurse Miyake Coscia.*

Four times she'd read the damn message. Four. Like reading it again would change what it said. Morning clarity window. Medical talk for: your mother might know who you are for five minutes before the morphine and the cancer in her brain steal her away again.

Kitchen felt like a box at 6 AM. Coffee happened somehow—grinding beans, water, pressing the plunger on that French press. Her mother's Christmas gift from five years back. "Proper coffee," she'd said, "not that machine Scheisse."

Three unread from Miyake now. Troll flipped the phone face-down. Next to that glossy hospice pamphlet—*Family Support for the End of Life Journey*—with its bullshit smiling nurses who'd never watched someone actually die. The coffee tasted like guilt and burned grounds.

Through the window: headlights on the B190. Regular people heading to regular jobs. Not stuck in this weird space between daughter and detective, between—

The marble jar.

There on the bookshelf, catching the kitchen light. Blue and green glass she used to count as a kid. Her mother's jar, brought from the old house when she moved in. Six months ago, before "concerning" became "terminal."

Yesterday—she'd counted them yesterday while on hold with the hospice. Old habit. Turn the jar, watch them tumble, count the green ones. Twelve. Always twelve.

She walked over. Lifted the jar. Counted.

Eleven.

The gap was like—like a missing tooth. One green marble gone, the one with the white swirl she'd called "smoke marble" when she was little.

The jar's rim had fingerprints, but old ones. Months old. Her mother hadn't been able to hold anything smaller than a water cup since March. Hadn't been home since February.

Work phone rang. Not her personal—the official one. Dispatch.

"Body at Fontanella. Bus shelter below the chemical plant. Responding units requesting detective. Unusual circumstances."

"Unusual how?"

"Victim found holding what appears to be a marble. Green glass."

The coffee mug just—went. Slipped, shattered on the tile. Brown liquid spreading everywhere.

"Coming," she said. Already grabbing her coat, stepping over the broken pieces. The guilt about Miyake's messages sat heavy in her chest: sixteen hours since she'd visited. Mother's morphine at seven. The clarity window closing. But now there was her mother's marble in a dead woman's hand, and that couldn't—that wasn't—

Couldn't be coincidence.

---

The shelter looked even smaller than dispatch said. Concrete box being eaten by the valley, fog turning the patrol lights into bruises on the glass. Officer Helene Klausmun stood at the tape—big woman, shoulders like a boxer, blocking some morning jogger who'd stopped to gawk.

"Detective Tearsen." Klausmun sounded tired. Night shift bleeding into day. "CSU's waiting on you. Haven't touched anything."

"Who called it in?"

"Local machinist. Anders Malm. He's in unit two, pretty rattled."

Troll ducked the tape. The shelter smelled like mildew and that other smell—death. Recent death. The woman sat where Anders left her, back against the clouded glass. Condensation beaded on her skin like she'd been crying. But corpses didn't—

No. Focus.

ID from her coat pocket: Nunito Volker, thirty-five, Götzis address. Work badge clipped to the coat—VOEHRINGER TEXTIL in blue, her photo from back when she still smiled at cameras. But stamped across in red: BEENDET 11.03. Yesterday. Fired yesterday.

The marble. Green glass, white swirl inside. Not just similar to mother's missing one—the same. She knew that chip on the surface. Tiny thing, you'd miss it unless—unless you were twelve and dropped it doing math homework instead of studying fractions.

Mage Meuller from CSU pushed his glasses up. Latex gloves squeaking. "Rigor's locked her fingers around it. Need to work it loose careful-like. But Detective—" Evidence bag in his hand, crystalline dust inside. "Glass powder under the nails. Fresh. Like she crushed something last hour before death."

"She was grinding glass?"

"Or crushing something. Particles consistent with thin glass. Like a lightbulb or—" He stopped. "Medical vial maybe."

"Her phone?"

Another evidence bag. iPhone, screen spider-webbed with cracks. "Forty-seven calls to same number. Between midnight and 2:17. All outgoing. That's not normal, that's—"

"Desperate."

"Yeah."

"What number?"

He showed her. French number, +33 code. Forty-seven times she'd tried.

"Dead line now," Meuller added. "But carrier should have records."

Troll crouched by the body. Pulled on gloves. The marble's surface had that specific feel—oils from decades of handling. She'd held this exact marble hundreds of times. Felt this same chip under her thumb.

Her phone buzzed. Miyake: *Your mother is asking for you. Very clear this morning.*

She killed the sound. Nunito's other hand was clenched too, but empty. Like she'd been holding something else. Something taken.

---

Anders sat in the patrol car, thermal blanket around his shoulders even though the morning was warming up. His hands kept moving—factory hands, scarred to hell, one thumb missing its nail from last winter's accident with the conveyor.

His phone lit up. Another message. Another. Another.

"Walk me through it," Troll said, sliding in beside him.

"I take this way when I'm late. Saves time getting to—" He looked at his phone. Twenty-six minutes late now. "That's thirteen euros Hauser takes. Half hour is twenty." Voice flat, doing math to avoid thinking about the body.

"What time exactly?"

"Six-fourteen. Checked right before I saw—" He showed her the phone. Seventeen missed calls from WORK.

"See anyone?"

"White van on the lower road when I was coming down. Wäger Heizung logo maybe. They do our boilers at the plant." His thumb worked at an old burn, nervous habit. "Heard something too. Before. Glass clicking, thought it was bottles. People dump bottles sometimes."

"The van—see the driver?"

"Nah, going too fast. But it's weird, them being out so early. They don't start til eight usually."

Phone buzzed. Text from HAUSER: *Don't bother coming in. Position filled. Final check will be mailed.*

Anders stared at the screen. Mouth opened. Closed. Opened. "Twenty-eight years," he whispered. "Twenty-eight fucking years and he fires me by text for finding a—"

"I'm sorry."

"Is there—" He looked at her. That desperate look. "Is there money? For finding bodies? For helping? I got rent. My Greta's at university in Innsbruck. I send her—"

"We can check with victim services. Sometimes there's compensation for witnesses who miss work."

But they both knew it wouldn't replace twenty-eight years.

---

Footsteps on gravel. Libaux Torrini came out of the fog, walking like someone who'd been watching before showing himself. Expensive coat gone shabby at the edges. Good shoes, old. Ex-Europol, his file said. Some suicide on his watch—witness went out a window rather than testify. Consulting now, reputation balanced on a knife edge between brilliant and trouble.

He nodded at Troll. No hello, just recognition of another cop.

Stepped into the shelter, eyes moving fast, cataloging everything. Stopped on the marble in its evidence bag.

"Three murders in Vorarlberg this year." Pulled out his phone. No preamble, no dance. Showed her files she didn't have clearance for. "All unsolved. Different colored marbles, same placement."

"Connected how?"

"Eastern district workers. Transit points—bus stops, platforms." Scrolling through crime scene photos. "First one eight months back. Bregenz station. Blue with yellow swirl. But here's what we kept quiet—all three fired the day before they died. Not quit. Terminated."

She looked at Nunito's red BEENDET stamp. "Like her."

"Like her." He studied the marble through plastic. "Bregenz victim—cannery worker, twenty-three years, let go for 'efficiency.' Innsbruck—hotel maintenance, fifteen years, budget cuts." Looking up now. "Someone's hunting the terminated."

"Or someone terminated is hunting."

"That's what I'm thinking." He crouched by Nunito. "She looks peaceful. They all did. No defensive wounds. Like they accepted it."

"Or didn't see it coming."

"Glass under her nails says otherwise. She fought something, just not someone."

Her phone lit up. Uma Arouje. Superior. She'd want her report, her theory, something clean for the briefing. Troll stepped away.

"Tearsen. What do we have?"

"Fourth in a pattern. All terminated employees, all with marbles."

"Marbles?" That tone. Deciding if this was worth her morning. "What pattern is that?"

"Someone's targeting fired workers. We need—"

"We need to not create panic. Keep it quiet til you have something solid. And Tearsen? Your mother's hospice is calling the station. Handle your personal shit."

Dead line.

Behind her, Meuller worked the marble free. Gentle pressure, patience. It came loose with a soft sound, rolled once in the evidence tray—that hollow click—then stopped. Through the plastic, she could see the chip clearer now. Remembered exactly when it happened. Twelve years old, kitchen table, fractions homework she didn't want to do.

"Detective," Meuller said, quiet-like. "Those forty-seven calls? Dispatch ran the number."

"And?"

"Groupe Marionne. French company. Their HR department's termination appeals line."

"She was appealing?"

"Forty-seven times between midnight and death. That's not appeal. That's—" He sealed the bag. "That's desperation, is what that is."

Troll looked at Nunito's face. Peaceful, Libaux had said. But forty-seven unanswered calls. A marble clutched like hope. Glass ground under her nails from something crushed—the vial Meuller mentioned, or maybe her fist against window glass in fury.

Her mother's marble in a stranger's dead hand.

Someone took it from the jar. Gave it to Nunito. Watched her die holding it.

The fog was lifting. Mountains showing their dark shoulders. Church bells somewhere counting seven. Her mother's window opening, and Troll standing over another woman's corpse, counting connections made of green glass and white swirls and twenty years of careful handling.

# **Chapter Two – Broken Footsteps**

The Europol database filled Troll's laptop screen. Same interface Libaux used eighteen months ago, before everything went to shit. He'd kept his login active—technically against regs, but who was counting? Four terminated employees. Four bodies. Four marbles. Took three hours to find the pattern, buried in cross-jurisdictional reports nobody bothered connecting.

"Look at this." Libaux turned the screen. "Voehringer Textil outsourced their HR six months back. Same timeline as the first marble murder."

Feldkirch station's conference room: burnt coffee, somebody's forgotten lunch going bad, and that particular smell of too many cops in one room for too long. Three other detectives pulled in, but Uma made Troll lead. Libaux consulting. The whiteboards looked like conspiracy theory madness—timelines, photos, red string connecting dots that didn't quite make a picture. Not yet.

"Which firm?"

"Groupe Marionne. Perpignan." He pulled up their website. Stock photos of diverse professionals grinning at computers like they'd never processed a termination in their lives. "Humanizing Human Resources Across Borders"—what bullshit.

Klaus Brenner from Bregenz leaned in. Coffee breath. "My victim—the cannery worker. Her termination went through them too. Form letter. No appeal process, just some number to call."

"Same number Nunito called forty-seven times." Troll cracked her knuckles—old habit when thinking. "What's the France connection?"

Libaux was already typing. Fast. "Eight hundred forty-seven terminations this year. All their clients. Forty-three filed appeals. Four are dead."

"Ten percent appeal rate?" Brenner made a sound. "Should be thirty for industrial terminations."

"Unless they make it impossible to find the process."

Troll's phone buzzed. Miyake: *Your mother asked about the green marbles today. She counted them this morning during her clear period. Said one is missing. She's very upset.*

Her stomach just—dropped. Her mother hadn't counted anything in weeks. Couldn't track sequences anymore. But muscle memory, that ran deep. Sixty years of counting those marbles, turning the jar, watching them tumble.

"Need to make a call." She stepped into the hallway. Linoleum squeaking.

Dialed the hospice. Two rings.

"She had forty minutes of clarity this morning," Miyake said. No preamble. "Insisted on the marble jar. Counted them three times. Eleven green ones. Should be twelve, she says."

"She's right. One's missing."

"She said someone took it. Said you'd understand why."

But Troll didn't. Not yet. Not—

"I'll call back."

---

The train left at 14:47. Regional service, stopped everywhere. Every small station, adding twenty minutes to what should've been three hours. Troll wanted to drive—rental car, autobahn, make time—but Libaux pointed out the French authorities. "Show up on the TGV, we look official. Show up in a Hertz special, we look like tourists."

The train took those Rhine valley curves too fast—Troll's coffee sloshed, nearly spilled. Industrial mess, then suddenly vineyards, then back to concrete. Switzerland couldn't make up its mind what it wanted to be.

Libaux sat across from her, reading printed files. Old cop habit—paper over pixels. Blue pen scratching in margins. Sometimes photographing pages with his phone. Click. Scratch. Click.

"Pattern's escalating." Not looking up. "Eight months between first and second murder. Three weeks between second and third. Ten days to Nunito."

"He's breaking down. Or getting confident."

"Or running out of targets." He showed her a list. Names, dates, rejection stamps. "Three more filed appeals in the last two months. All in Vorarlberg. All rejected."

"We need those addresses."

"Already requested. French bureaucracy being what it is..." He shrugged. That particular shrug that meant: could be twenty minutes, could be never.

St. Margrethen border. Train stopped. Border cops boarded, checking passports with that exhausted efficiency of people who'd done this ten thousand times. The officer paused at Libaux's ID—Europol expired, Austrian consultancy current. Long look. Move on.

"You miss it."

Not a question. She just knew.

"Europol?" He clicked his pen. Annoying habit. "No. I miss thinking we were the good guys. Stupid, really. We were just better-dressed thugs with paperwork."

"The suicide."

Click. Click. The pen stopped. "Marković. Serbian. Was going to testify about trafficking through Austrian construction. I had him in protective custody. Hotel room, two officers outside." He looked out the window. "Sixth floor. He went out anyway."

"Not your fault."

"I know." Click. The pen started again. "But knowing and believing—" He turned a page. "Like your mother's marbles. You know one's missing. But you don't believe it's connected. Not really. Too personal. Too close."

He was right. She hated that he was right.

---

Perpignan. Groupe Marionne took the whole third floor of some glass thing near the station. Could've been anywhere—Berlin, Barcelona, Birmingham. The receptionist looked about twelve, juggling three phone lines like a circus act.

"We need whoever handles the Voehringer account," Troll said. Her French was functional, not pretty.

"That's Monsieur Renoux." The girl checked her screen. Frowned. "But he's... oh. He's at our Collioure office today."

Troll and Libaux looked at each other. Collioure. Sixty kilometers south. Tourist trap in summer, fishing village in winter. Not where you'd expect HR operations.

"Since when?" Libaux asked.

"Let me..." Typing. Clicking. Little sound of surprise. "Three weeks ago. Temporary assignment for overflow."

Three weeks. Right after the third murder hit Austrian news. Hadn't made French papers though.

"We need that address."

The girl printed directions, basically threw them at them. Relief all over her face—these cops were leaving. As they walked out, Troll heard her answering another call: "Groupe Marionne, your human resources solution... No, I'm sorry, the appeals department is temporarily—"

The rental was a white Peugeot that reeked of cigarettes. No-smoking sticker on the dash like a joke. Libaux drove while Troll called Uma.

"Local police found something at Nunito's apartment," Uma said. Static on the line. "Box of documents. Employment contracts, termination letters, all from the past year. Like she was investigating."

"Investigating what?"

"Patterns. She'd highlighted specific clauses about dependent benefits. Parents with children seemed targeted for faster termination. Less severance."

"Send photos."

"Already in your email. And Tearsen? French police just called. They have a body."

---

The Collioure police station—converted house on a narrow street. White walls, blue shutters like every other building except for the tiny POLICE MUNICIPALE sign. Rousseau met them—mid-fifties, that tired look of someone dealing with tourist seasons and local politics in equal measure.

"Pulled from the harbor this morning," he said. Leading them to the morgue. "Miller Corbal, thirty-eight. Literature teacher from Montpellier. Missing two weeks. The sea doesn't always give them back quick."

Steel table. Body covered to the chest. Even after two weeks in salt water, she looked... dignified somehow. Right hand clenched tight.

"Had to x-ray to see what she was holding." Rousseau pulled up the image on a cracked tablet. "Glass sphere. Like a marble but old. Cracked."

"Where is it?"

Evidence bag. The marble was green like Nunito's, but fractured. Crack running through its center, splitting the white swirl into fragments. Different from Nunito's smooth sphere, but same glass, same age.

"Check for glass powder under her nails," Troll said.

Rousseau looked surprised but called the tech. Ten minutes later: "Oui. Glass particles. Fresh, before death."

"She terminated from her job?" Libaux asked.

"Let me..." Rousseau made a call. Rapid French. Waiting. Notes on the back of his hand. "Yes. Lycée Claude Monet. Budget cuts. Let go two weeks ago—day she disappeared." Another call. Quick exchange. "She filed wrongful termination through a company called—"

"Groupe Marionne."

"How did you—"

"They're all connected. Every marble victim was terminated and appealed through them."

Rousseau's tired face went alert. "We have their office here. Opened three weeks ago. Old fisherman's house by the port. One employee. Very quiet. Pascal Renoux."

"We need him," Libaux said. "Now."

"I'll take you. But first—" Rousseau opened a drawer, pulled out a file. "Miller Corbal's colleague. She'd been helping investigate something before she vanished. Something about children."

---

Fourth floor, municipal building. Fredda Stremler, Child Services Coordinator, Eastern Division. Harbor view that said 'I've been here fifteen years and know where bodies are buried.'

"Miller contacted me three times." Stremler's fingers drummed a manila folder. Her nails—Jesus, she'd chewed them down to nothing. One cuticle was actually bleeding. She saw Troll notice, curled her fingers under. "She claimed discrepancies in cross-border placements. Austrian kids placed in French care. Incomplete documentation."

"Which kids specifically?"

"That's the issue." The drumming got worse. "Case numbers she gave don't match our system. Either Miller was confused, or—" Glance at the door. Voice dropping. "Or someone altered the database after."

"Who has access?"

"Department heads. IT. And external processors—overflow contractors." She hesitated like she was weighing what to reveal. "Groupe Marionne handles our overflow. Which is always. We're down forty percent staff."

"Show me Miller's complaints."

She had two screens going now—flipping between them fast. Troll recognized the hunt, that moment when the pattern starts showing itself. Seven cases. Eight months. All Austrian kids, eight to fourteen. All placed with French families in seventy-two hours. All missing signatures, medical clearances, the works.

"These went through fast," Stremler said, getting angry now. "Usually takes weeks. Months sometimes. Interviews, home visits, background checks. These? Seventy-two hours."

"After the parents were terminated?"

She pulled up more screens. Cross-referencing. Her typing went frantic. Face going white. "Yes. Every one. Parent loses job Monday, kid's in French custody by Thursday." Voice cracking. "That's not protocol. We're supposed to try family reunification first, local options, support services—"

"Who approved these?" Libaux cut in.

"Final approval shows..." Squinting. Leaning closer like she couldn't believe it. "Pascal Renoux. Groupe Marionne, social services division." Looking up, confused. "But he doesn't do child welfare. He does employment paperwork."

"Unless someone authorized him special."

"I'd remember. Major breach. We had a scandal five years back—unauthorized placements. Since then, every special authorization goes through me." She pulled out a leather ledger from her drawer. Physical record. "I keep paper backup. Trust issues after the scandal." Flipping pages. Careful handwriting. "February through May. No authorizations for Groupe Marionne or Renoux."

"So he did it without permission."

"Impossible. System requires—" She stopped. Thinking. Back to the keyboard, fingers flying. "Unless he had someone's login. Someone with full access."

Pulling up system logs. Scrolling. Then freezing.

"Whose?" Troll asked, but Stremler's face already gave it away.

On screen: System Administrator - F. Stremler. Last login: Yesterday, 23:47.

"I wasn't—I was home. Midnight. With my daughter. We were watching—" She pulled out her phone. Shaking hands. Showed them a selfie. 23:52 timestamp. Both on a couch, popcorn bowl, TV paused on vampires. "She took this. Said we needed to document our 'mother-daughter horror nights' for when she's older and in therapy."

The joke fell flat. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking.

"Someone cloned your access," Libaux said.

"Has to be inside job. Remote needs biometric—fingerprint, retinal. But local access, from the building? Just password and ID card. If someone had my card details..."

"When'd you last change your password?"

"Six months ago. Required update." She looked sick. "Right before the first placement. Oh god. Those children. Where are those children?"

"We'll find them," Troll said. "We need everything. Names, addresses, contact info for the French families."

Stremler was already printing. Pages spilling out. "Here. Everything. But Detective, if these were fraudulent, if children were placed without proper authorization—" The implications hitting. "That's not adoption. That's trafficking."

---

The Collioure satellite office—old fisherman's house squeezed between seafood restaurant and tourist ceramic shop. Narrow, tall. White walls glowing in afternoon light but windows dark, blinds drawn.

"He's in there," Rousseau said. "Saw him go in an hour ago."

Narrow stairs to second floor. Small placard: Groupe Marionne - Services Administratifs. Libaux knocked. Nothing. Knocked harder.

"Police. Open up."

Footsteps inside. Slow. Reluctant. Door opened six inches, held by chain. Man's face in the gap—pale, sweating, eyes jumping between them like trapped mice.

"Pascal Renoux?"

"Yes."

"Need to talk about Miller Corbal."

"I don't know anyone—"

"She's dead," Troll said flat. "Found in the harbor. Holding a marble. Just like Nunito Volker in Austria. We know about the appeals, the rejections, the placements. Open the door, or we'll open it."

Chain rattled. Fell. Renoux stepped back.

Single room office. Desk against one wall, filing cabinets against another. Papers everywhere—floor, desk, pinned to cork board. Window view of harbor, boats bobbing.

But the box beside the desk. Cardboard, partially open. Shipping label: antique shop in Mulhouse. Through the opening: glass. Marbles.

"Explain," Troll said.

Renoux collapsed into his chair. Head in hands. "I need a lawyer."

"You need to start talking," Libaux said, "before more people die."

"I didn't kill anyone!"

"But you know who did."

Silence. Seagulls outside. Finally Renoux looked up. He'd been crying.

"They paid me," he whispered. "The terminated workers. Paid for priority review. Five hundred euros. Sometimes a thousand. They were desperate. I told them it would help."

"But?"

"I rejected them anyway. All of them. Took their money, sent form letters." Wiping his face. "My mother. Private care costs—"

"The marbles," Troll interrupted. "Explain the marbles."

He looked at the box like it might bite. "My grandmother's. Antique collection. Hand-blown, 1800s. Worth maybe fifty euros to collectors. But to desperate people? They were hope. I gave one to each person who paid. Said it was lucky. Said holding it during review would help."

"You sold them hope twice," Libaux said. "The promise and the marble."

"I didn't know someone would—I thought they'd be angry, maybe sue, but not—" Helpless gesture.

"Who knew about the marbles?"

"Nobody. My private addition. Made them feel they got something real for their money."

"Someone knew."

Renoux pulled out his phone. Scrolled. "Three weeks ago. Call from someone claiming to be Wäger Heizung. Heating company. Very professional. Had all the right references. Asked about appeals, about people who'd paid."

"What'd you tell them?"

"I told them—Christ, I told them everything." Words coming broken, like he was choking on them. "Names, addresses, amounts. And I mentioned the marbles. Said it was a kindness. Giving them something to hold."

"What number?"

He showed them. Austrian. Vorarlberg area code.

Troll called Uma. Walking to the window. Two minutes: "Walter Heindl. Former Wäger Heizung employee. Terminated four months ago. Assaulted his supervisor. Lost his daughter to French social services. The assault was right after his sixth custody appeal got rejected."

She turned to Renoux. "Did you process Walter Heindl's custody appeals?"

All color gone from his face. "Six times. Rejected six times. Orders from above—said he was unstable." He pulled up a file. "But he never paid for priority. Never got a marble."

"His daughter?" Libaux asked. "Was she one of the seven placements?"

Typing with shaking fingers. "Lena Heindl. Eleven. Placed in Toulouse four months ago. Express processing." Looking up, horror dawning. "I signed it. My first child services authorization. Didn't know I wasn't supposed to have that access."

"You stole children," Troll said.

"I was told they were in danger! Parents were threats! Emergency placement necessary—"

"Told by who?"

"Same number. Wäger Heizung. Said it was coordinated with Austrian services."

"Heindl convinced you to steal his own daughter?"

"No! Call came before Heindl's termination. Different voice. Professional. Said they were coordinating placements for at-risk children."

Troll and Libaux exchanged looks. Two callers, same number. Heindl wasn't alone—or someone was using him.

"The children. Where are they?"

"I have addresses." Pulling out a folder. Hands trembling. "But I haven't verified. Was supposed to do follow-ups, but—"

"But you were busy collecting money from desperate parents."

Renoux started crying again. "They're going to kill me too, aren't they? I'm a loose end."

Troll looked at the box of marbles. At the papers covering everything. At this small man who'd sold hope for euros and children for authorization.

"Maybe," she said. "Unless you help us stop them first."

# **Chapter Three – Salt in the Cracks**

Fourth floor, municipal building—Fredda Stremler had scored herself a harbor view. The kind of office that said 'I've been here fifteen years and know where bodies are buried.' Except now maybe bodies were getting buried because of what she didn't know.

"Miller Corbal contacted me three times," Stremler said, fingers drumming on a manila folder. Her nails—Jesus, chewed down to nothing. One cuticle actually bleeding. She saw Troll notice, curled her fingers into her palm. "She claimed discrepancies. Cross-border placements. Austrian kids in French care, incomplete documentation."

Troll opened her notebook. "Which kids?"

"That's the problem." Drumming got worse. "Case numbers she gave—they don't match our system. Either Miller was confused, or—" She stopped. Looked at the closed door. Dropped her voice. "Or someone altered our database."

"Show me."

Stremler had two screens going—employment records on one, child services on the other. Her fingers jumped between keyboards. Troll recognized the hunt, that moment when the pattern starts showing itself.

Seven case files. Past eight months. Austrian kids, eight to fourteen years old. All placed with French families. All missing signatures.

"Who processes these placements?" Libaux asked. His pen clicking. Click. Click. Annoying.

"Final approval goes through Groupe Marionne's social services division. They handle bureaucracy when companies terminate employees with dependents." Another screen. More typing. "Pascal Renoux's department."

Troll's pulse jumped. Renoux hadn't just scammed adults. He'd been moving their kids.

---

# **Chapter Four – The Wall of Silence**

# **Chapter Five – The Bitter Coast**

"I didn't. That was his addition. Said something about false hope needing a physical form." He stepped closer. "Your mother has marbles, doesn't she? Heindl mentioned visiting her."

Troll's chest went cold. "When?"

"February. He was looking for you. Found her instead. Lonely woman, confused, eager to talk to someone who seemed kind." His voice softened with false sympathy. "She told him about you. Your work. Your dedication to saving children. Gave him the idea, really."

"You're lying."

"Am I? Check her medical records. February 15th, sudden decline in cognitive function. Stress can do that to dementia patients. Guilt can do worse."

Her phone buzzed. Miyake: *Please come. She's asking for you constantly now.*

"The files," Stremler said. "Final chance."

Through the window, Troll could see the harbor. Somewhere in Perpignan, two girls waited. Somewhere in Paris, Toulouse, Nice, seven sold children lived new lives. Somewhere in legitimate homes, fifteen rescued children were finally safe.

She thought of her mother counting marbles, trying to make sense of what was missing.

"I have a counter-offer," she said.

"You have nothing to—"

"I have Pascal Renoux in protective custody. He's willing to testify. About you, the payments, Judge Kellner's murder—"

"Kellner killed himself."

"After you visited him. We have hotel records. Security footage."

Stremler's face didn't change, but his shoulders tightened. "Speculation."

"I also have Walter Heindl's suicide note. The real one. Names you as the person who gave him the victim list."

"You just said—"

"I lied. Like you're lying about my mother." She held up the USB. "Here's the deal. The seven trafficked children get returned to biological family or proper foster care. You arrange it, make it look like an administrative correction. The fifteen legitimate rescues stay where they are."

"Impossible."

"You have the connections. Use them."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then everything goes public. The trafficking, the murders you facilitated, Kellner's death. You'll spend life in prison, and yes, all twenty-two children get reviewed. But at least it'll be done legally."

One of the security men shifted, hand moving to his weapon.

"Don't," Libaux's voice came from behind. He stood in the doorway, weapon drawn. "Nobody wants a shootout in a church."

Stremler laughed—bitter sound in the sacred space. "You think you've won? Fine. Seven children returned. But you've condemned them, Detective. The Zhao family in Nice—they'll return Wei to parents who sold her sister to pay debts. The Martins in Lyon—they'll lose Amélie, send her back to a mother who traded food stamps for heroin."

"That's for social services to—"

"Social services failed them. I saved them."

"You sold them."

"I SAVED THEM!" His voice cracked off the stone walls. Control breaking. "Every placement, every payment, every forged document—all to save children the system abandoned."

"And the twins?"

He pulled out his phone, typed something. "Released. They're at the Perpignan police station." Another message. "There. Seven returns initiated. It'll take a month to process, but it'll happen. Satisfied?"

"The USB—"

"Keep it. Evidence of my good work." He turned to leave, stopped. "Your mother doesn't have long, Detective. February 15th, she told Heindl she wished she'd been a better mother. Said she wished she'd saved more children, like you do. That's what gave him the idea—parents who failed their children deserved to lose them properly."

"You're lying."

"Mulhouse Hospice. Ask Nurse Coscia about the visitor log. February 15th, 3 PM. W. Heindl, signed in as 'heating repair.' Your mother gave him tea. And a marble."

He walked past Libaux, security following. At the door: "I saved children, Detective. You've condemned them. Live with that."

They left.

---

The Perpignan police station was chaos. Henrik Foss sobbing, holding his daughters. The twins crying, clinging to him. Cameras flashing—somehow press had arrived. Uma on the phone, damage control with Vienna.

"Papa won't let go," one twin told Troll, almost apologizing.

"He doesn't have to," Troll said.

Libaux handled the paperwork. Troll stepped outside, called the hospice.

"Visitor log for February 15th," she told Miyake.

Rustling pages. "Yes, there was... W. Heindl. Signed in for heating repair. But Detective, that was before her decline. She was still clear then."

"What did they talk about?"

"I don't know. But that evening, she became agitated. Kept saying she'd done something wrong. Gave away something important. We had to sedate her."

The marble. Her mother had given Heindl the marble, thinking she was helping.

"I'm coming," Troll said.

"Hurry."

---

But first, she had to finish this.

Back at the station, she found Libaux with a laptop. "The seven trafficked children," he said. "I've been tracking the return orders. Stremler's actually doing it."

"Good."

"Is it? Look." He showed her a file. "Wei Zhao. Six years old. Parents have two prior investigations for neglect. But they're getting her back."

Another file. "Amélie Bernard. Eight. Mother's been in rehab four times. Failed every time."

"They're still the legal parents."

"Legal doesn't mean right."

"No. But it means lawful." She looked at the marble on the desk—evidence bag, tagged, chain of custody documented. "We do what we can within the system."

"The system that failed them."

"The system we're trying to fix."

Her phone rang. Unknown number.

"You've destroyed everything," Stremler's voice, controlled again. "But I have one last gift. Check your email."

An attachment. Audio file. She played it on speaker.

Her mother's voice, confused but clear: "Such a nice young man. He lost his daughter, can you imagine? I gave him my favorite marble, the green one. Said it would help him remember her. We all need something to hold when we're grieving."

Then Heindl: "Some parents don't deserve their children."

Her mother: "No, no. All parents fail sometimes. Even Troll—even my daughter. She tries so hard to save everyone, but she can't save me. I told her it's okay. We all fail. That's human."

Heindl: "Some failures deserve punishment."

Her mother: "Oh no, dear. Failure deserves forgiveness. That's what the marble's for. To remember that even broken things can be beautiful."

The recording ended.

Troll stood very still. Libaux put a hand on her shoulder.

"She didn't know," he said.

"She gave him the marble. Started everything."

"She gave him kindness. He turned it into murder. That's on him and Stremler, not her."

Through the window, she watched Foss carry his daughters to his car, one on each arm, their faces buried in his neck. Saved. Safe. For now.

Seven other children would soon make similar journeys, back to imperfect homes, uncertain futures. Because she'd chosen law over Stremler's vigilante justice.

"Go," Libaux said. "See your mother. I'll handle the rest."

She looked at the marble one more time. Green glass, white swirl, tiny chip from when she'd dropped it twenty years ago. Evidence of her childhood carelessness. Her mother had kept it anyway, called it beautiful.

Even broken things.

---

The drive to Mulhouse took three hours. She made it in two.

The hospice was quiet. That particular quiet that came before endings. Miyake met her at the door.

"She's been waiting," the nurse said softly.

Her mother looked smaller. Translucent almost, like she was already halfway to somewhere else. But her eyes found Troll immediately.

"My marble," she whispered. "Did you find it?"

Troll sat on the bed's edge, took her mother's hand. "Yes, Mama. I found it."

"The man—did he remember his daughter?"

"Yes."

"Good. Everyone should remember their children." Her fingers squeezed weakly. "I remember you. Every day, even when I forget everything else."

"I know."

"Are you angry? About the marble?"

"No, Mama."

"I wanted to help. He seemed so sad."

"You did help. You gave him what he needed."

"Did I do the right thing?"

Troll thought of Heindl dead in his apartment. Of Nunito Volker in the bus shelter. Of seven children sold, fifteen saved, all of them caught between system and circumstance.

"You did the kind thing," she said. "That's always right."

Her mother smiled, eyes closing. "The marbles are yours now. Don't drop them."

She slept then, breath light as tissue paper.

Troll sat holding her hand until the breathing stopped, sometime near dawn. The marble in her pocket pressed against her ribs—evidence and inheritance, memory and weight.

Outside, morning came to Mulhouse gray and quiet. Birds sang careful songs. The world continued its business of breaking and mending, saving and failing, holding on and letting go.

She had twenty-two files to process. Seven children to monitor. A report to write that would destroy Marcus Stremler or make him disappear into another identity, another crusade.

But first, she sat with her mother in the growing light, two women who'd tried to save what they could, failed often, succeeded sometimes, and called even broken things beautiful.

The marble caught the dawn through the window. Green glass, white swirl, small chip that proved it had survived its falling.

Still here. Still whole enough.

Still beautiful.

# **Chapter Six – The Heat of the Crowd**

Three days after her mother's funeral, Troll stood in the Mulhouse police station watching seven files turn into seven children coming home.

The first was Wei Zhao. Six years old, returned from Nice after fourteen months with the Dubois family. She didn't cry when the social worker brought her in. Just stared at her biological parents like they were strangers wearing familiar faces.

"She speaks French now," the social worker said quietly. "Forgot most of her Mandarin."

Wei's mother reached out, tentative. The girl flinched.

Troll stepped outside. Couldn't watch anymore.

Libaux found her in the parking lot, smoking even though she'd quit three years ago. "The Bernard girl's next. Amélie."

"I know."

"Her mother's high."

"I know."

He lit his own cigarette. "We did the legal thing."

"Yeah." She dropped the cigarette, crushed it. "Doesn't mean we did the right thing."

Her phone buzzed. Uma: *Marcus Stremler released. Insufficient evidence for trafficking charges. Judge ruled the adoptions technically legal.*

Of course. Good lawyers, careful paperwork. The system protecting itself.

"He'll do it again," Libaux said, reading over her shoulder.

"Not if we find him first."

---

The call came that evening. Unknown number.

"Detective Tearsen?" Young voice, scared. "My name is Lena Heindl."

Troll's chest tightened. Walter's daughter, the first child trafficked.

"Lena, where are you?"

"Paris. But they're moving me tomorrow. The people who bought me—they know about the returns. They're scared."

"Are you safe right now?"

"Yes. They're at the opera. The housekeeper's asleep." Voice dropping to whisper. "I heard them on the phone. Someone named Marcus told them to relocate me. Said you were coming."

Marcus Stremler. Still running his network.

"Lena, what's the address?"

She gave it—16th arrondissement, expensive area. "Please hurry. I want to go home. My papa—is he okay?"

Walter Heindl, dead by suicide four days ago. Troll closed her eyes. "We'll talk about that when you're safe. Can you get to a neighbor's apartment?"

"I'll try."

"Go now. We're coming."

---

They took the TGV to Paris, three hours of Libaux working his phone, calling old Interpol contacts. By the time they arrived, he had surveillance on the building.

"Family's still at the opera," he said. "Lena made it to the neighbor's. Local police are holding her."

But when they reached the address, the "parents" were already gone. Apartment cleaned out, only furniture left. On the kitchen table: a marble.

Clear glass, perfect sphere, with a note: *She was happy here.*

"Bullshit," Troll said, pocketing the marble. Evidence.

The neighbor, an elderly woman, brought Lena out. Eleven years old, auburn hair like her father's, eyes that had seen too much.

"Are you the detective my papa wrote about?" she asked.

Troll's throat closed. Heindl had mentioned her in his suicide note—*Tell Detective Tearsen I'm sorry about her mother.*

"Yes."

"Where is he?"

Not here. Not now. "Let's get you somewhere safe first."

---

The safe house was near Gare du Nord. French child services, one of the good ones—Libaux had a contact. While Lena was processed, they sat in a café across the street.

"Six more kids out there," Libaux said. "Sold. Hidden."

"And Stremler's still operating."

"Different name probably. Different country."

Troll's phone rang. Blocked number. She answered on speaker.

"You took her." Marcus Stremler's voice, controlled fury. "The Beaumonts loved that girl. Gave her everything."

"They bought her."

"They saved her. From poverty, from a father who couldn't provide—"

"Her father's dead because of you."

Pause. "Walter made his choice. I gave him purpose, targets for his rage. He chose to act."

"You manipulated a grieving father into murder."

"I showed him others who'd failed their children like he had. Natural selection, Detective. The weak fail, the strong survive, the smart profit."

"Where are the other six children?"

"Safe. Loved. Better off than they'd ever be with their birth families." His voice softened, almost paternal. "You returned seven children to hell today. Wei Zhao's parents are already fighting about selling her again. The Bernard mother overdosed two hours after getting Amélie back."

Troll's stomach turned. He was lying. Had to be.

"Check the news," Stremler said. "Mulhouse child services. Then ask yourself—who's really saving these children?"

Dead line.

She checked. Found it immediately—local news site. *Eight-year-old hospitalized after mother's overdose. Child welfare investigating.*

Amélie Bernard. Returned to her biological mother this morning. Already back in the system.

"Fuck."

---

That evening, they found the club in Pigalle. No name, just a red door. The lead was from Lena—she'd heard the Beaumonts mention it. "Uncle Marcus's favorite place."

Inside: mirrors everywhere, fracturing space into sharp angles. Bass thrumming through the floor. Smoke and perfume and sweat creating its own atmosphere.

And there—corner booth—Marcus Stremler.

Not old. Not young. Forty-five maybe, expensive suit, talking to a young couple. Showing them something on his phone. Photos.

Children.

Troll moved through the crowd. Libaux flanked left. But Stremler saw them coming. Smiled that pediatrician smile—kind, professional, utterly empty.

He stood, placed something on the table. A marble. Clear, perfect.

"Detective. You're persistent."

"You're under arrest."

"For what? Having drinks with friends?" He gestured to the couple. "The Laurents. Trying to adopt for six years. The system failed them repeatedly."

The woman was crying. "We just want a family."

"And Marcus can provide one," the husband added. "For a donation to his children's charity."

"It's not charity. It's trafficking."

Stremler's smile widened. "Prove it. I'm a consultant. I advise families on international adoption. All legal."

"Lena Heindl—"

"Was legally adopted through proper channels. The paperwork's immaculate." He stepped closer. "You have nothing, Detective. A few irregularities, some bitter birth parents, a confused girl. Meanwhile, I have lawyers, connections, and twenty-two successful placements of children in loving homes."

"Built on murder."

"Built on necessity. Walter Heindl was a tool. Broken, but useful. His targets were carefully chosen—parents who'd already failed their children. Natural correction."

"You're insane."

"I'm effective. Ask the Bernard girl how her overdosed mother's working out."

Troll's fist connected with his jaw before she realized she'd swung. Stremler went down, blood from his mouth spattering the perfect marble.

The club erupted. Security moving in. Libaux grabbing her arm. "We need to go."

But Stremler was laughing, blood on his teeth. "Thank you, Detective. Assault charges make excellent leverage."

---

Outside, rain starting. Libaux dragged her into an alley.

"What the hell—"

"He mentioned the Bernard girl. He's watching the returned kids." She pulled out her phone, called Mulhouse child services. "This is Detective Tearsen. The seven children returned today—I need welfare checks on all of them. Now."

"Detective, we're already—three have been removed again. Unsafe conditions."

Three. In one day.

"The other four?"

"Monitored. But Detective, these returns—they seem rushed. No transition planning, no support services—"

She hung up. Leaned against the wet brick.

"I fucked up."

"You followed the law," Libaux said.

"The law's fucking broken."

Her phone buzzed. Text from unknown number: *Check your mother's marble jar. Bottom layer. – M.S.*

Her mother's apartment was sealed, her things in storage. But the marble jar—she'd taken that. It sat on her kitchen shelf in Feldkirch.

"We're going back," she said.

"Troll—"

"He put something in the jar. When Walter visited my mother. February. Before any of this started."

They caught the midnight train. Six hours to Feldkirch. She didn't sleep, just stared at the marble from Stremler's table. Perfect clear glass, except—

There. Inside. Almost invisible. A tiny number etched in the center: 23.

Twenty-three what?

---

Her apartment at 6 AM. The marble jar on the shelf where she'd left it. She poured them out carefully on the kitchen table. Twenty-three marbles total. Eleven green, twelve blue.

Bottom layer. She separated them. Found it immediately.

One blue marble was different. Heavier. She held it to the light—something inside. A micro USB, sealed in the glass.

She wrapped it in a towel, smashed it with a hammer. The USB fell out intact.

On it: Everything.

Financial records. Real names and addresses of all trafficked children. The buyers. The bribes. Judge Kellner's murder—video from a hidden camera. Stremler strangling him, making it look like suicide.

And a note: *If you're reading this, I'm dead or disappeared. The children listed here need saving. The system won't do it. Will you? – F.S.*

F.S. Fredda Stremler.

Not the same person.

"Fredda was Marcus's sister," Libaux said, reading. "She tried to stop him. He killed her too. Made it look like—"

"Like she was part of it. Framed her own brother and died for it."

At the bottom, a list. Six names. Six addresses. The remaining trafficked children.

One was in Paris. The club in Pigalle—not just a meeting place.

A storage site.

"He's keeping them there," Troll said. "Under the club. Until he can move them."

Libaux was already calling his Interpol contacts. "We need a team. Quietly. If he knows we're coming—"

"The kids disappear forever."

---

They went back to Paris that night. Not on the train—helicopter. Uma had finally understood the stakes.

Interpol, French police, child services, all coordinated. The club raid would happen at 3 AM.

While they waited, Troll sat with Lena Heindl in the safe house. Had to tell her about her father.

"He died thinking he was saving you," Troll said. "He loved you. The things he did—they were wrong. But they came from love."

Lena cried. Eleven years old, lost between two languages, two families, two versions of what love meant.

"The people who had me," she said finally. "They weren't bad. Just... they weren't Papa."

"I know."

"Will I go to foster care?"

"We'll find you a good placement. I promise."

Another promise in a world full of broken ones. But what else could she say?

Her phone buzzed. 2:45 AM.

Time to move.

# **Chapter Seven – The Hollow Archive**

3:02 AM. The club's red door looked different with thirty tactical officers behind it.

"Basement access is through the kitchen," Libaux said, studying blueprints on his phone. "Secondary exit to the alley. We need both covered."

Commander Roussel of the Paris tactical unit nodded. "My team takes front. Yours takes rear. Child services waiting in the vans."

Troll checked her weapon. The marble from Stremler's table sat in evidence bag in her pocket—she didn't know why she'd brought it. Talisman maybe. Reminder of what they were really after.

"Remember," she told the team, "six children, ages eight to fourteen. They'll be scared. Might not trust police."

"Understood."

The ram hit the door at 3:03.

---

Inside: chaos. Even at 3 AM, the club had customers. Screaming, running, mirrors shattering as people shoved past. Strobes still flashing, bass still thumping, turning everything into stuttered snapshots.

"Police! Nobody move!"

But everyone moved.

Troll pushed through, Libaux behind her. Kitchen door, industrial steel. Locked.

"Stand back."

Libaux shot the lock. The door swung wide.

Kitchen: empty. Gleaming surfaces, smell of grease and bleach. But there—corner booth, concealed handle. Another door.

The stairs went down into black.

---

The basement wasn't what she expected. Not cages or chains. A dormitory. Six beds, children's drawings on walls, toys in a corner. Books in multiple languages. It looked almost... normal.

Except for the locks. Electronic, keypad entry. And the camera in the corner, red light blinking.

"They're gone," Libaux said.

But Troll heard something. Breathing. Quiet, controlled, but there.

"Check the walls."

They found it behind a false panel—another room. Smaller, hidden.

Three children huddled together. Two boys, maybe ten. A girl, younger.

"It's okay," Troll said in French, then German, then broken Mandarin. "Police. You're safe."

The older boy stood up, protective. "You're not Uncle Marcus's friends?"

Uncle Marcus. Stremler.

"No. We're here to help. Where are the others?"

"Taken. Hour ago. The lady came."

"What lady?"

"Madame Kellner."

Troll and Libaux exchanged looks. Judge Kellner's widow. She was part of it.

"Where did they go?"

The boy shrugged. "Airport maybe. They had suitcases."

---

While child services processed the three children, Troll called airport security. All flights, all terminals, looking for a woman with three children, no matching surnames.

"Got them," security called back twenty minutes later. "Terminal 2E. Flight to Singapore, boarding in forty minutes."

Singapore. No extradition for child trafficking.

They raced through empty morning streets, sirens splitting the dark. Made it to the terminal as the flight was boarding.

Louise Kellner stood at the gate with three children—all holding brand new passports, all looking drugged. Compliant.

"Madame Kellner," Troll said, weapon drawn but low. "Step away from the children."

Louise turned. Fifty-ish, pearl earrings, looking like someone's grandmother. "These are my nieces and nephew. We're visiting family."

"With forged passports and sedated children?"

"They took medication for flight anxiety. Perfectly legal."

The gate agents backed away. Security moved in. One of the children—a girl, maybe twelve—lifted her head slightly.

"Help," she whispered.

That was enough.

---

Louise Kellner in interrogation looked smaller. No pearls, no composure. Just a woman caught between grief and greed.

"My husband's dead because of this," she said. "Marcus promised no one would be hurt."

"Marcus murdered him," Troll said, sliding the USB evidence across. "We have video."

Louise watched her husband die on the small screen. Stremler's hands around his throat, making it look like suicide. She didn't cry. Just nodded.

"He said Martin was weak. Would talk if pressured."

"Your husband was going to confess?"

"He was sick of it. Sick of signing papers for Marcus, sick of the money." She laughed, bitter. "Fifty thousand euros per child. Do you know what judges make? Nothing. We had debts, medical bills from my cancer treatment. Marcus offered a solution."

"Trafficking children."

"Placing them with better families. That's what he called it." She looked up. "Have you seen where these children came from? Addicts, alcoholics, poverty—"

"That doesn't justify—"

"I know!" Louise slammed her hand on the table. "I know. But once you're in, you can't leave. Marcus made sure of that. Video of Martin taking bribes, photos of me with the children—leverage."

"Where is Marcus now?"

"I don't know. He contacts me, not the other way. But—" She hesitated. "There's an archive. He keeps records of everything. Insurance, he calls it."

"Where?"

"Archives Nationales. Reading room seven. He has someone inside—pulls files, plants evidence, makes problems disappear."

"What kind of evidence?"

Louise pulled out a tissue, dabbed her eyes. "Marbles. He puts them in files, marks territories. Says it's his signature, his art. Three in box seventeen, spine of ledger 247-B."

Specific. Too specific for lies.

"If you're lying—"

"I'm not. I want him caught. He killed Martin, used me, destroyed everything." She leaned forward. "But Detective, there's something else. Marcus mentioned your mother."

Troll's chest tightened. "What about her?"

"He said she was his first real success. Not trafficking—something else. February. He visited her, got something. Information about you, about your cases. Used it to identify targets."

The marble. Her mother gave Heindl the marble, but Stremler had visited first. Gathered intelligence.

"Six children safe," Louise continued. "But there are more. Marcus has a list—twenty more families waiting for children. International network."

"Names."

"I don't have names. But the archive does. Box seventeen."

---

4 AM. Troll sat in the hospital corridor while the three basement children were examined. One had bruises—grabbed too hard, moved too fast. Another was malnourished despite the toys and books. The third wouldn't speak at all.

Libaux brought coffee. Terrible hospital coffee that tasted like burnt rubber.

"Six saved," he said.

"Six of how many?"

"Louise is talking. Full cooperation for reduced sentence. She's giving us everything."

"Except Stremler's location."

"We'll find him."

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number. She almost didn't answer.

"Detective Tearsen?" Young voice. Female. Scared.

"Yes."

"My name is Sarah. Sarah Muller. You sent me back to my father yesterday."

One of the seven returned children. The legitimate rescue Stremler had mentioned.

"Sarah, are you safe?"

"He's drunk. Locked me in my room. But I climbed out the window. I'm at a neighbor's house."

"We're coming. Which neighbor?"

Address in Mulhouse, two hours away. Troll was already moving.

"Sarah, stay there. Don't go back."

"The man was right, wasn't he? Marcus. He saved me before. And you sent me back."

Troll closed her eyes. "We're going to make this right."

"How? My father has custody. Legal custody. You proved that."

No good answer. Legal didn't mean right. But illegal had meant trafficking.

"Stay put. We're coming."

---

They drove through dawn, leaving Libaux to handle the archive warrant. The French countryside blurred past—fields, villages, ordinary life that didn't know six children had been saved from trafficking or that one was hiding from the father she'd been legally returned to.

Sarah Muller sat in her neighbor's kitchen, holding a mug of tea like armor. Burn scars on her left arm, old ones, the kind cigarettes make. Fresh bruise on her cheek.

"One day," she said. "I was back one day."

"I'm sorry."

"The family I was with—the Martins—they were nice. Piano lessons, my own room, a dog." Her voice broke. "Why did you send me back?"

"The placement wasn't legal. Your father has custody—"

"My father burns me when he drinks."

The neighbor, an elderly woman, set down more tea. "I've called police before. Nothing happens. 'Family matter,' they say."

Troll's phone rang. Uma.

"The press has the raid story. Six children rescued, trafficking ring exposed. Good work."

"One of the returned children is being abused. Sarah Muller."

"Not our jurisdiction. Local child services—"

"Failed her. That's how Stremler got her in the first place."

"We can't save everyone, Tearsen."

"Then what's the point?"

Silence. Then: "The point is we save who we can, legally. Write your report. Include the Muller situation if you must, but we don't kidnap children from legal guardians."

"Even if the legal guardian—"

Line dead.

Sarah watched her, twelve years old with eyes that had seen too much. "You can't help, can you?"

"Not the way you need."

"Then I'll run. Find the Martins myself."

"They'll send you back. Without paperwork—"

"Then I'll run again. And again. Until I'm eighteen or dead."

The neighbor reached over, patted Sarah's hand. "You'll stay here tonight. We'll figure something out."

But they all knew there was nothing to figure. The system had rules. The rules said Sarah belonged to her father. The burns and bruises didn't change that, not enough, not fast enough.

Troll left her card. Promised to check in. Made calls to child services that went to voicemail.

Drove away from a child she couldn't save.

---

The Archives Nationales at 10 AM looked different from the raid footage she'd watched. Peaceful. Scholarly. Nothing like a crime scene.

Libaux met her at the entrance with the warrant and a tired smile. "Box seventeen exists. So does ledger 247-B. Louise was telling the truth."

Reading room seven was empty except for them and an archivist who looked personally offended by the warrant. She wheeled out the requested materials with the minimum possible courtesy.

Box seventeen: financial records, meeting minutes, correspondence. And there, pressed into margins, names. Nunito Volker. Miller Corbal. Henrik Foss. All in pencil, all in the same hand. Not Stremler's—too careful, too feminine.

"Fredda," Libaux said. "The real Fredda. She was documenting."

The ledger's spine had been cut, just as Louise described. Inside: three marbles. Clear, red, white with fracture lines. But also a micro USB wrapped in plastic.

On it: Everything.

Not just trafficking records. Murder evidence. Video files labeled by date.

February 15: Stremler in Troll's mother's room at the hospice. Her mother, confused but trusting, showing him photos of Troll. Talking about her daughter's cases, the terminated workers she was investigating. Giving him names without knowing it.

February 20: Stremler meeting with Walter Heindl, showing him the list of parents who'd "failed" their children. Heindl crying, grateful for purpose.

March 3: Judge Kellner's murder. Clear, brutal, efficient.

And more. Twenty-seven children trafficked over two years. Buyers in twelve countries. A network that made their six rescues look like nothing.

But at the end, a video dated five days ago.

Libaux appeared beside her. "Koller escaped. Private jet to Switzerland."

"Let him go. His investors saw the broadcast. They understand now—you can't privatize childhood without publicizing the crimes that make it profitable."

Outside the courthouse, protesters and supporters mixed into something more complex—people who understood that child protection was neither purely good nor purely evil, but necessarily messy.

On the giant screens, news channels tried to make sense of what had happened. "Acoustic Terrorism or Democratic Rebellion?" one chyron read. "Trial Hijacked by Truth" read another.

Troll's phone rang. Lena calling from Copenhagen.

"I've released everything. Every file, every recording, every piece of evidence from both networks. It's all public now."

"That's dangerous. People could—"

"Could what? Use it to save children? Expose corruption? Create chaos?" Lena laughed, tired but real. "It's all already happening. At least now they have true information instead of manipulated versions."

"What about you?"

"I'm done. Going back to my foster family. Going to be boring for a while."

"You earned boring."

"We all did."

She hung up. Troll stood among the crowd, holding the marble. Around her, people argued about what they'd witnessed—system failure, private exploitation, community rebellion, acoustic warfare. All true. All partial.

A child tugged at her coat. Maybe seven, with her father. "Was that real? The crying?"

Troll knelt to her level. "Parts of it were real. Parts were made to seem real. That's why we have to listen carefully and think about what we hear."

"But how do we know which parts?"

"We don't always. That's why we need each other—to compare what we heard, to remember together."

The girl nodded seriously, then held out her hand. "Can I see the marble?"

Troll gave it to her. The girl rolled it between her palms, listening to the small sound it made.

"It's just glass," she said.

"Yes."

"But it means something else too."

"Yes."

"Both things true?"

"Both things true."

She handed it back and walked away with her father, who nodded at Troll—acknowledgment from one parent to another, recognizing the impossible task of protecting children in an imperfect world.

The courthouse plaza was clearing. The trial would continue tomorrow, but the real verdict had been delivered—not guilty or innocent, but complex. The emergency intervention system would survive, scarred and scrutinized. Private companies would still circle, looking for profit. Children would still need saving. Parents would still fail. Systems would still break.

But now it would all happen in the open, with the echo of truth—messy, loud, human truth—making it harder to pretend simple solutions existed.

Troll pocketed the marble and walked toward the train station. Vienna continued its orderly existence around her, but something had shifted. The background frequency had changed. People were listening differently, suspicious of both silence and noise.

Her phone buzzed one more time. Miyake: *Your mother would be proud. Not of the outcome—outcomes are always imperfect. But of the attempt.*

The marble clicked against others in her pocket. Each one a story, a failure, a small tragedy that had led to this moment of public reckoning.

Not resolution. Just reckoning.

Sometimes that's all truth can accomplish—forcing everyone to reckon with complexity instead of hiding in simplicity.

The train to Grosses Walsertal left in an hour. Time to return the marble to the valley that had fought for its voice. Time to thank people who'd chosen chaos over silence.

Time to hear what echoes remained when the performance ended and reality resumed its ordinary failures and small salvations.

# Chapter Twenty-One ~ The Aftermath

October 3rd, 6:00 PM. Six hours after the trial broadcast. Vienna felt different—like a city that had witnessed its own nervous breakdown and was deciding whether to admit it.

Troll sat in the courthouse cafeteria, watching news coverage on three different screens. Each channel had a different interpretation: "Acoustic Terrorism Attack," "Democratic Sound Rebellion," "Trial Hijacking Exposes System Failure." All true. All incomplete.

Werner entered, looking like he'd aged five years in six hours. Set down two coffees and a suspension letter.

"Yours?" Troll asked, nodding at the letter.

"Mine. Effective immediately. Allowing you into the courtroom, failing to prevent the broadcast, 'conduct unbecoming.'" He almost smiled. "Twenty years of service, ended by telling the truth."

"You could fight it."

"With what? They're right. I let you disrupt the most important trial in EU history."

"You let the truth disrupt it."

"Same result, different phrasing."

They drank coffee that tasted like institutional giving up. Through the window, protesters and supporters had mixed into something more complex—people holding signs that contradicted each other, unsure whether they'd witnessed terrorism or revelation.

Libaux arrived with his own news. "Stremler got life without parole. Judge fast-tracked it—guilty plea, no jury needed."

"That was quick."

"They want this over. The EU Parliament's in emergency session. Twelve countries reporting copycat acoustic protests. Someone in Brussels hijacked their emergency speakers to play whale songs."

"Whale songs?"

"With a child's voice saying 'listen better' every thirty seconds."

Despite everything, Troll almost laughed. The chaos was spreading, but becoming absurd rather than violent.

Her phone buzzed. Helene from the valley: *Koller's equipment abandoned. We're dismantling it. Found something you should see.*

A photo followed: shipping manifest from RIVAJE. But the destination wasn't Europe. It was Singapore, Hong Kong, Los Angeles. Koller hadn't just planned European privatization—he'd been preparing global expansion.

"He got away," Werner said, reading over her shoulder.

"But his investors didn't. Look." Libaux showed his phone—financial news. "Stock market's responding. Every private security firm down 20-30%. Child protection services under investigation in six countries. Kellner's frozen assets being traced to fourteen shell companies."

"So we won?"

"We exposed. Whether that's winning depends on what happens next."

Another message, this one from Uma: *Your suspension is postponed pending review. Too much public support. Media calling you 'The Detective Who Told Truth.' Don't let it go to your head. Also don't come back to work until ordered.*

"Suspended but not suspended," Werner translated. "They can't fire you without looking complicit, can't keep you without admitting you were right."

"Bureaucratic purgatory."

"The most honest kind."

A commotion outside—journalists surrounding someone. Through the glass, Troll saw Lena Heindl, flanked by Danish embassy security. The girl who'd nearly become Koller's weapon, walking free.

Lena saw her through the window, nodded once—acknowledgment, not friendship—then disappeared into a diplomatic car.

"Where will she go?" Libaux asked.

"Forward, hopefully. Away from all of this."

Troll's phone rang. Miyake Coscia.

"Have you seen the children?"

"Which children?"

"All of them. The ones from the trial broadcast. Parents are bringing them to hospitals, convinced they heard their specific child's cry in the acoustic attack."

"Mass hysteria?"

"Mass recognition. Some of these children were actually in danger. The broadcast—manipulated or not—made parents listen differently. We've identified seventeen cases of actual abuse that parents suddenly noticed."

"So Koller's manipulation accidentally—"

"Saved children, yes. While trying to exploit them. The irony's not lost on anyone."

After the call, Troll stood. "I need to go to the valley."

"Why?" Werner asked.

"To return something that doesn't belong to me."

She pulled out the marble—green with white swirl, her mother's, the one that had started everything. It had traveled from kindness through murder to truth. Time to let it rest.

---

The train to Grosses Walsertal left at 7 PM. Troll found herself in a compartment with three others, all scrolling through news about the trial. One looked up, recognized her.

"You're the detective. From the broadcast."

Troll nodded, waited for anger or acclaim.

"My daughter heard it. The crying. She asked if those children were real." The woman paused. "I didn't know what to tell her."

"What did you say?"

"That some were real, some were made to seem real, and we had to help the real ones without being tricked by the fake ones."

"That's a good answer."

"Is it true?"

"It's as true as we can manage."

The woman went back to her phone, but softer somehow. The compartment filled with the sound of ordinary people trying to process extraordinary revelation.

At Feldkirch, Troll switched to the local train. The mountains rose around her, already shadowing into evening. The valley that had fought for its voice just hours ago, now returning to its ordinary existence.

But not unchanged. Through the train window, she could see lights in houses, people gathered in yards. Not protesting—talking. The acoustic rebellion had become something else: community comparing notes, trying to understand what they'd participated in.

Helene met her at Fontanella station. "You look tired."

"Everyone looks tired."

"The valley's exhausted. But proud. We did something today. Not sure what, but something."

They drove to the high pasture where Koller had built his amphitheater. The equipment was being dismantled by locals, carefully, like they were defusing history. The acoustic baffles that had silenced the valley lay in piles, ordinary foam and fabric when not weaponized.

"We're keeping some," Helene said.

"The chapel wants one. Says it helps with the singing."

Even in defeat, Koller's technology found purpose. Just not his purpose.

Troll walked to the center of the pasture, where the stage had been. The grass was trampled but recovering. She knelt, pressed the marble into soft earth, pushed until it was buried.

"For the valley?" Helene asked.

"For my mother. She gave it away in kindness. It traveled through horror. Let it rest in earth that fought back."

They stood in gathering darkness. The valley sounds returned—tentative at first, then stronger. Owls, water, wind in pines. The ordinary orchestra of a place being itself.

"What happens now?" Helene asked.

"Investigations. Reforms. Promises. Some kept, most broken. The system will patch itself and continue failing differently."

"That's cynical."

"That's experienced. But..." Troll paused, listening to the night sounds. "Today people heard children crying—real or fake—and couldn't ignore it. That's something."

"Something's not nothing."

"No. Something's not nothing."

They walked back to the car. Behind them, the marble rested in earth, its journey complete. From kindness through murder to truth to rest.

---

Back in Vienna, October 4th, dawn. The city waking to aftermath. EU Parliament had passed emergency funding for child protection services—not enough, but more than before. Private companies were withdrawing bids, spooked by public hostility. Koller remained free but irrelevant, his network exposed, his investors fled.

Troll met Werner and Libaux at their old café. All three suspended, all three uncertain.

"Job offers," Werner said, sliding papers across. "Private consulting. Three different firms want the 'team that exposed the truth.'"

"Ironic," Libaux said. "Private companies wanting us to investigate private companies."

"Or perfect," Werner countered. "Who better to watch them than us?"

Troll read the offers. Good money, legitimate work, the kind of second chance that rarely came to disgraced detectives.

"I need time to think."

"We all do."

Her phone buzzed. Message from an unknown number. She almost deleted it, then saw the first line:

*My daughter was one of the cries. Real, not manufactured. She's been missing for six months. Please help.*

Then another: *My son disappeared after the divorce. I heard him in the broadcast.*

Another: *The crying sounded like my niece. My sister says she's fine but won't let me see her.*

Within minutes, forty-seven messages. All parents who'd heard something specific in the chaos. Most probably wrong, traumatized by suggestion. But some...

"Look at this." She showed them the messages.

Werner whistled low. "That's a lot of desperate parents."

"Who else will they call?" Libaux asked. "The system that just got exposed as broken?"

Troll thought about the marble, buried now in Austrian earth. Her mother had given it to one desperate parent. That act of kindness had led to horror, but also to revelation, change, possibility.

"We investigate," she said. "Unofficially. No authority, no badge, just three suspended detectives with time and experience."

"For free?" Werner asked.

"The real ones, yes. The confused ones we redirect to proper channels."

"How do we tell the difference?"

"We learn."

They sat in morning light, three careers ended, three new purposes emerging. The trial broadcast had broken something that needed breaking. Now came the harder work: building something better from the pieces.

Troll's phone kept buzzing. More messages, more parents, more missing children who might or might not be real.

She opened a notebook, began writing: *Case 1: Marina Hoffmann, daughter age 7, missing six months, heard crying at specific frequency...*

The work continued. Different now—no badge, no authority, no system except the one they'd build case by case. But still necessary.

Outside, Vienna went about its morning routines, a city pretending nothing had changed. But in cafés and kitchens, on trains and streets, people were listening differently. Suspicious of silence, skeptical of noise, alert to the frequency of children who might need saving.

The marble was buried, but its work continued in every parent who'd learned to listen for the difference between real and manufactured crisis.

Not perfect. Not even particularly good.

But better than yesterday's comfortable ignorance.

Sometimes that's all aftermath can offer—the chance to fail differently tomorrow.

# Chapter Twenty-Two ~ Epilogue: Good Echoes

April 2026. Six months after the trial.

The office had no sign, just a number: 247. Third floor of a building in Vienna that used to house insurance adjusters. Now it housed three suspended detectives who'd found forty-seven missing children in six months.

Troll sat at a desk covered in case files, each one started by a parent who'd heard something in the October broadcast. Most had been false leads—trauma creating patterns where none existed. But eleven had been real. Eleven children found, seven returned to safety, four in situations requiring different solutions.

Werner entered with coffee and newspapers. "We made the news again."

The headline: "Shadow Detectives Find Another Missing Child - No Official Status, No Official Denial."

"The Boyer case?" Troll asked.

"Front page. Girl's safe, father's arrested, mother's in therapy." He set down the coffee. "That's four this month."

"Five. The Lindqvist boy in Stockholm was confirmed yesterday."

Libaux looked up from his computer where he was tracking financial patterns—Koller's network hadn't disappeared, just scattered into smaller cells. "We're getting too visible. The official system won't tolerate us much longer."

"Or they'll legitimize us," Werner countered. "The new EU protocols reference 'auxiliary investigators' three times."

Troll's phone buzzed. Text from Helene in the valley: *Spring finally here. Your mother's marble sprouted.*

Attached photo: the spot where she'd buried the marble six months ago. Tiny wildflowers growing in a perfect circle, green stems with white blooms.

"I'm going to the valley," she said.

---

The train to Grosses Walsertal carried tourists now—people who'd heard about the "Acoustic Rebellion" and wanted to see where sound had fought silence. The valley had become accidentally famous, unwillingly historic.

Helene met her at Fontanella station. "We've had to put up signs. 'Site of Democratic Sound Event.' The tourists love it. The locals tolerate it."

They walked to the high pasture. Where Koller's amphitheater had stood, locals had built a small memorial—not to anyone specific, just to the idea that communities could fight back. A plaque read: "Here a valley found its voice. October 3, 2025."

The spot where Troll had buried the marble had indeed sprouted. The flowers were delicate but determined, growing despite altitude and season.

"Every former installation site has them now," Helene said. "These white flowers. The botanist from Innsbruck says it's impossible—wrong soil, wrong climate. But here they are."

"My mother would have liked that. Beauty from burial."

They walked to the bus shelter where everything had started. Someone had installed a small shelf with a jar of marbles—not the original but the same idea. A note: "Take one if you need hope. Leave one if you have extra."

"Do people use it?" Troll asked.

"Every day. Sometimes the jar's empty, sometimes overflowing. It balances."

A message from Werner: *New case. Prague. Three children, diplomatic immunity complications. Can you consult?*

Work calling her back. But first, she had one more stop.

---

The chapel hadn't changed except for one thing—a new window installed where plain glass had been. Stained glass showing marbles falling like rain, each one a different color, each one catching light differently.

"Anonymous donor," the priest said. "The card just said 'For the children who were heard.'"

Troll suspected Lena but didn't ask. The girl had earned her anonymity.

Inside, the acoustic dampener from Koller's installation had been repurposed into a meditation corner—the enforced silence now voluntary, contemplative.

She sat there for ten minutes, holding the clear marble with gold spiral she'd kept—the perfect one her mother had saved for the case that never needed compromise. Still waiting. Maybe always waiting.

Her phone rang. Not a number but a name: Lena Heindl.

"Detective Tearsen?"

"Just Troll now. How's Denmark?"

"Finished. I graduated early. Law degree next month."

"Seventeen years old with a law degree?"

"Turns out trauma accelerates certain types of learning." A pause. "I'm coming back. Not to Austria—to Brussels. The EU Commission wants someone who understands both sides of the intervention system."

"That's dangerous territory."

"That's why they need someone who's already been broken by it. Can't be broken twice the same way."

"Lena—"

"I know what you're going to say. Be careful, don't become them, remember my father. I remember everything. That's why I can do this right."

After the call, Troll walked back through the village. The RIVAJE equipment was long gone, but the rebellion had left traces. Small speakers in windows, connected to nothing, just reminders. A child's drawing in the post office of the day the valley shouted. An old woman wearing a t-shirt: "I Was The Loudest."

---

Back in Vienna, she found Werner and Libaux had taken a new case while she was gone. Twin boys, disappeared from foster care, spotted in three countries simultaneously—the kind of impossible that turned out to be trafficking.

"The Prague children?" she asked.

"Can wait. These boys can't."

They worked through the night, tracking financial patterns, behavioral profiles, the small digital traces that even careful criminals left. By dawn, they had a location: Hamburg, a warehouse registered to a shell company that traced back to one of Koller's former associates.

"We call it in to official channels," Werner said.

"They'll take three days to verify."

"Or we go ourselves," Libaux suggested.

"No authority, no backup, no legal standing."

"But faster."

They looked at each other—three careers destroyed by truth, three new purposes built on necessity.

"I'll call our contact in Hamburg," Troll decided. "The one who owes us from the Lindqvist case."

---

Forty hours later, the twins were safe. The traffickers arrested. The media confused about how "suspended detectives with no official status" kept solving cases the system couldn't.

Uma called from whatever position she'd moved to after the trial. "You're making us look bad."

"We're making children safe."

"Same thing, different optics. There's talk of creating an official position for you three. Consultant status, limited authority, legitimate backing."

"And oversight, restrictions, bureaucracy."

"Yes. But also resources, protection, sustainability. You can't run on coffee and favors forever."

She was right. They'd found forty-seven children but missed hundreds more. The unofficial worked until it didn't.

"Send the proposal," Troll said.

"Already did. Check your email."

---

Spring advanced into summer. The office at 247 acquired a sign: "Alternative Investigation Consultancy." Deliberately vague, accidentally honest.

Marcus Stremler sent a letter from prison:
*I read about your shadow agency. You've become what I was trying to be—outside the system but necessary to it. The difference is you're not killing anyone. That's probably important. The children you're saving—tell them the truth about how they were saved. Don't let them grow up thinking the system worked. It didn't. You did.*

She didn't reply but kept the letter. Evidence that even monsters could occasionally observe truth.

The clear marble with gold spiral still sat on her desk, waiting for the perfect case. It would probably wait forever. Perfect didn't exist in child protection, only degrees of imperfect salvation.

But imperfect was better than nothing.

And nothing was what too many children had been getting before three suspended detectives decided to stop waiting for permission.

---

One year after the trial, October 3, 2026.

The EU held a memorial service for the acoustic rebellion. Politicians spoke about democracy and voice. The media called it "The Day Truth Went Viral."

Troll, Werner, and Libaux didn't attend. They were in Prague, following leads on the three diplomatic children. The work didn't stop for anniversaries.

But at noon, when the memorial moment of silence was held, they paused. Through the office window, they could hear it—not silence but its opposite. Across Europe, people had decided to mark the moment not with quiet but with noise. Pot banging, singing, shouting, the same rebellion now becoming tradition.

"Good echoes," Libaux said, the phrase now familiar in his mouth.

"Good echoes," Werner agreed.

Troll held the gold-spiral marble up to the light. Still perfect, still waiting. Maybe that was its purpose—not to mark a perfect case but to remind them perfection was worth reaching for even if never grasped.

Her phone buzzed. New message, unknown number: *My daughter's been missing for three years. I heard something in today's memorial broadcast. Not her voice, but something about her. Please help.*

She opened a new file: *Case 244: Martha Voss, age 9, missing since 2023...*

The work continued. Imperfect, necessary, true.

Outside, Europe made its noise of remembrance. Inside, three detectives who'd learned the difference between legal and right continued saving children one case at a time.

The marble caught the light, threw it back honest—golden spiral ascending endlessly, going nowhere, going everywhere, perfect in its purposeful waiting.
