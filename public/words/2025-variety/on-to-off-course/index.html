On To Off Course

← Words / 2025 Variety

A city archivist following reports of strange documents pinned to trees discovers they're all connected—fragments of a movement that transformed garbage into gospel, leading her off course into something she never expected to find.

Chanson Rubble had been the city archivist for eleven years. $42,000 a year to preserve what the city wanted remembered, ignore what it wanted forgotten. Her apartment: $1,400 a month for 400 square feet above a check-cashing place. The neon light buzzed through her floor all night. Green. The color of money she'd never have.

Her job: collect, catalog, preserve. But Tuesday morning, her supervisor—Mr. Fitzgerald, Irish name, African American man, the complications of history in his hyphenated existence—dropped a folder on her desk.

"People are pinning stories to trees. The mayor wants them documented before we remove them."

The first one was stapled to an oak outside the courthouse, the paper already soft from rain. Chanson had to peel it off carefully. Twenty-pound copy paper. The cheap kind. "The Bubble Lesson" it said. A father and daughter, soap bubbles, something about economies collapsing and floating at the same time.

She photographed it with the city camera—Canon from 2009, took batteries, always dying. Filed it under "Vandalism—Public Trees." Moved on. But at lunch, eating her packed sandwich (turkey, no cheese, bread she'd baked Sunday to save money), she went back. Read it again. The little girl named Pearl who said "Numbers are bubbles too, Papa. They pop."

Chanson's own father had said something similar in 2008. Right before jumping. Not from a building. From hope. Stopped trying. Stopped eating. Stopped. The bubble of him popped. She was twenty-three then. Still paying off his debts. Still.

By Thursday, she'd found seven more. Each one signed "Oyparaploo" in different handwriting. Or same handwriting, different hand. Hard to tell. Like the signatures on foreclosure documents. All looking the same. All different people losing homes.

At the abandoned mall—Randall Park, once the largest in America, now the largest absence—she had to crawl through a broken window. Cut her palm on glass. Blood on her khakis from Target. $19.99. Would have to last the month. Expected emptiness. Found the opposite.

"You're the archive lady," said someone in an inflatable unicorn costume, like they'd been expecting her. Voice muffled. Young though. Maybe twenty.

"How did you—"

"We've been watching you collect us. Tuesday. Thursday. Today. You walk the same route. We know your loops."

The unicorn led her deeper into the mall. Every store was... inhabited? Transformed? Alive? Spencer's had become a classroom. Someone teaching children to make puppets from bankruptcy forms—Chapter 7, Chapter 13, chapters in lives that ended one way, began another.

"Every component matters," the teacher was saying. Vessel, though Chanson didn't know her name yet. "Every action taken. Every decision—"

"Made," the children finished in chorus.

One child, maybe eight, maybe nine, looked exactly like the Maybe from one of the stories. Same paint-splattered hands. Same way of squinting at the world like it might disappear if she looked at it straight.

"Are you Maybe?" Chanson asked.

"Maybe," the child said, then laughed like this was the best joke. Like her whole existence was hypothetical. Foster kid humor. Dark early.

In the old food court, Chanson found a museum janitor she recognized from downtown. Wrack. Made eye contact every night when Chanson worked late, researching what the city had demolished for "progress." Two women. Working class. Different jobs, same exhaustion. Wrack was arranging trash into patterns while explaining to no one, or everyone: "Museums display the byproducts of their funding sources. We're just making the loop visible."

"The loop?"

The janitor—Wrack, $11.50 an hour, no benefits—held up a plastic bottle. Dasani. Coca-Cola product. "This paid for the museum through oil executive donations. Now it's art in the museum. The snake eats itself."

"That's..." Chanson started writing in her official notebook, then stopped. Tore out the page instead. City property, that notebook. $7.99 at Staples. Her whole life was other people's property. Folded the page into something that wasn't quite origami, wasn't quite trash, wasn't quite art.

"Better," Wrack said.

By the second week, Chanson wasn't archiving anymore. She was following. The stories led to places. The places led to people. The people were doing things that made the stories true retroactively. Or had the stories always been true? Had the truth been waiting for bodies to inhabit it?

At the bankruptcy court, she found ashes mixed with concrete, wildflowers growing through impossible cracks. Dandelions. Crabgrass. The weeds that survived. A group doing something—she couldn't tell what—with benches and paint and movement that looked like dance but felt like prayer.

"We're still doing something," they told her. Marie Debris Johnson, foreclosure on a house that had been in her family since 1947. "Always doing."

She found Marlène at a bar—dive bar, $2 PBRs, the kind where failure was a communal activity. Playing violin made from fishing line and found wood. The sound shouldn't have been beautiful. Was.

"The bounce back," Marlène explained between songs, "isn't up. It's through. You fall through until falling becomes flying."

"That makes no sense—"

"I know!" Marlène said, delighted. "That's how you know it's true."

Chanson started stealing time. Supposed to be archiving 9-5. Instead: following stories 9-5. Archiving at night. Unpaid overtime. Nobody noticed. Nobody ever noticed archivists until something was lost.

At the Contemporary, the museum had given up fighting what Whisp and the others were doing. Galleries transformed daily. Questions written on walls. Dirt on the floors—real dirt, from real ground, not the sanitized museum experience. Visitors adding to assemblages or taking pieces home.

"We're the archaeologists," a visitor told Chanson, "and the civilization. Both. Same time."

Chanson's apartment filled with fragments. Not archived—alive. Pieces of stories. Torn documents. Photos of people who might be characters or might be real or might be both. Her supervisor called, concerned.

"The mayor wants your report."

"I'm still collecting—"

"It's been three weeks."

Had it? Time moved different now. Not forward or back but through. Through the stories. Through the connections. Through the discovery that the city she'd been archiving for eleven years was actually a different city. A secret city. A city that existed in the gaps between what was documented and what was real.

She found the final piece at Jefferson Elementary. Not a story but a note: "For healing what has been off course for years."

Below it, a child's drawing. Dinosaurs dancing with pizza slices. A violin growing flowers. Bubbles with people inside them. All of it labeled: "ON TO OFF CORSE" (misspelled, perfect).

Chanson sat on the playground. Swings rusted. Slide removed—lawsuit risk. All her collected fragments spread around her. Started tearing her official reports into strips. Not destroying—transforming. A parent walked by, saw her surrounded by paper.

"You okay?"

"I'm off course," Chanson said, weaving the strips into something. A map maybe. A nest. A refusal.

"Is that bad?"

"It's exactly where I need to be."

She went back to city hall. Fourth floor. Archives department. Fluorescent lights that gave everyone the same gray pallor. Told her supervisor the stories couldn't be archived because they were still happening. Quit before he could fire her. Eleven years. No severance. At-will employment. At-will termination.

That night, she pinned her own story to a tree. Not about the others but about herself—the archivist who thought she was collecting something already dead, discovering it was all still living, still transforming, still leading people beautifully off course.

Someone found it the next morning. Read it. Started looking for the other stories. Started their own journey from on to off.

The movement had no name except maybe Oyparaploo. No leader except maybe Maybe. No purpose except transformation. No endpoint except continuing.

Chanson joined the mall people. Or the museum people. Or the bankruptcy court people. They were all the same people. They were all different people. They were all doing something. Living on nothing. $7.25 minimum wage when they could find it. Food stamps. Mutual aid. The real economy that existed beneath the documented one.

"We are thrilled," she heard herself saying one day, teaching someone new to tear paper just right, to make the fragments speak, "that you love these assembled things."

She meant it. They all meant it. The assembled things loved them back.

She never went back to archiving. But she was still an archivist. Just archiving different things. The undocumented city. The stories that wouldn't stay pinned down. The movement that was always moving. The connections that existed beneath surfaces.

In her notebook—not the city's, her own, bought with her last twenty dollars—she wrote: "Off course is the only course that leads anywhere real."

Then she tore it out. Pinned it to a tree. Let it go. Let someone else find it. Let the story continue without her authorship. Without her authority. Without anything but the truth that sometimes getting lost is the only way to find what was always there, waiting, in the city that exists between the documented and the real, in the stories that won't stay still, in the movement that has no name except maybe Oyparaploo, which might mean nothing, which might mean everything, which might mean exactly what it needs to mean for whoever finds it next.

We are all archivists of our own transformation, following fragments until we become fragments ourselves, discovering that the real collection is the one that collects us—leading us off course into exactly where we need to be, which is here, which is now, which is always in the middle of doing something that makes no sense except it makes all sense when you're inside it, assembled from all these beautiful broken pieces into something that refuses to be complete, that refuses to be filed under any category except maybe Oyparaploo, which is both a word and a world, both a movement and a moment, both off course and exactly on target.
