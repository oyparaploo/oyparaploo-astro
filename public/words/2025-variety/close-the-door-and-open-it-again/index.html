Close the Door and Open It Again

← Words / 2025 Variety

The milk expired on Tuesday but she doesn't discover this until Thursday morning at 6:34, standing in her kitchen in Cleveland Heights, the carton already tipped toward her coffee. The date stamp reads like an accusation. She pours it anyway, watches it curdle into small islands floating in the dark.

Her neighbor is putting out bins. Thursday recycling. She knows this without checking the calendar because his wheels scrape the same way they've scraped every Thursday for six years. Through the window, they make eye contact. Both attempt the expression—not quite smile, not quite grimace—they've been exchanging since 2019. Neither knows if this is required daily or if once a week would suffice. Neither will ever ask.

The fork in her drawer has a scratch down the middle tine. She's been trying to clean it since before the pandemic, running her thumbnail along the groove each time she washes it, convinced it's food residue. Last month, holding it to the light, she finally understood: the metal itself is gouged. She still scrubs it. Every time.

At 10:45 PM, Martin checks tomorrow's weather on the phone he swore he wouldn't touch after dinner. San Francisco: 62 and partly cloudy. It was 62 and partly cloudy at lunch when he checked. It will be 62 and partly cloudy when he checks again at 6:30 AM, pretending he's just checking the time. The weather app registers 847,000 identical checks across the Bay Area between 10:30 and 11:00 PM.

His refrigerator contains: expired yogurt, half an onion in plastic wrap, something in foil he can't identify without opening and won't open without identifying, three kinds of hot sauce, no milk. "Buy milk" is written on the notepad magnetted to the freezer door. It was written Monday. And last Friday. The pen hangs from its string.

He opens the refrigerator. The light illuminates nothing worth eating. He closes it. Opens it. As if something might have appeared in the interval. Closes it.

The houseplant arrived as a housewarming gift in 2021. Nobody remembers who brought it. The tag said "Pothos - Unkillable!" Half its leaves are brown, crisp as old paper. The other half shine green and keep unfurling new growth.

The woman in apartment 3B waters it Tuesdays. Sometimes. When she remembers. When she forgets, she floods it Thursday. The plant continues its performance: dying on the left side, thriving on the right, perfectly balanced in its contradiction.

She sketches it sometimes while her coffee cools. The drawings never look right—the leaves like ears, the pot's proportion always wrong. She keeps drawing. Ten minutes each morning. Not for art. For the ten minutes.

The utility crew has been fixing the same section of road since Monday. One person operates the jackhammer. Three supervise. This ratio appears in the cave paintings at Lascaux: one figure holding a spear, three figures pointing at the mammoth. It will appear in the Mars colony construction logs of 2087: one person welding, three consulting tablets.

During lunch, through office windows, seventeen people watch the crew. Each person eating at their desk thinks they're the only one noticing the absurd supervisory ratio. Each one composes mental commentary about efficiency, taxes, the state of everything. None will mention it aloud.

"Buy milk" migrates across lists, between notebooks, through apps. Almond milk in Berkeley. Oat milk in Portland. Whole milk in suburban Minneapolis. All unbought. All carried forward to tomorrow's list like a small inheritance of failure.

At the Kroger in Columbus, a woman stands before the dairy case at 9:17 PM. She forgot her list. She knows milk was on it. Also something else. She buys milk. At home: three full gallons already in the fridge. What she needed was eggs.

The text arrives at 2:47 PM: "Did you see that article about—"

The response, immediate: "Can we talk about literally anything else?"

"Fair. Jamie's making banana bread with mayonnaise."

Neither knows Jamie. Neither questions why banana bread requires discussion. They spend ten minutes debating moisture content in theoretical baked goods neither will ever make. This conversation happens simultaneously in twelve time zones, with minor variations. The banana bread remains unmade everywhere.

At 6:30 AM, the phone lights up beside the bed. Seventeen emails arrived overnight. Five from the same company about the same sale she unsubscribed from last month. And the month before. The unsubscribe link leads to a page confirming her successful unsubscription. The emails continue.

She makes a new rule: no phone until after breakfast. She checks to see what time it is. The notifications are already there, glowing their small emergencies. So much for that rule. Tomorrow she'll try again. And tomorrow. And tomorrow.

Someone in Tucson throws away expired milk at the exact moment someone in Detroit discovers theirs has gone bad. The synchronicity means nothing but happens constantly—this global constellation of small domestic failures lighting up like a map of everywhere humans live and forget to buy fresh milk.

The man in Detroit pours the spoiled milk down the drain, running hot water to chase the smell. He writes "buy milk" on the envelope of an unopened bill. He'll throw the envelope away later, unread, milk unbought, and write "buy milk" on tomorrow's envelope.

The alarm was set. She knows she set it. She checks anyway, 10:45 PM, like every night. While holding the phone, might as well check the weather. Still partly cloudy. Always partly cloudy in this particular way that means nothing about tomorrow's actual sky.

Her neighbor's kitchen light is on. She sees him standing at his refrigerator, door open, studying the interior like it's a painting he's trying to understand. He closes it. Opens it again. She recognizes the gesture. They've never spoken beyond the Thursday morning grimace-smile, but she knows this about him: he too is looking for something that isn't there.

Close the door. Open it again. The motor hums its patient tune, the same frequency in every kitchen, the cooling system that keeps working while we keep looking for what we forgot to buy. The light inside comes on, reliable as morning, showing us exactly what we have, which is never what we came for.

The scratch on the fork will outlive the person scrubbing it. The plant will continue its half-death, half-life. The milk will spoil on schedule. Tomorrow someone will promise not to check their phone and fail within four minutes, and this failure will be so human, so particularly universal, that it becomes its own kind of success—the success of continuing despite everything, of opening the door again though we know what's inside, of adding "buy milk" to the list with perfect faith that tomorrow we might remember, might change, might become the people who buy milk when they write it down.

But probably not. And that's fine too. The refrigerator light still comes on. The morning still arrives. The neighbor still puts out bins on Thursday. The ordinary world continues its ordinary failures, and we live inside them, patient as the appliances that hum around us, keeping time.
