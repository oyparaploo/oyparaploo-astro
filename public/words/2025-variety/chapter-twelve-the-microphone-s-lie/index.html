Echoes of Glass Stones

Chapter Twelve

The Microphone’s Lie

Entrepôt B had two faces. The public one wore banners and glass. The other—the one Troll Tearsen and Libaux Torrini slipped into—smelled like belt grease and breath, a catwalk spine over a ribcage of ducts. The warehouse’s private lungs exhaled a constant hush through metal, the kind of air that made paper curl and voices behave.

Below, the forum assembled itself in sentences about care. Lanyards murmured. Chairs knocked their small opinions into concrete. Someone tapped a microphone with a knuckle—tok—and the sound went everywhere and nowhere, diffused by a dozen speakers hung from truss like flowers with wires for stems.

Libaux led the way along the catwalk, steps memorizing the grid. “Count speakers,” he said, as if it were a prayer. “Find where the lies go to change clothes.”

Troll counted. Twelve public boxes, four delay fills, two side-fills disguised as decor. She listened for the seams between them the way a tailor finds a hem by touch. Somewhere near the roof, a superfluous mmm threaded the official signal, a faint sub-bass not on the console’s mind.

“Hidden transducers,” she said. “He’s feeding the room from underfoot.”

“Of course he is,” Libaux said. “You don’t stage a sermon— you stage a heartbeat.”

They reached the audio booth—plexiglass and polite menace. Through the window, a technician watched a console bloom with colors. Beside him, the smooth liaison from last night, wearing a smile like a handrail. He leaned to speak, and the tech’s fingers adjusted faders with the obedience of someone who knew where salary came from.

Troll took in the layout in a glance: main desk; a patch bay with more cables than reasons; two small boxes on a shelf neither labeled nor needed by any normal event. Her skin tightened. Those were the repeaters—smart, smug, born to be deniable.

Libaux found the service door and worked it with a key that belonged to a city with a thousand names. The latch offered a soft chk—consent masquerading as accident. They entered on silence feet.

“Routine check?” Libaux said to the tech, friendly as a lie that knows it’s temporary, flashing a half-retired badge with Europol’s ghost still faint on it.

The liaison’s smile didn’t change. “We have routine enough.”

“Then you don’t need us,” Libaux said, and didn’t leave.

Troll took the second chair. The console’s meters moved with well-behaved light. She studied the bus matrix; the public words bussed to obvious places. Beneath that, a submix labeled ROOM TONE fed the floor. Not tone at all. A curated hush, tuned to swallow consonants and carry vowels, the better to make policy sound like prayer.

On the shelf, the two smug boxes blinked. She opened one. Inside: a pocket transmitter and a shallow tray of glass stones. Not children’s marbles. Seeded glass—each with bubbles, fractures, tiny asymmetries exactly where a mic would hear them. Mage Meuller’s phrase arrived in her head with calm cruelty: He’s signing in glass.

Libaux lifted an eyebrow: Proof?

Troll placed a single stone on the tray. A contact mic under it took the weight and bloomed its small tik through the hidden floor, a pulse no human could resist calling significant. On the floor beyond the booth glass, heads lifted a fraction, like grass in a wind that had not yet committed.

“He rolls these during key points,” Troll said quietly. “The room agrees with him by design.”

The liaison tilted his head, measuring their sentences as if scanning barcodes. “You make everything sound so conspiratorial. We’re just fighting the acoustics.”

Troll glanced at him. “So are we.”

The host stepped to the lectern. One, two. Bonsoir. Applause arrived on cue, a pre-recorded warmth mixed into live hands until the ratio made sincerity a math problem. The word protection rode the first paragraph like a polished boat.

Libaux leaned in. “We give the boat a leak.”

They worked in whispers, fingers doing nonviolence. Troll nudged the room-tone bus down two decibels, enough that consonants could stand up without being pushed. She shifted one delay fill half a heartbeat later, turning echo into argument. Libaux found a feedback notch and widened it by a hair, not to shriek but to scare—a ghost at the edge of the PA’s throat.

The host faltered on a phrase and recovered. The room twitched, dismissed it. The liaison’s smile thinned to truthlessness. He reached to correct the delay. Libaux’s hand got there first, palm easy on the knob.

“Don’t,” he said softly.

“Why?” the liaison asked, smiling the question like a dare.

“Because we’ll feed your applause at the wrong time,” Libaux said. “And your donors will learn to hear.”

The liaison turned his calm toward Troll instead. “You came to a forum to do tech theater.”

“We came to a theater to meet a forum,” she said. She kept her eyes on the tray of stones and the small box blinking its bland little blink.

A side door opened below. Fredda Stremler entered at a moderate speed that meant he owned duration. He went not to the stage but to the front row, shook three hands as if telling a story with the grips. His shoes made honest sounds on the floor. He sat. He looked up into the lights like he pitied them.

The host gestured, deferential as gravity. Stremler ascended, took the lectern, and placed his notes in a tidy stack he would not need. Microphones leaned toward him.

He did not speak at once. He let the room make him. The hush obeyed.

“Transparent work,” he began, and the adjective behaved as if it were not made of air. “We are here to ensure oversight crosses borders more easily than harm.”

Low, warm, humble as returns policy. Words that did not invite nouns like guilt. Troll glanced at the ROOM TONE bus and took it down another whisper. Libaux slipped their pre-recorded applause off the program and replaced it with a file of coughs. When Stremler paused for his first handclap, the sound that arrived instead was a polite clearing of throats, a thousand tiny refusals.

A ripple crossed the audience—amusement, then confusion, then the private self-consciousness that asks whether one’s palms are part of the problem. Stremler did not flinch, but the smile that lived inside his eyes moved a millimeter out of its seat.

He continued. “We are not in the business of outcomes. We are in the business of—”

Troll opened the smug little box and took out one seeded stone. She set it lightly on the tray. In the under-floor, the transducer sang its signature. The warehouse picked up a halo note, a borrowed omen, exactly as designed.

“—oversight,” Stremler finished, and the halo tried to seat itself under the word like a tail under a dog.

Libaux leaned to the console. He routed that undernote back to a spare send, flipped its phase, fed it back into the room a breath late. The halo arrived, then un-arrived. The word oversight fluttered; the tail found itself cut by its own echo.

Tiny, the kind of sabotage a musician would call tasteful.

Troll put her mouth near the talkback mic and breathed her mother’s name into it, softer than paper. The syllables rode the same under-floor channel that usually carried agreement. Two vents exhaled the name like a secret they could not keep.

Libaux glanced at her. She kept her face set. On the floor, two heads turned as if they had heard a person they knew call from another aisle in a supermarket.

Stremler’s smile did not break. “We are here to ensure that when files cross frontiers they do not lose their faces.”

“Then show the faces,” someone said, not yet brave enough to be heard, exactly the bravery Stremler cultivated.

The liaison tried to switch applause back in. Libaux lifted his wrist and showed him a patch cord as if it were a leash. “Wrong cue,” he said.

Troll tilted the tray of stones until one rolled to the edge and stopped. The small contact mic made a patient tik. She thought of the bus shelter, of the breakwater, of Pigalle’s bass inside the pristine sphere. She thought of the archive’s ledger, marbles hidden like punctuation inside a book history had tried to edit.

Stremler reached the paragraph where he needed myth. “What we are against,” he said, lowering the pitch of the last word as if bending metal with his mouth, “is the weaponization of noise. The fogging of rooms.”

Libaux flicked one switch. The PA gave a half-second of its opinion— a soft, suppressed ee at the edge of feedback. Not scream—threat. The room leaned away as one organism, then returned because money tells spines how to posture.

Troll lifted the ROOM TONE send one notch and routed the cough file to it instead, not to embarrass him but to make the room breathe on its own terms. The coughs arrived at random. Stremler paused, waited for obedience. The coughs did as they liked.

A woman stood in the aisle. No lanyard. A public body that refused to be private. “I have a question,” she said, voice trembling the way good bridges tremble—flexion, not failure. “If oversight is not outcomes, then what do you call children who vanish between registers?”

Security turned in their chairs; the liaison lifted two fingers. Troll saw it in the glass and felt the old habit of rooms protect rooms.

Libaux cut the sub beneath the lectern. Suddenly Stremler belonged to the same air as everyone else. When he answered, the warehouse stopped carrying his commas like beads.

“Madam, we don’t adjudicate— we facilitate.”

“And the child?” she asked.

He looked at her the way a table looks at a hand placed on it: politely, insistently neutral. “We ensure the process is sound.”

The sentence laid its polite throat on the stage. Troll pressed record.

On the tray, a single stone waited for a cue. The liaison shifted to roll it. Troll caught his wrist. They looked at each other. His smile was a small machine. “Aren’t you supposed to be at a funeral?” he asked, almost kindly.

“You’re not invited,” she said, and let go.

Stremler stayed calm. The talent of a man who had practiced never needing volume. “We are not here to debate anecdotes.”

The word struck the room with a plastic sound. Troll’s hand moved before she had planned it. She slid the seeded stone into the liaison’s palm and, with the other hand, pulled the patch that fed the floor’s agreeable halo.

“Roll it,” she told him.

He did, because people do what they are told in the moment they are most certain they do not. The stone ticked, modest, precise. The under-floor did not answer. The sound stayed small and plain and lonely, a child’s toy on a warehouse floor.

It was not much. It was everything. The room heard it without instruction. An audible fraction of the audience tilted their heads, like dogs, like machines taught what “human” means. The marbles had always lied. Tonight, one told the truth.

A hand clapped at the back—one— then another—two— not applause for a man or a sentence, but the body’s approval of unvarnished physics. The liaison’s smile failed to renew itself.

Stremler took in the shift the way a pilot reads a panel. Nothing in his face changed, and all of it did. He closed his notes with two fingers and set them aside where they looked respectable while doing nothing. “We’ll take written questions,” he said, without the microphone’s adoration. He nodded to the host; the host obeyed the nod written between syllables.

Troll pressed stop. Libaux leaned on the console as if tired and not yet allowed to be.

The liaison adjusted his cuff and exhaled through his nose. “You don’t win with coughs and cables,” he said softly. “He will remaster the room by dinner.”

Troll put the seeded stone in her pocket with the others, a small, defiant weight. “He can remaster the room,” she said. “We’re remastering the city.”

“Big project,” the liaison said.

“Big budget,” Libaux replied. “Invisible line items.”

For a moment, the man looked like what he was—someone who had gone to work and found himself officiating a magic trick he had not invented. He let his eyes give away nothing and left them there anyway.

They exited along the service corridor. The river spoke its exhausted speech beyond the dock’s half-open door. The warehouse had the smell of having pretended it mattered and now relaxing into being a building again.

On the quay, Troll’s phone lit. Uma Arouje: Flight 06:10. I expect you on it. Do not fail to attend. This is not a suggestion.

Another message beneath it, a new subject line from Mage Meuller:
Your cough track was art. Send me the stone signature you muted. If he’s coding pieces, we can build a lexicon.

And from Aïcha:
Stream clipped, but it reached us. Some people heard what they were not supposed to. Thank you for the wrong applause.

Troll stood with her back to the river wall and read them all, then slipped the phone into her coat like a note into a pocket of a dress she hadn’t decided whether to wear. She looked at Libaux. “Tomorrow Vienna,” she said. “Today we made a room remember itself.”

He nodded, jaw set where humor usually lived. “Rooms talk,” he said. “Streets gossip.”

Troll set the newest stone among the others in her palm: clear, red, cracked, blue, white coil, seeded plain. They knocked together once—tik—not the engineered halo, not a stage cue, just glass telling the truth about proximity.

“Next,” she said.

“Next,” he echoed.

Paris fed them back the word as a quieter copy, and the river dragged it a little way downstream, where it could be repeated by someone who hadn’t thought of it on their own.
