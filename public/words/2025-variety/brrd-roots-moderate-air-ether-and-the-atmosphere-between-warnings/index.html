In The Middle.

The sky looked fine. Not brilliant. Not heavy. Just fine.

At first glance, the neighborhood moved as it always did. Lawnmowers hummed in suburban syncopation. A cyclist waved lazily as he passed, earbuds tucked into a tempo no one else could hear. Children drifted around chalk-sketched sidewalks, kicking up invisible swirls of dust.

And yet—there was something unspoken in the atmosphere. A pause. A hint of weight where there should be none.

Kara noticed it as she stepped off her porch with a cup of coffee cooling rapidly in her hand. She took a breath. The air didn’t sting. It didn’t soothe either. It simply... existed.

This was *moderate* air quality, the specialists said. A term that sounded more like a compromise than a condition. The kind of description that wouldn’t halt a jogger mid-stride or send school districts scrambling, but might—over time—etch itself quietly into lungs unprepared for its patience.

She remembered when the air was clearer. There was a difference then, though slight. The way the morning breeze moved faster. The way plants stood just a little straighter. The way breathing felt like something you earned, not just inherited.

Now, the changes were more hesitant. Microscopic particles from traffic, industry, smoke trailing in from places with names she didn’t know—these moved silently through her street, unannounced, unresolved.

No one talked about it. Not because they didn’t care, but because there was no moment demanding it. No red alerts. No apocalyptic sunsets. Just the slow drift of the tolerable.

But Kara knew better. The middle ground had always been the place where things began to tip. Where the future negotiated its terms behind closed doors of habit and convenience. And when the warnings come quietly, they’re the easiest to ignore.

She sipped her coffee, let it linger at the back of her throat.

Today’s air was livable. But barely.
