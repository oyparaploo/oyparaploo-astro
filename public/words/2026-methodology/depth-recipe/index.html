DEPTH RECIPE — how deep space is made, for the Marks new-territory sessions

← Writings / 2026 Methodology

Written October 31, 2026, from twenty-five reference pictures Reach chose (the zip named RESOURCE SELECTIONS). Each picture was looked at whole and close, then measured in eight bands from top to bottom for lightness, contrast, colour strength and fine detail.

The reference pictures never go into the repository. This file is words only. Every pixel of a Marks artwork stays Reach's own drawings and Reach's own photographs. The zip held no folder of Reach's own photographs, so none of these twenty-five pictures is material. (One picture, CCCMT-033, a red field with glowing shapes, has no artist's name. If it is Reach's own photograph, he says so, and only then can it become material.)

The recipe is at the end. Before it come the study of each picture and the techniques they share.

Part one: each picture, one at a time

Positions are given as a fraction of the picture's height, measured from the top. "Far" means near the horizon; "near" means near the bottom edge.

Albert Goodwin, Mountain Mist. The valley floor meets the mountains about two-thirds down. The near bank is a warm gold slope on the left, coming in on a diagonal from the bottom-left corner. A river and a road start wide at the bottom and narrow as they run to a small bridge laid across the valley. The mountains behind are blue-grey and veiled. Measured, the mountains have under half the colour strength of the foreground and about two-thirds of its fine detail. The brushwork shrinks too: broad touches on the near bank, tiny ones in the far village. A tiny figure on the road gives the scale. The eye enters on the river at the bottom, follows it to the bridge, and climbs into the mist.

Anselm Kiefer, Das einzige Licht (the snowy field with the bench). The horizon sits very high, about one-fifteenth down. The whole field is rows of short dark stubble marks. Near the bottom the marks are long and thick and far apart; toward the top they become tiny ticks packed close together, and the rows bend toward a point at the upper right. Colour is almost absent, and contrast and detail stay nearly even from top to bottom. The depth is carried almost entirely by the size and spacing of repeated marks. A real bench loaded with branches sits across the bottom, in front of everything. Of all twenty-five, this is the picture closest to the Marks: depth made from marks shrinking.

Anselm Kiefer, the bridge under the gold field. The bridge deck is about three-quarters down. The bridge's arches repeat, and each one to the left is smaller than the one before: five steps that shrink to almost nothing. The water below is dark green and heavily textured, carrying twice the fine detail of the gold above. The gold field and the three hanging figures are flat, with no depth. This picture shows that depth can live in one band while the rest stays flat.

Anselm Kiefer, Unternehmen Seelöwe. The horizon is a pale strip about an eighth down. Thick pale strokes fan out from one point on that horizon, a little left of centre, like furrows. Near the bottom they are long, wide and far apart; near the horizon they are short, thin and crowded. Light marks on a dark ground carry the whole depth. A zinc tub painted with ships lies across the lower middle, in front of the furrows.

Anselm Kiefer, the figure walking toward the fence. The horizon is about a quarter down, under a gold sky, and is drawn as a row of fence posts that get smaller toward the left. A figure seen from behind walks straight into the picture at the centre. The viewer stands where the figure started. A pale green puddle at the bottom mirrors the figure upside down, doubling the distance from the near edge to the horizon.

Anselm Kiefer, the forest opening. Dark trees lean in from both sides like stage wings. Between them, a little above the middle, is the brightest place in the picture: an opening of gold light. A band of low dark shrubs, about seven-tenths down, closes the far ground. Below it is a pale clearing, and a small figure seen from behind stands at the bottom centre, facing in. The picture is darkest at its top corners and lightest at the far opening. The clearing is calmer than the trees, with about three-quarters of their detail.

Anselm Kiefer, the raised furrow with straw. The horizon is about a third down. One raised furrow runs from the bottom centre straight to the middle of the horizon. Real straw stands huge in the foreground; the far field is fine and pale. Measured, fine detail doubles from the horizon band to the lower bands, and lightness falls from very light at the far edge to dark near the bottom: far is light, near is dark.

Anselm Kiefer, Cosenza (the rock in the sea). The sea's horizon is about two-thirds down. One great rock stands in front of it and cuts across it. The sea strokes are small and fine at the horizon and broad and thick at the bottom. The space is shallow: one mass in front of one flat plane.

CCCMT-033 (red field, glowing shapes). There is no ground. Shapes with sharp bright rims read as near. One shape with a faint, dim rim (left middle) reads as behind them. The corners darken and the top is lighter. Depth comes only from how strong each edge is, and from the light falling off toward the edges of the picture.

Georg Baselitz, the crouching figure. This is a figure, not a landscape. The feet at the bottom are large and close; the head at the top is small and far. That is foreshortening: the body tips toward the viewer. Heavy dark outlines lie on the near edges, and a small blue shadow sits under the feet. The bare pale canvas around the figure reads as open space.

George Price Boyce, In the Auvergne. The horizon of far hills is about a fifth down. The near slope is a huge diagonal from the lower-left corner, covered in stones. The stones are large at the bottom and shrink as the slope rises. Two pines stand on the slope's edge, in front of the drop to the valley. Field strips across the valley become thinner and more crowded with distance. The far hills are pale mauve, with almost no colour: measured, about a third of the foreground's colour strength and half its detail. The eye slides down the slope into the valley, then follows the field rows to the village.

The butterfly-drawn sulky print (signed Dalí; file named Harness-Racing). A single drawn horizon line sits about three-fifths down. Thin ruled lines fan out from one point on it across bare white paper, and that alone builds a deep floor out of almost nothing. A small printed picture of two trotters lies on that floor, close to the viewer. The sulky's wheels are drawn as overlapping, repeated circles. Everything above the horizon (the butterflies, the driver) floats without depth.

J. M. W. Turner, On the River Ouse. The horizon is a little below the middle. The river is narrow at the pale cathedral and widens to fill the bottom. Dark green banks frame both sides and close in toward the far point. Above the horizon, contrast measures less than half of what it is below. The far point is the brightest and warmest place: the light comes from behind the distance. A boat's mast and its reflection make one long vertical that measures the water.

Joseph Zbukvic, the country street. The street runs away to the right of centre, about two-thirds to three-quarters down. The building and big tree on the left are dark and near; the trees down the street fade to pale wash. Cars and people shrink with distance, and the power-pole wires lead the eye down the street. The road in front is a pale, quiet wash.

Joseph Zbukvic, the boatyard. A big dark hull fills the left, near and heavy. A middle boat is grey, and the masts behind it are pale ghosts. The foreground is a broad flat wash, and measured, it has under half the contrast of the boats. Small working figures stand in the middle distance and set the scale. The richest band is the middle, not the bottom.

Luc Tuymans, the two figures walking toward the mountain. Two dark figures seen from behind walk into a pale blue-grey mountain distance. The light is behind them, so near is dark and far is pale. A path of pale streaks runs in under their feet.

Monira Al Qadiri, the red room with the two shells. A curved room with the brightest red glow at the back and top, where the room opens. Two great dark shells hang on either side in front, like wings. A small figure in the middle distance gives the scale. The floor is darkest at the bottom, and colour is strongest at the far glow. (The figure is blurred by movement; blur is not a tool here.)

Njideka Akunyili Crosby, the layered garden. There is no horizon. The picture is layers stacked like sheets of glass: a far layer of patterned printed pictures, then flat pale-green leaf shapes, then the solid painted branches and pink flowers in front. The front layer is the most solid, the most detailed and the most strongly coloured. The back layers are flatter, paler and see-through. Depth comes from the order of the layers.

Robert Zehnder (four paintings). In each, a high horizon sits between a fifth and a third down, often with a thin dark band of water. The land is rounded lobes stacked on top of one another, and each near lobe overlaps the one behind it with a clear dark outline on its top edge. Trees on the far crests are tiny; trees in front are large. Measured, there is no fading at all: across the land, contrast and colour are about the same from back to front. Depth comes from overlap and size alone. The eye zigzags upward, side to side, through the gaps between lobes. In the tall painting with the yellow tree, a pale river path winds from the bottom to the back, and a row of trees at the bottom right halves in size from one end to the other.

Samuel Palmer, Study of Waves. The horizon is a pale haze about a fifth down. The sea is horizontal bands of waves. Near bands are tall and broken, with large white highlights; far bands are thin lines. Measured, contrast and fine detail are highest in the middle of the sea and fall off both toward the horizon and toward the very bottom.

Trotters racing on the wet beach (file named Sulky-Horse). The camera is low, and the horizon is lost in haze about two-fifths down. The front horses are big, dark and sharp; those behind are smaller and paler. Each horse stands on its own reflection. The foreground is empty wet sand. Measured, fine detail is concentrated in the band of horses and almost absent in the foreground and sky. (The camera's soft focus is not a tool here.)

Triple Dead Heat (the photo finish). Three horses are stacked one above the other at almost the same size. The higher a horse sits in the picture, the farther away it reads, even without shrinking. The striped track and the repeated shadows lie flat. The depth is shallow; it shows that height in the picture alone says distance.

Part two: what they share

1. The ground carries the depth, so the horizon sits high. In most of the deep pictures the horizon sits between one-fifteenth and one-third down (Kiefer's field and furrows, Boyce, Palmer, Zehnder). That leaves most of the picture as ground for things to recede across. A middle horizon gives a calm sweep (Turner, the Dalí print). A low horizon leaves the sky flat (Kiefer's bridge).

2. Repetition makes shrinking readable. Rows, posts, arches, waves, stones, horses, trees: the same thing repeated at smaller and smaller sizes is the strongest depth tool in the set. A thing standing on the ground is drawn at a size in proportion to how far its base sits below the horizon line, so halfway between the horizon and the bottom edge means half size. Equal rows crowd faster than things shrink: their gaps close up quickly toward the horizon.

3. Lines run to one point. Furrows, stubble rows, rivers, streets, wires and ruled lines lead to one point on the horizon, usually a little off centre.

4. Far is finer, plainer and paler, and still sharp. Fine detail falls most reliably: in every picture that fades, the band at the horizon carries between a third and two-thirds of the fine detail of the richest near band, because the marks themselves get smaller and closer together. Colour strength falls to about a third in Goodwin and Boyce. Contrast falls to under half in Turner and Palmer. The far colour leans toward the colour of the light (blue-grey mist, mauve haze, gold glow).

5. Far is light, near is dark. The brightest place is usually at or just above the far point: Turner's sky, the forest's gold opening, the red room's glow, the pale mountain behind the walkers. Near masses are dark.

6. Dark wings frame the sides. Near forms stand at the left and right edges like stage wings (trees, banks, hulls, shells), leaving a lighter opening in the middle distance.

7. Overlap with a clear near edge. The near thing cuts across the far thing, and its edge is crisp and often outlined dark (Zehnder's lobes, Kiefer's rock and tub, the pines over the valley).

8. The richest band is not the bottom edge. The busiest, most contrasted band sits between about halfway and three-quarters down. The bottom strip is calmer (empty sand, flat wash, still water, the clearing), so the eye can step in.

9. The eye enters low and is led in. It enters at the bottom centre or a bottom corner, then travels along a river, furrow, path or diagonal slope to the far point. In Zehnder's pictures it zigzags upward through gaps.

10. One small thing of known size sets the scale. A tiny figure on a road, small workers, a tree on a far crest, the figure seen from behind on the centre line.

11. Reflections double the distance. The puddle under the walking figure, the river under Turner's mast, the wet sand under the horses.

12. Depth without fading. Zehnder, Akunyili Crosby, the Dalí print and the photo finish make depth with no haze at all, through overlap, size, the order of layers, or height in the picture. Those tools are all fully available for the Marks.

Part three: the depth recipe

For each new-territory picture. Use only Reach's Marks drawings and Reach's own photographs.

1. Set the horizon high, between a fifth and a third down, for a deep field. Everything below it is ground that recedes, and everything above it is the light.
2. Make the depth from one Marks motif repeated at falling sizes. A mark standing on the ground is drawn at a size in proportion to how far its base sits below the horizon: full size at the bottom edge, half size halfway up the ground, almost nothing at the horizon. The largest is never bigger than the drawing's own scan. Marks are only ever reduced, never enlarged.
3. Lay the ground in rows or bands that crowd toward the horizon, each band shallower than the one in front of it. Rows of marks or pieces run toward one point on the horizon, a little off centre.
4. Fade with distance, and keep everything sharp. At the horizon, give about half the fine detail of the richest near band, and lower its contrast and colour strength to between a third and a half, leaning toward the colour of the light. Do it by paling and by using smaller, finer marks, never by blurring.
5. Keep the far light and the near dark. The near marks carry the deepest ink, so the drawing still leads from across a room.
6. Frame the sides with dark wings of dense, near Marks, leaving a lighter opening at the far point.
7. Let near things overlap far things, with a crisp, dark near edge.
8. Put the richest band between halfway and three-quarters down, and keep the bottom strip calmer so the eye can enter.
9. Lead the eye in from the bottom along one path, furrow or diagonal to the far point.
10. Add one small thing of known size near the far point (a single small mark, or a small cut piece of Reach's photograph) so the eye can measure the distance. Nothing may arrange itself into a face.
11. Photographs are never resized. They keep their own pixels and are only cut and turned. To make a photographic surface recede, choose finer-grained photographs for the far bands and coarser ones for the near bands, and cut narrower pieces toward the horizon.
12. Reflections are allowed. A piece mirrored and laid below its original is only turned over, so no pixel is invented.

Depth here never uses: blur or soft focus, enlarged photographs or drawings, any pixel from the reference pictures, or a comparison with what known work looks like. This is new territory.
