Flowing Pieces

How a new way of making pictures came about, told plainly for anyone

October 18, 2026

What this is

This is the story of one day of work. It began with a wish to print some pen drawings very large, and it ended with a way of making pictures that, as far as we know, has not been done before. It is written so that anyone can follow it. No training in art or computers is needed. Where a technical word cannot be avoided, it is explained the first time it appears.

The drawings

For many years the artist has filled sheets of paper with pen marks that look like handwriting but are not words. They loop, dash, cross and pool. Some sheets are dense as thickets; some are sparse, with the marks drifting like seeds across the page. He calls them the Marks. There are about eight hundred of them, and each one took time to think out. These are not doodles. Each is a concept, and every one carries a title.

Three groups of them, 429 drawings in all, were scanned at enormous size, about 28,000 pixels across. A pixel is one of the tiny dots a digital picture is made of; the more of them a picture has, the larger it can be printed before it goes soft. At 28,000 across, a drawing can be printed five feet wide and every line stays perfectly sharp. You can see where the pen pressed hard and the ink pooled, and where it ran dry and skipped.

The drawings are black on white, and they are strong. From across a room, a wall of them reads at once. But the artist knew something about them that an honest maker knows about his own work: they are graphic and simple. A broad audience, the kind that stands in line for Van Gogh, loves strength, but it also loves detail: texture, colour, things interacting, something to discover when you walk up close, and, in Van Gogh's case, an unmistakable sense of hope in every picture. The artist wanted his posters to give all of that.

He had a second body of work to draw on. For decades he has arranged found things, rope, cloth, gloves, boards, leaves, broken plastic, soil, in deliberate placings, and photographed them in strong light. There are thousands of these photographs. Each one is at most about 6,000 pixels across, roughly a fifth the size of the drawing scans. That number will matter.

What he wanted, and what he refused

The wish, stated at the start of the day: large posters of the Marks, about four by five feet, to sell. Inside and along each black mark there should be texture and movement, as if the hand that drew the line had also set the texture moving. The space around the marks should be alive too, so that the whole reads as one intricate image rather than a pattern laid on top of a picture. The textures must come from his own photographs. His phrase for the look he wanted: lucid, sharp, textural; photographic on top of photographic; close to realism.

And the refusals, which did most of the steering. No watercolour effects, no painted textures, no added grain, no warping: all of those say "a known craft," and the point was something absolutely new. The marks must never read as flat cutouts, a shape from one picture stuck onto another like a sticker. Nothing may be generated by a machine: every pixel on the poster must be his drawing or his photograph. The drawings are never redrawn, reshaped or improved. Where the pen pooled, keep the pool. And because 429 posters cannot be made by hand, everything must be done by a script, a set of written instructions a computer follows, so that once one poster is judged perfect, the computer can make all the rest overnight while the artist sleeps.

So the day became a search for a way of making one picture, with the artist judging each try on screen and the method changing after every verdict.

The first picture: a window

The first idea answered the cutout problem directly. If a mark cut from one photograph sits on a second photograph, the eye finds the seam at once. But if one photograph runs under the whole drawing, edge to edge, and the mark only changes how that photograph is seen, sharp and full-coloured inside the line, soft and pale outside it, then the mark is a window onto the same world, not a sticker from another one. One world, seen two ways.

The first drawing chosen was called No Name Terre, a dense island of marks in open space, so that both the inside and the outside treatment could be judged at once. Under it went a photograph of grey cloth and a knot of red thread lying on a sheet of lit plastic. The first poster was made at 60 by 43 inches. Up close, inside every mark, there was real grit and real crumpled plastic.

It needed one correction at once. A pale rim had formed around every mark, and a rim is exactly what a sticker has. The fix was to let the photograph come back gently into focus near the marks, as if the lines were pulling the world in toward them, and to widen that zone until the rim was gone.

A second drawing followed, Drink The Ocean Before You Burn Up, a sheet filled edge to edge with looping marks, over a blue-lit photograph of gloves, rope and a red cloth on wet plastic. The title is about water, and that photograph has the most watery light of all. This drawing carried the rest of the day's tests.

The measure of a pixel

Then the artist raised the fact that changed the method. His photographs are 6,000 pixels across; the drawings are 28,000. A 6,000-pixel photograph stretched to five feet goes soft, because each of its dots is being asked to cover an area it cannot describe. His ruling: any photographic material on the poster must be assembled, scrap by scrap, until it accumulates to the size of the line art, every scrap used at its own true size and never enlarged. That way everything on the print is crisp, and it looks like artwork done by hand, not digital art struggling to stay sharp.

So the single photograph was replaced by thirty scraps, cut at random places from forty photographs of the same set, each used exactly as the camera took it, gathered until they covered the whole drawing. Where one scrap meets the next, the edge wanders and melts over a short distance, like torn paper rather than tiles, and most of those edges fall in the quiet space between the marks, where they vanish. The poster grew to 18,000 by 12,956 pixels, 300 to the inch, the sharpness of a fine photographic print. At that size you can count the water droplets on the plastic, see the weave of a rope, and pick out single grains of grit.

Light or dark

The artist asked which read better: dark marks on a light ground, or light marks on a dark one. Dark marks on the pale haze looked like an ink drawing on paper with a texture added, and the haze itself was milky, like frosted glass; a familiar thing. Reversed, each mark became a lit window of full colour and the world around it dimmed to dusk. It looked like nothing else, and it suited the photographs, which are mostly dark and lit in pools.

The question of focus

He then asked whether the soft background could be made to interact with the sharp lines, to flow around them, so that one layer was plainly being affected by the other. A way was found. Instead of blurring the background evenly, it was smeared along the direction of the nearest strokes, a direction read from the drawing itself, so the haze streamed with the lines like water combing past reeds. He liked it. And then he said the sentence that became a law for everything after: "It's still giving me the feeling that it's out of focus, and we can't have an out-of-focus print."

He was right. A five-foot print hangs where people walk up to it, and anything soft reads as a mistake, however deliberate. So blur was banished entirely, including the combed blur he had liked. The next version used light instead of focus. Everything stayed sharp; the world around the marks was only dimmed and quieted in colour; the marks were lit, and their light streamed along the strokes. At print size a braided rope and a taped cable ran straight through the lines and the dark around them without a break, razor sharp on both sides.

And he looked at it and said the truest thing of the day: "It still feels like we have an image layer with a cutout over a light version of the same image."

Because it was. However the light fell, it was one photograph shown twice, bright inside the marks and dim around them, and the eye finds the stencil every time.

The turn

He offered two ways forward. First: let the detail inside the lines flow, so that what seems to be the background, when it enters a mark, moves differently, yet stays in focus. Second: make the lines out of a different photograph altogether, married into the background photograph, with the two slightly interacting; one version where the background is affected by the line, another where the line is affected by the background. If either could be made, he said, it would be a first in the world of art.

The answer was the oldest rule in picture making: the mark follows the form. Van Gogh's strokes run along the thing they show, around the cypress and across the wheat. Two thousand years before him, the mosaic makers of Rome laid the tiny stones of their finest floors along the contours of a figure rather than in rows, so that the whole surface flowed with the form; they called it worm-like work. The same rule could be applied to photographs. Fill each mark with small, sharp pieces cut from a photograph. Turn each piece so that the grain inside it, a straw, a stem, a fold of cloth, runs in the direction the hand was moving at that exact spot. The direction is read from the drawing, stroke by stroke. And take the pieces from a different set of photographs than the ground, so the lines are made of a different material from the world they lie in.

For the pieces, the set called Ignore The Fallen Leaves: dry leaves, straw, twigs, gold and rust. For the ground, the blue-grey marine photographs already in place.

The first try used small pieces from all ten leaf photographs mixed together. The cutout feeling vanished at once, which proved the idea, but the lines read as confetti: too many joins, too many colours. The second try made each piece about three times longer along the stroke than across it, and drew neighbouring pieces from the same photograph, so the grain carries on down the line. The lines became ribbons of straw and leaf laid into lit water. Behind them, the rope, the droplets and the dust stayed as sharp as the ribbons. Nothing blurred. Nothing invented.

The artist's words: "I absolutely love this as a possibility." He added that it was not exactly what he had described, and that is how new things tend to arrive. The description points; the material answers with something the description could not have foreseen.

Why this is a new thing

The drawing is not placed over the photograph, and it is not cut out of it. It is built out of it. Two bodies of work made decades apart are married in one picture: the pen's direction, recorded in ink years ago, now tells every fragment of a photographed world which way to lie. The hand's movement is carried by the camera's detail.

Every pixel is the artist's own. A machine followed the rules, cutting, turning and placing, but it generated nothing: every grain was photographed by him and every line was drawn by him. Nothing on the print is soft. And because the drawing holds still while the material inside it can change, the same drawing can be clothed many ways, which also gives a moving version in which the lines stay fixed and what they are made of slowly changes.

In the artist's own terms, the strong pattern is kept, and a larger audience now gets what it loves: from across the room, the drawing, simple and bold; and walking up to it, detail that keeps giving, colour against colour, gold straw in blue water, texture answering texture. And light, which is where the hope lives.

Where it stands

These pictures are not mosaics, and the word does not fit them. A mosaic arranges pieces to depict something. Here the pieces depict nothing; they are the artist's own photographs, and they follow the movement of his own hand. What the work shares with the Roman floors, and with Van Gogh, is only the rule: the mark follows the form. The nearest things in recent art are pictures built out of material, portraits assembled from small painted cells, or from found objects arranged and then photographed. Those works arrange material to copy an image. This one arranges material to follow a line, and both the line and the material are the artist's own.

It also takes a position. In a year when pictures are generated by machines in their millions, this is a method in which a machine is used as a tool and makes nothing. It cuts, turns and places by rules, and every piece it places was photographed by the artist, inside a line the artist drew. In an age of invented images, everything here was seen by someone first.

Where it goes next

Twelve versions of the same drawing, each with its own name, so that the best can be chosen and all twelve can become a changing picture that loops. Among them: longer pieces with fewer joins; a fine weave of narrow pieces; one photograph carried much further along each line; pieces that take on the colour of the ground behind them, so the line is affected by the background; a ground laid in large pieces that turn to follow the strokes, so the background is affected by the line; a darker night with brighter gold; the sources reversed; other photograph sets, pinks and reds, rust and sand; the grain turned across the stroke like ribs; and two materials alternating along each line.

Then the chosen versions run at full size, 60 inches on the long side at 300 pixels to the inch, on cotton art paper with a soft photographic sheen. Then the script runs overnight over all 429 drawings. And after the still is perfect, the moving version: the lines sharp and still, the world around them slowly alive, made from the artist's own film of kites in the wind.

The rules that held all day

Every pixel is the artist's drawing or the artist's photograph. Nothing is generated. The drawings are never redrawn. Every photographic piece is used at its own size, never enlarged. Nothing on the print is ever out of focus. The marks must never read as cutouts. The artist judges one picture on screen until it is perfect, and only then does the script make the rest. The scripts were written by an artificial intelligence working at the artist's direction; it made no imagery of its own, and every verdict that moved the work was his.
