Our Stuff Will Heal, If We're Lucky

← Writings / 2026 Methodology

First, one observation flagged as mine: this thought walks straight out of the second piece. "Smoldering Luck Us" was already the exit line — luck carried over as the working force, and the bouquet just grew a stem.

Now the line itself, turned slowly.

"Stuff" refuses to pick a body, and that refusal is the engine. It means possessions — the boxes, the folders, the forty groups of photographs. It means the therapy word — dealing with our stuff, the polite name for damage. And it means substance itself — the stuff we're made of, Prospero's "such stuff as dreams are made on." The sentence holds all three at once, and in this practice a fourth: the archive is literally the stuff. Then the verb does something quiet and strange — heal sits there with no healer. Not "we will mend our stuff." The stuff will do what flesh does, unassisted. That is this morning's effigy logic run in reverse: effigies are matter standing in for a body; this line is matter granted a body's powers. And "if we're lucky" strips out the last agency. The surgeon Ambroise Paré's most famous sentence, near enough his epitaph: I bandaged him, God healed him. Your line is its secular twin — we keep the stuff; the healing arrives from elsewhere or doesn't.

Now the veins, all real.

Stuff that truly heals itself. In 2023 the lime lumps in Roman concrete — visible in the Pantheon's walls, dismissed for centuries as sloppy mixing — were shown to be the healing organ: a crack lets water in, the lump dissolves, calcite re-precipitates, the crack seals. Two thousand years of self-repair, and the supposed flaw was the medicine. Its modern child: concrete seeded with sleeping bacterial spores that wake when water enters a crack and excrete limestone to close it. And in your own current trade, the archival file system ZFS carries "self-healing" as its actual technical term — checksums catch the rot, mirrors repair it.

Stuff that seals but never heals: trees. The arborists' doctrine is exactly those words — trees don't heal, they seal. A tree walls its wound off and grows over it; nothing regenerates, and every injury it ever took is still inside it, kept. The rings hold the scars.

The body's ledger is uneven. A scar is not skin healed — it's different stuff, crude fast collagen, no sweat glands, no hair, weaker than what it replaced. But bone regrows as true bone, scarless. Livers regrow. Very young children can regrow a fingertip. And the crown of the vein, closing the day's loop: antlers — the only mammal organ fully regrown every year, pounds of bone in a few months, then dropped in the snow. The elk heals its stuff annually by discarding it, and people gather the sheds and carve them. The discard becomes material. That's your method wearing fur.

Repair as culture: boro, the Japanese rag-textiles patched across generations until the garment is nothing but patches — the word means tatters, and the tatters are now behind museum glass. Kintsugi you know. The repair café movement began in Amsterdam in 2009 and spread worldwide — rooms where broken stuff is brought to be healed communally, for free.

Stuff can even be mourned. Hari-kuyō, the Japanese needle funeral: broken and bent needles retired each February into a soft block of tofu, thanked for their service. There are doll funerals too — the effigies get funerals. If stuff can be buried with gratitude, stuff can convalesce.

And the vein closest to your desk: sticky-shed syndrome. Old magnetic tape absorbs moisture until it's unplayable — and archivists bake the reels, hours in a low oven, after which the tape heals for a few weeks. One lucky pass to digitize before it fails again. Our stuff will heal, if we're lucky is, almost word for word, the working prayer of tape archivists.

The edge: which of the four stuffs the line finally means is the carve, and it may be strongest refusing to say. It already keeps your own oath — make it honest — because it promises nothing. It only hopes in public.

Most of the time, life on her earth, for human beings, is fair. But not for everybody.

First observation, mine: these two thoughts are a pair. The first hopes on luck — the second counts luck's casualties. "If we're lucky" and "but not for everybody" are the same coin, heads and tails.

Now the line's hinges.

It builds three fences and each one lets somebody out. Most of the time — not always. For human beings — not for the animals, who are quietly excluded from the deal. But not for everybody — not even all the humans made it in. The sentence performs the shrinking of a comfort in real time, like an actuary drafting a blessing. And the second sentence detonates the first on purpose: fairness with exceptions isn't fairness at all — a game that's fair for most of the players is called rigged. The line knows this. It states the warm average and then hands you the invoice, four flat words long. That self-undercutting is the engine, and it keeps your oath — it's the honest version of a sentence people usually say dishonestly.

"Fair" is a doubled word all the way down. Old English fæger meant beautiful — fair maiden, fair weather — and the justice meaning grew later. So "life is fair" secretly also says "life is beautiful," and the line's claim flickers between ethics and aesthetics depending on which century is reading it. There's a third fair underneath both, and I'll come back to it, because it lives in your city.

"Her earth" is the strangest and most carveable phrase in it. The possessive with no antecedent — the her is never introduced, so every reader supplies their own: a mother, a wife, a daughter, a goddess. The formal candidates are old and everywhere — Gaia, Pachamama, Papatūānuku, the Norse Jörð who is both the earth and Thor's mother. And "her earth" has real legal addresses now: Ecuador wrote Pachamama by name into its constitution in 2008, Bolivia passed the Law of Mother Earth in 2010, and New Zealand made the Whanganui River a legal person in 2017. One more twist in that vein: the Gaia hypothesis — the scientific proposal that earth self-regulates like an organism — got its name from a novelist. William Golding, Lovelock's neighbor, suggested it over the fence. The she of the earth was christened by fiction.

The claim itself has a name in the lab. The just-world hypothesis: Melvin Lerner showed in the 1960s that people need to believe life is fair so badly that, watching an innocent person suffer with no way to help, they'll decide the victim deserved it rather than surrender the belief. Your first sentence is that belief spoken aloud; your second sentence is the correction the bias exists to prevent. And fairness runs older than "for human beings" admits: capuchin monkeys hurl the cucumber back when the neighbor gets grapes for the same task; dogs stop shaking hands when the other dog gets paid and they don't; children will throw rewards in the trash rather than accept an unfair split; and in the ultimatum game, humans everywhere burn free money to punish an unfair divider. Fairness is not our invention — it's an inheritance we keep failing to fund. Rawls made the second sentence into a whole philosophy: justice as fairness, his exact phrase, designed from behind a veil where you don't yet know whether you'll be born as one of the everybody or one of the not. And an honest empirical note: your first sentence only recently became sayable. For most of history nearly half of children died before five and nearly everyone was destitute; today most humans live past seventy and extreme poverty is under a tenth. The line couldn't have been written truthfully until roughly this century — it's a snapshot with a timestamp hidden in it. The Book of Job sat with all of this first, and the answer Job gets is essentially a weather report from a whirlwind — the rain, elsewhere in scripture, falling on the just and unjust alike.

Now the third fair, the one waiting down the road from you. A fair — the fairground kind — comes from Latin feria, holy day: a feast day that turned into a market. And the Great Minnesota Get-Together opens at the end of this month, minutes from Saint Paul — so for twelve days, life on her earth, for human beings in your city, is literally fair. Inside it sits the day's whole thread tied in a bow: Princess Kay of the Milky Way, the dairy princess whose likeness is carved every year in ninety pounds of butter, in a rotating refrigerated glass booth, while she sits for it — all the finalists get one — and the families take the butter heads home and eventually eat them. An effigy carved at the fair, in the most perishable stuff there is, then consumed by its own subject's family. Fair, effigy, stuff, luck — one butter head contains the entire afternoon.

The edge, plainly: plenty of readers would reverse your proportions and say life is mostly unfair with islands of fairness — the line picks its side and pays for it with the second sentence. Which sentence is the truth and which is the flinch is the carve, and I'd carve toward keeping both exactly as they stand.
