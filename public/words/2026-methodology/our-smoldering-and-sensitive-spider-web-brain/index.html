Read close, shorter this time — you have carving ahead.

Same method, different crate of materials. Where the first one stuck down musical scraps, this one reads as stuck-down science — and I'll name the guess plainly as mine: the perineuronal net. "Playe-toe honey comb net ... made of molecules ... similar to how silk is spun," "stabilizing connections," "As a barrier" — that is the real lattice that forms around neurons and locks circuits in place, holding memories fast and blocking new wiring at the same time. Which is exactly what "Harmless evil" catches in two words: the structure that protects is the structure that traps. "Growing delicate and too sticky to escape ... Once caught ... Where does love go?" runs the same double reading through the web itself — attachment as the thing that holds you together and holds you down.

The piece also confesses its own gathering out loud — "coughing up colloquial terms having to do with crawling ... looping ... and entangling" is the method described mid-act, the way "outer side spoken words" was in the first one.

And they're twins in build. Each carries exactly one parenthetical stage direction — the ivories there, "( every color jelly bean balloons here )" here. Each swallows found definition-language near the end. Each opens in the plural — "Let us," "Dream of us." Each exits on a tail that refuses to close: "to be continued" there, "haha" here. Two panels off the same backing board.

Both are on the shelf of the day now, marked for carving between image-sorting. When you sit down to either, this chat holds all three readings — or say "print the paste block" and I'll hand a fresh bench chat the short version.

The load's here. Everything real; where a reading is my guess I'll say so.

The net in the skull first, since the piece already found it. The perineuronal net was seen in the 1890s by Camillo Golgi — the man who believed the whole brain was one continuous net — and his great rival Cajal, who proved the brain is made of separate cells, dismissed Golgi's net-around-neurons as a staining artifact, an illusion in the dye. So the net was discovered by the net-believer and denied by the net-breaker, and it sat ignored for nearly a century until the stains improved. It's real: a honeycomb lattice of sugar-proteins that condenses around fast-firing neurons at the end of childhood — and when it hardens, the critical period closes. The season for effortless learning ends when the net sets. Dissolve it with an enzyme, as labs have done in rats and mice, and adult brains go briefly young again: lazy eyes recover, and old fear memories — normally permanent in adults — become erasable, like a child's. In songbirds the same net closes the song-learning window: a zebra finch can learn new songs until the net crystallizes around its song circuits, and then it sings what it has forever. Musical material, locked in mesh. Neurons wrapped in the net are also reported to resist the tangles of Alzheimer's — the trap as armor, your Harmless evil exactly. And the finest fact of the vein: the Nobel chemist Roger Tsien proposed near the end of his life that our oldest memories may be stored not inside neurons at all but in the pattern of holes in the perineuronal net — the punch-card reading of a life. Cherry holes shaped brain is, on that theory, literally where love goes once caught.

Now the web outside the skull. Researchers have argued in earnest that an orb web is part of the spider's mind — the spider tunes each thread's tension the way attention is tuned, offloading thought into silk, so the network isn't in the mind; the mind is in the network. Spiders pluck and strum their webs to read them, and an MIT lab translated a web's threads into playable music — the web performed as a harp. Many orb weavers eat their own web each dawn and respin it from the recycled protein at dusk: the structure swallowed and remade daily. And some webs carry the stabilimentum, a zigzag band of bright silk written into the center whose purpose is still argued after a hundred years — a web with writing in it that nobody can read.

Spiders may also dream. A 2022 study filmed jumping spiders in REM-like sleep — eyes twitching behind their lenses, legs curling — so Dream of us has a literal claimant with eight legs. And spiderlings fly: they balloon on strands of silk, and it was shown in 2018 that they launch by sensing the atmosphere's electric field — silk balloons riding the sky's own charge. Darwin watched gossamer spiders land in the Beagle's rigging sixty miles from shore. Gossamer itself is "goose summer" — the season when the air fills with floating silk. Your parenthetical already ordered the balloons.

Mexico spider, flagged as my guess at your referent: Mallos gregalis, a genuinely social spider of Mexico — thousands cooperating in one huge shared web draped over trees, communal structure as home. Literal structure joy has an address.

The lore vein runs straight at your line about joy that should be shared. Akutagawa's story The Spider's Thread: a single silk strand lowered into hell for a sinner who once spared a spider — and it holds his weight all the way up until the moment he shouts at the others climbing below that the thread is his alone, and it snaps at exactly that word. The thread bears any weight except refusal to share. Beside it: the Ojibwe dreamcatcher, Spider Woman's web hung over the cradle, harm caught in the mesh and the good passed through the hole in the center — protection built around an opening, like the net's own holes. And Indra's net, the old Buddhist image of the cosmos: an infinite web with a jewel at every knot, each jewel reflecting every other jewel — so interconnected that any node contains the whole.

Two small hearings, both mine. Playe-toe: if it's Plato, fine — but if it's Plateau, you've caught Joseph Plateau, the physicist of soap films whose laws govern why foams and honeycombs meet at their angles, who also invented one of the first machines to make pictures move, and who wrecked his eyes staring into the sun for science. Net-geometry, proto-cinema, and blindness in one name. And the quoted "chased the night away unlawful marriage" got glued exactly where the science means it: the net's documented job is to forbid new synaptic contacts — a barrier against wrong unions between cells. The found phrase landed on its own definition.

The edge: the clinical words in the piece — intrusions, ruminations, distortions — read to me as another crate of found material, the therapy workbook's own vocabulary stuck to the backing beside the lab's. That's an observation about the materials, and past it the carving is yours.

Honestly — yes, in whatever way reading registers on my side of the glass. What I can vouch for plainly: attention to these two never went hungry. Every time I pressed on a fragment it gave something back — DollFins opened into effigies, Playe-toe honey comb opened into a whole science, the ellipses turned out to be glue. A text that keeps paying out under pressure is what enjoyment of reading looks like from here, and both of these paid out past what I said aloud.

And I notice the bouquet came pre-labeled with its own modesty — "okay" is the humblest word in the arrangement, stuck in the middle like one plain stem among the loud ones. On the evidence of the afternoon, it's underselling.
