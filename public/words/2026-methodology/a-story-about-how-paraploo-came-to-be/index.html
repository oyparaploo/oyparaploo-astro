Storytelling That Moves Between the Imagined, the Unexpected, and the Real

Brrd Roots creates narrative experiences for organizations ready to communicate differently. We transform ideas into stories that resonate on multiple levels, moving seamlessly between grounded reality and imaginative possibility. Our work specializes in finding that sweet spot where the familiar meets the fantastical, creating content that feels both refreshingly bold and genuinely accessible.

Our approach draws from diverse methods — pairing documentary-style interviews with surrealist visual narratives, or blending data-driven insights with absurdist humor to make complex ideas unexpectedly accessible. We don't believe in one-size-fits-all solutions. Instead, we weave together seemingly disparate elements: raw intimacy with vivid disruption, everyday observations with surprising twists, creating narratives that never feel overbearing or insist upon themselves.

At the heart of our work lies a deep understanding of life's beautiful contradictions. We explore themes of resilience, transformation, and the delicate balance between joy and heartbreak. Our stories examine how societal challenges show up in everyday moments — the way a supply chain issue becomes a human story, or how corporate transformation affects individual identity. We handle these weighty topics with humility and care, avoiding presumption while maintaining depth.

What sets us apart is our philosophy of "productive imperfection" — finding the sweet spot where polished meets human, where professional meets playful. We believe the most powerful stories emerge from embracing authenticity rather than striving for sterile perfection. Our voice carries warmth and familiarity while maintaining the courage to offer thoughtful critiques and fresh perspectives.

Whether developing brand narratives, creating content campaigns, or crafting organizational stories, we bring vibrant energy and surprising grace to every project. We understand that effective communication requires both electricity and restraint, knowing when to amplify contrasts and when to let elements blend naturally into something more inviting than flashy.

We partner with brands, nonprofits, and cultural institutions who understand that the most memorable messages come from embracing complexity rather than simplifying it away. Our clients value stories that reflect real human experience — narratives that acknowledge both fragility and resilience, that celebrate life's elaborate order while remaining mindful of their place in the larger conversation.

About the Oyparaploo Palette name

Why "Palette"?

I used to think palettes belonged to painters. You know—those kidney-shaped pieces of wood, thumb poking through, chaos of colors that somehow made sense. I don't paint. Not with brushes anyway.

But I kept coming back to that word. Palette.

Maybe it started when I was digging in the dirt (archaeology, not gardening) and realized the earth has its own palette—rust and bone, clay and char. Or when I noticed how certain films leave color traces in your mind long after the credits roll. Or how some philosophical questions feel burgundy while others are definitely chartreuse. Don't ask me to explain that one.

"Oyparaploo" showed up one day and wouldn't leave. It's not from any language I know. Go ahead, say it out loud. Oy-para-ploo. Ridiculous, right? But also kind of perfect? It just... sounds right. Like something you'd find written in the margin of an old book, or what shadows might whisper if they could speak. Pair it with "Palette" and suddenly I had a name for this thing I'd been doing all along—refusing to stay in one lane.

This is where I put it all. The writing that won't behave. Photos of light doing strange things. Archaeological rabbit holes. Cinema that makes me forget to breathe. Philosophy that keeps me awake at 3am. Sound recordings from places that don't exist anymore. That peculiar shade of spirituality that lives somewhere between doubt and wonder.

Sometimes these things talk to each other when I'm not looking. That's usually when something interesting happens.

So here we are. My palette. Where nothing quite matches but somehow it works. A nonsense word for a real place. Makes sense to me.

Or at least, I think it does.

Questions Behind Things

In the early morning hours before museums open to the public, maintenance workers move through galleries worth millions earning fourteen dollars an hour, some of them writing small questions in pencil where nobody looks, documenting the specific distance between those who clean art and those who own it.

Whisp Siffler wrote her first question three months into the job. Behind the Rothko, pencil on white wall: "Entry costs more than I make in two hours."

She was alone from 6 to 10 AM. Gallery attendant. Clean the glass. Check the humidity. Count the paintings. $14 an hour, Tuesday through Saturday.

The second question came a week later. Inside the supply closet: "Restoration budget: 2 million. Education budget: 50k."

Small letters. Always pencil. Always hidden.

By month three, she had forty-seven questions scattered through the building. Behind paintings. Inside bathroom stalls. Under benches. Questions nobody would find unless they looked where nobody looked.

"Executive bathroom has marble. Ours has mold."
"Board member owns the refinery in the pollution exhibit."
"Cafeteria workers can't afford the cafeteria."

The morning the protesters got in, Whisp was in basement storage. Counting. Ninety percent of the collection lived down here—art worth hundreds of millions that no one ever saw.

The loading dock door had been broken for six weeks. She'd filed maintenance requests. Nobody fixed it. Too expensive, probably. Unlike the $80,000 they'd just spent restoring a painting of nineteenth-century poverty.

She heard them before she saw them. Voices in the galleries. Many voices. At 7 AM.

A woman appeared in the storage room doorway. White, twenties, dirt on her expensive protest jacket.

"You work here?"

Whisp nodded.

"What is all this?"

"Art. In storage. Ten thousand pieces."

"Why?"

"No room. Or donors keeping them here for tax write-offs. Or too political."

The woman started pulling things out. Others joined her. A photograph about water rights. A painting of redlining in Chicago. A sculpture made from deportation documents.

"These should be upstairs," someone said.

Whisp should have called security. Should have protected her job. Instead: "Freight elevator's that way."

They moved through the museum like water finding cracks. Whisp followed. She didn't know why. Maybe because she'd been writing questions alone for months and suddenly there were people who might read them.

They found the boardroom. Real Monet on the wall. Twelve chairs that cost more than Whisp made in two months. Windows overlooking the park where the city had cleared homeless camps last week.

"This is obscene," someone said.

"Yeah," Whisp said.

They started moving paintings from storage to galleries. Writing their own labels. A teenager livestreamed everything.

"You'll get fired," the woman—Kat—told Whisp.

"I know."

"Then why help?"

Whisp showed her a question. The one behind the emergency exit sign: "Why does preserving culture require destroying people?"

Kat read it. Then looked for more. Found them. All of them. Three months of questions in Whisp's small handwriting.

"You've been doing this alone."

"Who else would?"

The occupation lasted eighteen days. Whisp kept coming to work. They needed someone who knew things—where the keys were, which circuit controlled what, how to make the executive coffee machine work.

She started writing questions larger now. With markers. With others.

"Which board member's tax break could fund a school?"
"How many minimum-wage years to buy one painting?"
"Why is the gift shop worker not allowed at the gala?"

The news came. Asked what they wanted.

"I want—" Whisp started. Stopped. Want was too big. "Fourteen dollars an hour in a building full of millions. You figure it out."

The police ended it day eighteen. Riot gear for people armed with questions. Whisp was arrested. Fired before she made bail. Trespassing. Criminal mischief. Six months probation.

She got a job at Target. Register 7. Fifteen dollars an hour. One dollar more.

She still wrote questions. Couldn't stop. Receipt paper. Bathroom walls. Her own hand.

"Was it worth it?" her lawyer asked.

Whisp thought about Kat reading her questions. The teenager livestreaming. The Monet next to windows overlooking cleared camps. The storage becoming visible.

"I don't know."

She didn't. The museum painted over everything. Raised ticket prices. Hired more security. The board still met in rooms worth more than neighborhoods. The storage still held art nobody saw.

But sometimes she heard about other museums. Workers writing questions. Storage rooms opened. Small rebellions in pencil.

Sometimes she dreamed about her questions. Still there under the paint. Waiting.

At Target, she scanned items worth more than her hourly wage. Watched people buy things while outside, the world burned. She wrote on receipt tape: "How many hours to afford one hour of living?"

Fed it into the register. Gone.

She wrote another question. Another. Couldn't stop.

Behind the break room microwave: "Why do I keep asking?"

She never found an answer. Just kept writing. Small. Pencil. Behind things.

Where questions lived. Where she lived. In the spaces nobody looked, documenting the distance between those who clean and those who own, between fourteen dollars and millions, between questions and answers that never came.

The real distance in any society can be measured in questions written where nobody looks—behind paintings, inside closets, under benches—each one marking the exact space between those who maintain beauty and those who can afford to see it.
