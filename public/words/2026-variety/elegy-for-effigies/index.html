Short Piece · 9 minutes

Elegy for Effigies

Effigies — plural of effigy: a fashioned likeness of a person, made so that what cannot be done to the person may be done to the image.

That is the timeless core, and it holds across every culture that has used the word or the practice. The image stands in for the body and receives what the world wants to give it — in both directions. Honor: the carved knight lying on his own tomb, the sovereign's head struck on a coin. Hatred: the stuffed figure of a tyrant hanged or burned in the square when the tyrant himself is out of reach — Guy Fawkes in England, Ravana burning at Dussehra across India, Judas set alight at Easter in Greece and Latin America. Same word, same logic: veneration or violence, transferred to the likeness.

The root carries the making. Latin effigies, from effingere — to shape out, to fashion — the same fingere that gives English figure and fiction. An effigy is not a picture of a person; it is a person shaped in substitute material.

The word itself has a doubled body. Latin effigiēs was singular; English borrowed it whole, and for two centuries "effigies" was a singular noun of four syllables — Shakespeare has a man's likeness witnessed "in his effigies" in As You Like It. "Effigy" was carved off it later. So the plural you asked about is the older, truer form, and the singular is the fragment.

Rome kept ancestor effigies as a legal right. The imagines — wax masks of the family dead — hung in the atrium, and at funerals actors wore them and walked, so the whole lineage attended each new death in person. The right to keep these masks marked nobility. An effigy not for burning but for company.

The English kings were buried twice. The corpse went in the coffin; a dressed wooden or wax effigy rode on top of it through the streets, because the realm needed a body that did not decay. Westminster Abbey still holds them. Beneath some medieval tombs runs the harder version: the transi tomb, the effigy doubled — the dressed sleeper above, and below, carved with equal care, the same body as a cadaver.

Law used them for real. Executio in effigie — when the condemned escaped, courts hanged the portrait, and the sentence counted as carried out.

The folk vein: the poppet of European magic, wax or cloth, worked by likeness — what the world calls a voodoo doll is mostly this older neighbor projected elsewhere. The scarecrow is a field effigy. Caesar reported the Gauls burning colossal wicker figures. A wax leg left at a shrine is an effigy of the afflicted part, offered so the healing lands on the right limb.

And they are not always human-shaped or hand-sized. In Wisconsin and Iowa, earth itself was raised into bears and birds a thousand years ago — effigy mounds, likenesses readable whole only from above, made for a viewer who is not standing on the ground.

The living uses: the sovereign's head on every coin is called an effigy in the trade. Ravana burns each autumn across India, Judas at Easter, the Man in the Nevada desert annually — the burning that is festival rather than hatred.

And the root under all of it: effingere, to shape out of — the same fingere that gives fiction, figment, feign. An effigy is fiction's blood relative, worked in matter.

The oldest effigies on earth wear the person inside them. At Jericho, nine thousand years ago, people took the skulls of their dead, modeled faces back onto them in plaster, set shells in for eyes, and kept them under the floors of houses. The same logic reappears half a world later in Vanuatu: the rambaramp of south Malakula, where a dead man's actual skull is overmodeled with paste onto a tree-fern body painted with his own face designs — an effigy that contains its subject. And in the lower Congo, the Bwende made the niombo: the smoked body of a chief wrapped in hundreds of layers of cloth into a giant figure, taller than a man, painted face, one arm raised, the corpse riding inside its own colossal likeness to the grave.

The Inca made effigies of themselves while alive. The chronicles record that each ruler commissioned a huauque — a "brother" statue, sometimes holding his hair and nail clippings — which could stand in for him at ceremonies and even accompany armies. The Spanish hunted them down as rivals to the king's person, which they were.

Some effigies exist in order to rot. The Zuni carve the Ahayu:da, twin war gods, and set them at open shrines precisely so weather will take them; when museums acquired stolen ones and conserved them, the Zuni fought for decades to get them back and return them to decay — the one case where restoration is the sacrilege. In New Ireland, malagan figures are labored over for months, displayed once at the mortuary feast, and then deliberately abandoned.

Some outlived their own meaning. Every May in Rome, the Vestals threw twenty-seven rush puppets called the Argei off the bridge into the Tiber — and Ovid, a Roman writing about Roman rites, admits nobody could tell him why. In West Witton, Yorkshire, they still carry an effigy called Bartle through the village every August, chanting his crimes, and burn him — and no one can say who Bartle was.

The makers sometimes deny the making. Mesopotamian ritual tablets for animating a god's statue require the craftsmen to have their hands symbolically severed with a wooden knife and to swear they did not make it — the effigy must be born, not built. Mencius quotes Confucius cursing the first maker of burial figurines to childlessness, because a likeness convincing enough to serve the dead stands one step from burying the living.

The word "colossal" descends from a small doll. Archaic Greek kolossos meant not a giant but a substitute figure — the Cyrene sacred law prescribes making a male and a female kolossos to house an unquiet ghost, feasting them, then planting them in unworked ground; the colonists' oath was sworn over wax figures melted with the words that oath-breakers should melt likewise. Only later, through Rhodes, did the word swell to mean enormous. And "guy" — the ordinary English word for a person — is the burned effigy of Guy Fawkes, generalized: first the figure, then any strange fellow, then everyone.

Violence kept its ledger in effigies too. Egyptian state magic inscribed enemies' names on clay figures and smashed them by the hundreds. Scottish Highlanders set the corp creadha, a clay body, in a stream so the victim would waste as the water wore it. Renaissance Florence painted condemned men hanging upside down on public walls — Andrea del Castagno did it well enough to be nicknamed Andreino of the Hanged Men. The Inquisition burned absent heretics in effigy wearing the sanbenito, then hung those garments in the parish church with names attached, a shame registry in cloth that outlasted generations.

And some effigies are for the dead's own use. In Tibetan funerary rite, a paper effigy or name-card stands for the deceased through the weeks of bardo teaching — the dead attend their own instruction by proxy — and is burned at the end to release them. In Japan the living still do the mirror rite: paper hitogata rubbed on the body, breathed into, and floated away with one's faults aboard. The Japanese also keep the gentlest scarecrow fact we know: Kuebiko, a god in the Kojiki who cannot walk but knows everything under heaven — the omniscient effigy in the field.

One modern container: Zozobra, fifty feet of Old Man Gloom, burned in Santa Fe every year since 1924, stuffed beforehand with the town's actual divorce papers, mortgages, and written griefs.

And one twist from Greenland to end on: the tupilak was assembled from bones and parts, animated in secret, and sent to kill — but it was never meant to be seen. When Europeans asked to look at one, carvers made representations to show them. Every tupilak in every museum is an effigy of an effigy.

We called the quarry empty one turn early. There is an elk vein, and it runs close to where you're pointing. Everything below is documented; where it thins we'll say so.

Start with the elk that IS an effigy. In the Kalevala, Lemminkäinen must ski down the Hiisi elk to win a bride — and Hiisi, the forest demon, builds the elk: head from a rotten stump, antlers from forked willow, legs of stakes, back of fence rails, sinews of dry grass, eyes of water-lily blooms, hide of fir bark, flesh of rotten wood. Then he animates it and sets it running. Lemminkäinen glimpses it, chases it across Lapland, corners it once, boasts aloud of the hide he'll sleep on — and it tears loose and smashes his skis. A spirit elk assembled from forest refuse and set moving, seen briefly and never held. We'll name what we noticed rather than pretend we didn't: that is a Wonderful Garbage elk.

Then the dreamed elk that demands an effigy as payment. Among the Lakota, men who dreamed of the elk received its medicine — the bull elk's power to draw others from a distance — and the dream obligated public enactment; a dream left unperformed sickened the dreamer. Elk dreamers danced in triangular masks with real branches bound on for antlers, carrying hoops with small mirrors set at the center, the mirror-flash carrying the charm to whoever it touched. Collections hold Dakota mirror boards carved in the outline of an elk, and the courting flute's voice is the elk's bugle. So the closest thing on record to your daydream elk: the elk arrives in the dream, and the dreamer pays for the glimpse by becoming its effigy in public.

The oldest elk effigies known are staffs in graves. On Deer Island in Lake Onega, Karelia, a Mesolithic cemetery more than eight thousand years old holds a few burials with batons topped by carved elk heads, the dead dressed in garments sewn with thousands of elk incisors. And the rock carvings at Onega and at Nämforsen in Sweden show human figures carrying elk-headed poles — the object from the grave, pictured in use. The same rock art gives boats with elk-head prows: the vessel itself made an elk, read by some as the ferry of the dead.

At Pazyryk in the Altai, horses went into the frozen tombs wearing leather masks with tall antlers — costumed as elk for the death road. One animal buried wearing the effigy of another. And across Mongolia stand thousands of deer stones, carved with flying, bird-beaked elk streaming toward the sun — while the frozen Pazyryk dead carry the same flying elk tattooed on their shoulders. One scholarly reading holds the stones stand for tattooed warriors: stone effigies of people whose skin carried elk effigies.

In Siberia the sky is a running elk. The Evenki say the elk Kheglen steals the sun each evening and the hunter pursues forever — the Big Dipper is the chase, renewed nightly. The Khanty version has a six-legged elk cut down to four so men could ever catch it, the hunter's ski track left across the sky as the Milky Way. And the Evenki shaman's drum, elk hide on a wooden rim, is spoken of as his mount — he rides the elk he beats.

Kin for the bison, since you said large game: the Alberta ribstones, boulders carved with spine and ribs into bison effigies, offerings still left on them now; and the Blackfoot iniskim, buffalo-calling stones — ammonite fossils shaped like sleeping bison, the first one found by a starving woman who heard it singing. Effigies found rather than made.

The edge, plainly: no tradition we can vouch for names elk spirits in daydreams. The documented neighbors are the Lakota dream that must be performed, the widespread reading of the fleetingly seen animal — the white elk that shows itself once and must not be shot — as spirit rather than game, and the Huichol deer Kauyumari arriving inside peyote vision. Past that line the territory is yours, which may be exactly where the piece lives.
