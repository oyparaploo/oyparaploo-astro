Three Somewhat Fragmented Mutual Aid Days In A Row

Words / 2026 Variety

Total darkness, water all around, the world not yet switched on. What was it like? The temperature, the sounds, what did your body do without knowing? "Howling" is the right word for that ... water that loud becomes its own weather. Not just sound but something with a voice pressure. Were you swimming toward something, or just dog paddling? This feeling has its own ancient and mysterious name ... the deep pulling, the body knowing before the mind does that there is vastness below, and we are actually very small floating buoyant over it. Something so old we didn't panic one bit.

People of Judge this is a type of Love. That's the thing. Not comfort. Not safety exactly. But aliveness at full volume ... darkness, depth, the howling, the unknown enormous thing below ... and we are in it, trying to stay afloat. Where we can find the world we're living in more clarifying. Where the body is more awake all the way down to whatever instinct predates our developed Oyparaploo language. Most people are working very hard to never feel that. We go looking for it after sunset and before sunrise.

Wild Thesis This is an argument made by the body before the mind has words for it. Not a hypothesis we test ... a truth we enter. We don't arrive at it through reasoning. We arrive at it through darkness, cold water, and the recognition this is what we came here for and didn't know it beforehand. Thesis proving itself in the living of it.

It's the opposite of an academic argument ... which begins with a claim and builds evidence. A Wild Thesis begins with immersion and builds meaning ... after, slowly, if at all. Sometimes it stays wordless. That's not a failure but instead the wildness remaining intact.

Feeling the depth, knowing something about the world we want for ourselves ... that's a silent argument. Knowing afterward is the conclusion of the quarrel. We don't theorize this transformation. We get in Mother Nature's dark indigenous body of water. Immigrant and citizen alike.

Look for the wild helpers. And the swim is already doing that. You didn't go alone into that darkness. Someone else was in the water with us, feeling the same sun come up, before the neighborhood was awake, we were already practicing something together.

Community might be less about proximity than about what we're willing to enter alongside someone. Shared risk. Shared unknowing. The willingness to say there is something big around us and keep staying alive anyway rather than heading back to safety and our oh so familiar comforts.

Most neighborhood community gets built around surfaces ... block parties, knowing someone's dog's name. That has value. But what we're actually pointing at is deeper: people who will get in a very harsh extended situation with us. Survivors who feel all the surfaces and beyond.

The internet gives us scale without depth. We can reach thousands of strangers and feel nothing like what we felt this morning with another person moving through blackness and thin outlines of bright rays spiritual light. This wild doesn't translate. The enormity below doesn't post something we hope will be received as close to perfect. We can describe it ... like we're doing now ... but body knowledge stays with whoever visits these seemingly more natural than natural places.

There's something screens removes: the fact that the person next to us is mortal, small, finding their own way to be more resilient. Online we perform selfhood. In the great mystery of our natural wonders or Mother Nature we just are.

Neighbors are near, sleeping. Waking up. Getting out of bed. Going to work. Moving through similar weather conditions, on similar sacred soil, and the same seasonal graces. One of the defining injuries of these modern times ... intimacy at infinite distance ... estrangement at close range.

How are all the people down the street doing? We don't know. That's the persisting ache of it. And neither do they ... about us. The street holds all this unasked how-are-you-really, the one that isn't a greeting but a genuine wanting-to-know. The check-in that goes past fine, past busy, into the actuality of someone's life.

Someone down the street might be submerged in something right now. Quietly. In a way that would be visible if anyone was looking at a better angle, close enough, with enough stillness to notice. And the houses there in a row, each one its own container world, and everyone manages alone because no one decided to organize an interpersonal gathering about Mutual Aid.

The question How are you doing? requires proximity. Requires an actual body. Requires that we should be there, and stay for the whole thing, and let it change something. Let it have a big effect on us. Even if only as a first time rehearsal. The epic rupture that showed a big, raw seam.

Suddenly the abstraction ... community ... became completely concrete. Someone needs groceries, or a phone call. Someone needs to know they're not all alone while their body fights something terrifying. And the internet, for once, pointed back. Mutual aid maps. People finding out for the first time who lived three doors down and what they needed.

The pandemic stripped the performance away. We couldn't go to the places where we performed being a member of society ... the coffee shop, the office, the gym, etc. All that was left was the street and physical fact of neighbors. Something woke up in lots of people. A memory of a capacity that had been sitting unused, possibly for decades.

And then it unfortunately receded. The emergency passed and so did the infrastructure of care that emerged around it. People went back inside, back online, back to that "managed distance." But we didn't forget what it felt like. That's what's been living in the back of our mind ... the proof. We saw people together because they had to, and found out it meant something. The question now is whether you have to wait for another emergency.

Mutual Aid. The term has a history as deep as the practice ... Peter Kropotkin wrote about it, watching animals and humans survive, not through competition, but through cooperation. Not charity, which flows downward from the one who has to the one who doesn't have. Not government assistance, which routes everything through bureaucracy and eligibility, and the slow machinery of institutions.

Mutual Aid is lateral. Neighbor to neighbor. The assumption isn't that some people are givers and some are receivers ... it's that everyone has something and everyone has need, and those two things just need to find each other. The grocery list. The hand-sewn mask. The text that says We're making soup, do you need some. No application. No proof of deservingness.

What the pandemic revealed is that this capacity was always there, lying underneath the surface of ordinary neighborhood life like ... and you'll recognize this feeling ... something enormous waiting. It didn't have to be built from scratch. It just had to be activated. Groups still going strong five years later figured something out: we don't need a crisis to justify caring for each other. The need is always there. The isolation is always there.

Aunties. That word carries everything ... authority without title, care without sentimentality, the kind of attention that misses nothing because it's rooted in love and long presence. An auntie doesn't wait to be asked. And kupuna ... elder ... not burden, not demographic, not a problem to be managed by a system. Someone whose length of days means something. And whose presence on the block is itself a form of wealth the neighborhood holds.

The rapid response works because we're already out there. Not dispatched from somewhere else. We live here and know the rhythms ... who's usually on the porch by eight, whose lights were off last Tuesday when they're always on, whose grandchildren come every Sunday and haven't come in three weeks. That knowledge isn't data. It can't be collected by an app or a survey. It lives in people who are present, consistently, over time, paying the particular attention that comes from genuinely caring what happens.

This is what institutions can't replicate. The government can send a caseworker. The caseworker can't be an auntie. That's not a failure of the caseworker ... it's a structural impossibility. Aunties are made by years of showing up on the same streets, in the same houses, watching and being watched, building the kind of trust that means someone opens the door when you knock. The street is the infrastructure and relationships are the system.

This is government. Not the building downtown, not the agency with the acronym ... this is what governance actually looks like when it works. People organized around function, need, the texture of a specific community's life. Food team. Transportation team. Patrol. Rent support for a hundred families. That's a hundred evictions that didn't happen. A hundred households that stayed intact. Children who woke up in the same bedroom. Elders who didn't have to move. The neighborhood that remained the neighborhood.

And the knock on Tutu's door. That might be the most important thing on the whole list because it's the one no system can automate. The knock that says we noticed you. We track your presence because your presence matters to us. You are not invisible on this block.

What you're describing is accountability without surveillance. The patrol that comes from Love rather than enforcement. The noticing that protects rather than threatens. The difference is everything ... same streets, completely different type of power.

Tutu knows when we knock that we're knocking because she belongs to something. Not because there's a protocol or someone filed a report. Because the aunties on the block have decided that belonging to each other is the whole point.

2020 was the proof. The government didn't have a plan. And people didn't wait. They built something out of nothing in a matter of days because the need was right there and the official response wasn't. That's the lesson that doesn't unhappen. Once we've seen our neighbors organize faster and more effectively than the federal government, something shifts in our political imagination. Not cynicism exactly ... something clearer. A realistic accounting of where the capacity actually lives.

And now FEMA pulling back. The calculation is being made at the highest levels that people can be left to manage catastrophe themselves ... but without the resources, without the infrastructure, without the mutual aid networks already in place. The cruelty of that is precise: withdraw the assistance while also doing nothing to build the lateral systems that could replace it.

Which means the communities that survive what's coming are the ones that already knocked on each other's doors. Already had a food team, transportation team, and a hundred families who know they can call someone. No one is coming to save us is not despair. In the mouth of someone who has already built the repair circle, already organized the patrol, already provided rent support ... it's just a description of reality that frees us to act. Waiting is dangerous. Organizing is much safer.

Before building anything, look for what's already there. Someone may have already mapped the need. The first act of mutual aid might be finding the people who are already doing it. The goodness is out there. That's the thing. It's not waiting to be made or funded into existence. It's in the neighbor who brings meals. It's latent in almost every block in every city ... it just needs a thing that is loose enough to let people bring what they actually have, and specific enough to direct it somewhere it can be.

The beginning is of course humble. Search your neighborhood. Ask around. Show up once and see what's needed. Maybe the group needs drivers or someone who can fix things. Maybe they need someone who will make calls. And if there's nothing ... if we search and the street comes back empty ... that absence is also information. The beginning is us, another person, and a list of things to do.

The wheel has already been invented many times over. By the free breakfast program. The indigenous communities that never stopped practicing reciprocity, because they never had the luxury of forgetting how. By every neighborhood that organized through a disaster and kept the structure alive.

There is wisdom in those older groups that took decades to earn. How to handle conflict within the circle. How to prevent burnout. How to keep it all lateral. We don't have to learn those lessons the hard way if we're willing to learn them from people who experienced it all.

If the street is empty ... we pool. Not fundraise or apply for a grant. Pool. What do we have between us. What can we put in the middle where everyone can get to it. Time, skills, a truck, a basement, a phone tree, awareness of who lives where and what they need. It starts embarrassingly small. A hand full of neighbors. A group chat. One person who knows how to fix bicycles, one person with a car, one person who bakes, and suddenly we have the start of a system.

Mutual aid is as old as humanity. As old as the first winter someone shared meat they had with someone who had none. It predates every institution, every government program, every nonprofit structure. It's the original human technology. We just forgot to call it anything because it was just called being alive together.

The item exchange is quietly radical. Everything in it was on its way to being garbage ... outgrown, outlived, no longer needed in one life ... and it comes back as exactly what someone else needs. Beautiful Garbage made social practice. The circle of stuff that mirrors the circle of care.

And the child walking to school carries the full weight of the moment. Someone's parents are inside, afraid to cross the threshold because it's too dangerous. And a neighbor says ... we will be the body that stands between this child and that danger. We will walk the route. Bring them home. No organization required. No funding. Deciding that any child on our block is also our responsibility.

That's the umbrella held wide enough to cover everything. The food. The exchange. The escort through a neighborhood, that has become frightening for some neighbors. The ride. The repair. The rent support. All of it the same thing.

Solidarity not charity. The distinction is everything and it lives in one word: own. They're meeting their own needs alongside their neighbors'. Not descending from a position of having enough to help those who don't. Standing in the same temperature of circumstances. Eating from the same table. Vulnerable to the same immigration raid, the same natural disaster, the same week the rent comes due and the bank balance number is low this month.

Charity requires a separation ... the helper and the helped, the giver and the receiver, the one who has and the one who lacks. That separation is encoded in the tax structure. The incorporated nonprofit with the executive director, and the board, and the annual report exists inside the logic of institutions, which is the logic of hierarchy, which eventually becomes the logic of who decides what the community needs, from a position of not being entirely inside that community.

Consensus is slower. Messier. Sometimes maddening. But it means the person most affected by a decision has the same weight as the person who's been organizing the longest. No one's voice gets amplified by a title. The repair circle doesn't have a Chief Executive Officer of Fixing Things.

And the tax deduction question is revealing. It asks: who benefits from this being formalized? The person who can write off a donation is usually not the person who needed the food. The structure that makes giving tax-advantaged also makes it legible to a system that wasn't built for solidarity. Mutual aid stays partly outside that system by design.

The table with food on it. No application. No proof. No one standing there to assess whether you deserve to take something. Just the table, and the food, and the implicit statement: this is here because you might need it. The simplicity of that is almost sort of shocking against the backdrop of how complicated we've made need and response.

And the community power map in New Orleans is genius because it solved the actual problem rather than the official version of the problem. The official version: power is out, people are suffering, wait for the grid to be restored. The mutual aid version: who still has power right now, tonight, and how do we connect that resource to the people who need it before morning. A porch becomes a charging station. A neighbor becomes a kind of infrastructure.

That's the solving our problem and our neighbor's problem at the same time. The person with electricity needed to know their electricity was useful to someone. The person without needed a place to plug in. The map just let those two people find each other. No institution required. No budget. Someone with a sheet and enough trust built up in the neighborhood that people would answer honestly.

It scales down beautifully. The table with food is the same logic as the power map. Here is what we have. Here is where we are. Come and take what you need. Tell us what you know and we'll tell you what we know and between us we'll get through tonight. Not the total solution. Just the things that could be done immediately, by the people who were there.

The money flowing through Venmo looks like income to the IRS, because the IRS wasn't designed to see solidarity. It sees transactions. Thousands of dollars moving through an account and it reads commerce, not care. So the same nimbleness that makes mutual aid powerful ... the quick, direct, no-bureaucracy flow of resources ... can create legal exposure for the people doing the work. The infrastructure of mutual aid has to think about the infrastructure of money without becoming the thing it's trying not to be.

And the address list. That might be the most serious thing. We're holding the location of people whose location could be used against them. That's not a data privacy question in the abstract — that's a specific human being, a specific family that trusted us with their whereabouts because they needed food and had no other choice. That trust is sacred. A phone confiscated, a platform that complies with a subpoena ... the consequences land on the most vulnerable person in the chain, not on the organizer.

So the nimbleness has to extend to security. Encrypted apps. Minimal data retention. Need-to-know information sharing. The same instinct that gets food out has to protect the address with equal urgency. What survives in all of this is the accountability. Not to a donor or funder. Not to the government. To the family that couldn't leave the house. The accountability runs close to the ground, to human beings, which is exactly where it should run. That's what makes it worth protecting.

Without a lawyer, without a board, without an official risk assessment ... just people who decided the need was more urgent than the liability. That's always been the calculus at the edge of care. We balance what could go wrong against what is already going wrong, for the person who needs help today. Most of the time the scale tips toward showing up.

And the word itself ... mutual aid ... is new to a lot of people who have been doing it their whole lives. The grandmothers who rotate dinners on the block don't call it anything. The neighbor who picks up prescriptions for the elderly man two doors down doesn't file it under a political philosophy. It's just Tuesday with Monique. It's just what we do. The practice is ancient, unnamed, and present everywhere that formal systems have failed to reach, which is most places, most of the time.

What's new is the visibility. The moment of recognition where someone hears the words mutual aid and thinks ... that's what we've been doing. That naming matters because it connects the dinner rotation to the power map. To the guy watching the street to the repair circle. It shows them as the same thing. A tradition. A lineage. Something with a history. The increase in participation right now isn't surprising.
