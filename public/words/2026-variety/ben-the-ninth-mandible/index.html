Warped Plays

Ben : The Ninth Mandible

IntroductionMovementsFirst·Second·Third·Fourth

Before every race a trumpet sounds from the booth, and this work has counted the notes. There are thirty-four, with no sharps and no flats in them. It knows the morning jog goes the wrong way around, so the horse will know it is not race day. It knows a horse will not urinate on command, so a man in a state jacket holds one note on a whistle, and holds it again. It knows that two dollars to win on the seven pays nine dollars and forty cents, and that the hotel on the grounds has a hundred and sixteen rooms.

Then it gives what it knows to the rats. Out of the fog behind the cart comes the colony, going the wrong way too, keeping the gait. The whistle is answered from a storm drain. Eight horses eating make one sound, and the audience is asked to listen for the ninth jaw, which is under the floor. The attention is obsessive, and every strange thing in this short work is made out of an exact one. That is what makes it different.

Ben : The Ninth Mandible is a work of bizarre visionary fiction by Paraploo. It sets two worlds on one stage. One comes from a 1972 horror film about a lonely boy and a rat named Ben. The other is a working harness track, with its hotel, its card room and its wetland.

It is told entirely as stage directions for a play in which nobody speaks. Whatever is said is reported. Six times the directions say that the audience is not told. A few plain things keep coming back and weigh more each time: a lead rope with nothing on the end of it, a lunch pail, a pair of hopples still bent to the horse that wore them, a hand of empty air, and one light that stays on.

The rats are called the hidden, and they live under the tote board. When the hunt comes it is seen from their side, and they are never made into monsters. The state, the directions say, has never thought to have a word for a rat. Here the hidden are ratified. The rat was inside that word before anyone said it.

The first movement tells the whole story in brief. The next three tell it again, slowly. It reads in about an hour.

The stage is dark with a sound in it. Water in the grass, and things calling out each other. A light comes, far upstage, a picture window’s worth, enough . Light at the shelters, and someone is in there, always is ...

The oval is drawn around the water. In the middle of the water stands the board, and it is dark. Under the board a storm drain opens, round and black, older than the track and older than the ponds, and goes down under the water to a chamber. The chamber holds thousands of the hidden. They came up from the city under the city, through two hundred miles of pipe, because a man in an office ordered the exterminators and one of them was at the door listening. The ground does not care who lives under it. It never has ...

Enter a boy, from the barns, with a lead rope over his arm and nothing on the end of it. He walks the way people walk who have been told not to run. His name is Danny. He has a scar down his chest that the audience cannot see. He lives in a trailer on the backside with the grooms, where the house puts the people who feed, and the house has never walked out to count how many sleep in it. He is the only child whose name is on nothing, no stall card, no program, no webbing. His father is dead, his mother is away all night, and his sister is at the hotel making a hundred and sixteen beds for nobody ...

In the tack room a pair of hopples hangs on a nail, still bent to the horse that wore them, and a rat asleep in the shape. He is large and dark with small ears and a pink nose. He has a price on him in a city a long way south and a name in a diary the newspaper printed. The name is Ben. He came up the drain behind the colony, past the chamber, up through the grass, under the rail, into the barn where the one light is always on. Danny opens the lunch pail. Danny holds out half a sandwich. The rat takes it from the hopples without coming down, and the hopples swing, and the horse that is not in them swings too ...

The boy tells the rat that he can tell the rat understands him. The rat says nothing, and the nothing is kept the way a nicker is kept, a note saved for the handful of people a rat knows. That is the whole play, and the rest is weather ...

Lights down. Lights up on the feed cart coming down the shedrow, eight horses calling for it, then the sound of eight horses eating, which is one sound. The audience is asked to listen for the ninth jaw. It is under the floor. The colony taking what falls through the boards, hay and oats and the sweet feed, and the sound comes up through the soles of the feet, and the groom stops with the wheelbarrow and starts again. A barn talks in its sleep. This one talks in two voices ...

Five rats play one rat. The trainer raised them from birth, and the trainer’s son lay in a box for months with peanut butter on him while the colony ate its supper off a boy, two hundred at a time, so that none of them would be afraid of a hand. On this stage the trainer’s son is the groom, and the straw is the box, and when the night check comes down the row with the flashlight off there is a boy asleep in the fourth stall with the hidden eating off him in the dark, gently, and the night check counts his ribs rising three times and goes on. Twelve breaths a minute is a horse asleep on its feet. Under the floor they breathe faster, at the rate of something small that has learned to listen for boots ...

The morning jog goes clockwise, the wrong way, so the horse knows it is not race day. Out of the fog on the backstretch comes a horse made of fog with a hooded driver, two beats on the lime, and behind the cart, in a line, the colony, keeping the gait. They go the wrong way too. A rat that breaks into a run is pulled wide by the others and brought back to its pace, and the one that gained ground by running is set back in the placings. The state has a word for a horse that does that. The state has never thought to have a word for a rat ...

A woman with a folder paces off the far bank for a wedding, and writes, and paces again. Where she paces, the grass gives, and the couple with her notice nothing. At six the light will come copper off the far turn and stand behind them for eleven minutes, and nobody will remember a word they said and everybody will remember the water. The audience is asked to remember what is under the water ...

The housekeeper on the fifth floor opens a room and it is already made. It has been that way since the four months. Every morning the bed is tight, and the curtains are open on the oval, and nobody has been in it, and the housekeeper strips it and makes it again, because the list says so. In the wastebasket this morning there are four torn tickets and a cheese rind. The housekeeper looks at the rind a long while and empties the basket, and says nothing to the supervisor, since the supervisor was here for the five days too and knows the building was never empty, and both of them keep that ...

Enter the house, rolling. It is the mansion, the old one, sawn into three pieces along the lines of its own blueprints, and it comes down the backstretch at eleven at night on twelve trucks, the guests aboard, a farewell party lit by stable lanterns, the mayor and the mayor’s wife among them, and a man with a famous name at the piano. The hidden ride it too, in the walls, where they lived before the policemen pried the panel loose like the lid of a coffin and the first man through did not come back up the cellar stairs. The house crosses the gap in the rail and goes around once on the inside, slowly, with its windows up, and out through the paddock, and its tires leave three lines across the combed sand that the tractor will not take out by morning. The guard at the gate writes something on the clipboard and says something short. The house is a hotel now. It has a hundred and sixteen rooms and was made for nobody for four months, and the people in its walls have never left it ...

Lights up on the test barn. A man in a state jacket holds one note on a whistle, and holds it again, because a horse will not urinate on command and learns to tie the whistle to it. From the drain under the board, far off, a whistle answers. One note. Then another, from the other side of the water. The state man stops and looks at the groom and the groom looks at the floor. The horse’s ears are turned back toward the drain. The state keeps no sheet for what answers a whistle ...

The boy is in the tack room with the harmonica. He plays a song he has made up about a friend, and the song never says what the friend is, because a song about a rat does not use the word cheese. There is a line the boy plays twice, where the one singing now says us and we. The boy is winded after the song. He unbuttons his shirt and shows the rat the scar. The rat looks at it the way the rat looks at everything, straight on, and the audience is not told what the rat knows about hearts ...

At dusk the starting car comes out with its wings spread, and the field noses up behind it, eight horses and thirty-two legs and sixteen wheels and eight men sitting on nothing. The car rolls down the stretch. The wings fold. The car pulls off and does not pull off into the paddock gap. It drives onto the infield, to the board, folds its wings all the way in, and goes down the storm drain headfirst, and the field runs the mile without it. It was the curtain that was moving. The stage held still ...

In the stretch the favorite breaks, and its driver stands up in the bike like a man given bad news on the phone, and the man with the pencil at the rail writes down the time of night. He has eleven years of them and no pattern. He says, to the horses going into the turn and to nobody else, that living is a crazy wild night at the track. Under the apron the hidden hear it through the paving, every word, and from that night it is theirs, kept in the older set of books, the ones with no columns ...

The winner stands for its picture under the lights with a blanket across its back and people in good coats placed around it. The rat stands for a picture too. There is a prize for animals who act, a small statue, and the rat won it, and the photograph was taken in a magazine office with the trainer holding him up to the light like a trophy fish. At the gap the blanket comes off the horse. The statue stays on the rat. Nobody has found a way to take a prize off a rat ...

The last night of the meet. The twelfth race goes official and the board says thank you for the season and the lights go off in banks, backstretch, far turn, stretch, and the apron is white with tickets. The man with the canvas bag waits ten minutes by the watch, because people come back for what they dropped. Then he bends at the knees and turns every paper over with two fingers and reads it and lets it go face down. Behind him the papers turn back over, one at a time, face up, the way a room full of cards turns when a hand goes under the table. The hidden are reading the tickets. They read the two hundred dollars to win on the horse that ran fourth, and the torn nine forty, and the show ticket on the eight that ran sixth that a girl carried past the last bench in her hand, whole. The sweeper comes through at eleven and the ridge of paper goes into the pan and the pan into the bag, and the bags are full at the fourth bench, which has never happened on a Tuesday. The apron is bare from rail to building by midnight, and under it the chamber is paper to the ceiling, every losing half of every wish, kept all winter ...

Enter the men. They come at three in the morning, down the service road, construction workers and police and firefighters, working from the engineer’s maps of the drains, with flamethrowers and the hoses the barns use to wash down a horse, and they go in under the board. The oval lights come on, every bank at once, a ring of light standing in the dark around the water, and the cranes get up out of the shallows calling and circle once low over the far turn and do not leave. The hidden drive the men back up the drain twice. The two policemen go down themselves. The audience sees none of it. The audience sees the apron, bare, lit, and feels the paving tremble under the benches in two beats, the way a rail trembles when the field is passing, for most of an hour. On the fifth floor the curtains are open and a man who could not sleep stands at the glass with a hand flat against it, looking out through the shape of his own hand ...

The boy goes down the drain with a flashlight to warn his friend. His sister goes down after him and is lost in a tunnel, and the hidden fall on her, and some of them are stuffed and tied to her with string so that they swing when she thrashes, and some of them are real, and the audience is not to be told which. The boy finds the rat and tells him that he loves him and begs him to run. The rat does not run. A rat that runs is set back in the placings. The boy follows the rat’s directions out and meets his sister and the two climb a ladder through a manhole in the middle of the infield, under the board, and stand on the grass in the ring of light with the fire going on below them and the cranes circling, and the sister is still wearing a few of the hidden on their strings ...

Dawn. The lights go off. The fog comes up off the water as high as the top rail. The rail comes out of it first, one line, then the posts under it, then the sand, combed in thousands of fine lines after the twelfth, and across the lines the tracks of a deer, and the tracks of a crane walking the turn, and the three lines of a house. The firemen come up the drain carrying nothing. They tell the newspaperman they have never faced such a determined adversary, and the old newspaperman answers that all any creature wants is room, and the word is left on the apron with the tickets. A groom and a horse go down the gravel together, two legs beside four, with a hand of empty air between the fist and the halter ...

In the tack room the boy is crying over a marionette he built in the shape of his friend. There is a squeak in the hopples. The rat has come back, wounded, up through the grass and under the rail and in at the one window with the light on. The boy gets the first aid kit from the shelf above the liniment. The boy tells the rat that they will get well together. The audience is not told whether either of them does. The rat looks straight out at the house, at us, the way he did at the end of the first play, and on the far wall of the hallway a figure drawn in one line with a tremble in it looks out the same way, and a sentence comes up in the handwriting beside it, stays long enough to be read twice, and goes. The one warm incandescent light in every shelter stays on ...

Half past four, and the one light at the barns is the only light. Enter Danny, down the shedrow, with the lead rope over his arm and nothing on the end of it, ahead of the grooms, who come ahead of the feed, which comes ahead of the light. In the tack room the hopples hang on the nail, bent to the horse that is gone, and the rat is awake in them. He was awake before the boy. He is awake before anything on this property, the cat included, and the barn keeps its clock by him the way the oval keeps its clock by the board. Danny sets the lunch pail on the trunk and opens it. Ben comes down the hopples one strap at a time and takes the crust from the boy’s fingers, and the hopples swing behind him, empty, with the old horse still in them. That is the day begun. Nothing on these grounds starts until he puts a foot on the floor ...

Lights up on the feed room. A sack of sweet feed leans on the wall with its corner open the size of a coin, the same corner as yesterday. The feed man blames the cat, and the cat on the bale, holding the only job on the property with tenure, does not get up to answer. Danny knows the corner. Ben showed it to him the first morning, the way the trainer shows a boy a leg, and then sat on the sack like a thing accused of nothing. Under the floor the ninth jaw takes what falls through the boards. The feed man kicks the sack square to the wall and goes out. The rat watches the feed man go the way he watches everything, straight on, and the corner is open again by five ...

The newspaper has printed the diary. A groom reads it out in the tack room with the radio down, that the first man trained the hidden to heel and beg and roll over and play dead, and that the one at the front was called Ben. Danny’s sister, stopped in on the way to the fifth floor, says the boy’s friend is probably that one, the rat in the paper, and laughs, and Danny does not laugh. He tries the name on the rat in the hopples. A horse comes to a nicker. The rat comes to nothing. He looks at the boy straight on when the name is said, and that is how the name takes. Danny keeps it. At the rail that night the man with the pencil reads the same diary between the third race and the fourth and says, to the horses going into the turn, that playing dead is the one trick nobody ever had to be taught ...

Enter the exterminator, in a county truck, with a sprayer and a clipboard and a stack of green boxes. A deputy goes down the shedrows ahead of the truck and tells the trailers that traps and poison go down tonight along the barns and under the apron, and that children and cats are to be kept in. Danny goes down the row with Ben in the crook of his arm and shows him each thing, the wire, the plate, the pellet in the green box, the way the trainer goes down a leg with a hand, and the rat looks at each thing and then at the boy. That night the line comes up out of the drain and goes past the first green box because Ben goes past it first, and past the second the same way, the way grooms go past a horse resting on three legs. In the morning the deputy counts the boxes and finds every one full and every pellet dry, and writes that on the sheet, and the sheet has no column for a rat that was shown ...

Fog on the backstretch at six. The four horse goes out the wrong way with a kid in a parka behind him, two beats on the lime, and under the inside rail the colony keeps the gait. Danny stands at the gap with the lead rope, where the oldest pony on the grounds stands flat-footed and every green two-year-old is walked past him twice. A heart going too fast is stood in front of the old pony and the heart slows and nobody is charged. Ben sits on the rail post at the pony’s nose. The boy’s hand is on the pony’s neck and the rat is at the boy’s hand, and the heart with the scar over it comes down, softly, and the pony does not look at the rat and the rat does not look at the pony. The barn keeps the pony in cheap peppermints for it, and has not yet noticed what it keeps the rat in ...

Three in the morning, the tavern kitchen. The cook is filtering the fryer oil, and the pump sends it through the paper and back, and the crumb stays in the paper. Danny is in the chair beside the walk-in with his shoes off, waiting for his mother’s break, which comes at half past one and has not come, since the table with eleven screens does not close. The lunch pail is on his knee. The walk-in holds six trout. The cook takes the clipboard off the door and goes in and counts and comes out and writes, and behind the cook the floor drain lifts a quarter inch and settles. Ben is not in the pail. At four the bartender asks whether the trout is gone. The cook says there were six. There are none, and not a bone on the floor, and the cook looks at the boy, and the boy looks at the pail, and the pail has the rat in it again, asleep, and the bartender writes six on the pad by the register, the one place on the grounds where a rat’s supper is entered as a loss ...

The breakfast room at six. The mural on its wall holds the fish farm and the card tables and the horses and the skyline of the city a long way off, all in one picture. Danny carries the case of small water bottles up from the kitchen, which is worth a dollar to the man in whites, and sets it by the ice machine, and goes in for the first coffee of the morning for the sister, and stops in the door. The cereal is out of its clear tubes and across the floor. The sugar packets are open one at a time. A box with a bird on it lies on its side with the bird looking at the ceiling. On the counter, under the painted skyline, Ben sits on the lever of the one dispenser still full, holding it shut. Danny sweeps the floor with the side of his shoe and wipes the sugar with his sleeve, and the rat gets down and goes into the pail. The housekeeper who dusts the sand table first comes in with the cloth and sees a clean floor, and the empty tubes, and the boy, and says nothing, since the five days taught the fifth floor that a building is never empty ...

The enclosed walkway joins the hotel to the long building so that nobody has to cross the lot, and at three in the morning it carries guests in socks going over for a plate. Tonight it carries the hidden the other way, in the ceiling, from the bare walk-in toward the pool kept warm all night for nobody and the hot tub beside it, because warm wet air is where a colony sleeps when it can. Danny is on the walkway under them, coming back from the kitchen with the pail, and he hears them the way the ground gives word of horses before the ears have any. The pail goes still on his arm. The stillness is an order. The line passes over the boy in the ceiling and does not come down, and goes on, and at the far end a woman in socks meets what comes out of the ceiling, and the screaming is on both sides of the glass at once. At the table with eleven screens Danny’s mother plays the hand out, spreads both hands, turns the palms up to the camera in the ceiling and steps away, and the camera that has seen those palms every night for five years sees the walkway too and does not ask ...

At the gap there is a way everybody turns, and a bigger boy, the son of a man in a good coat, is there on a Thursday to see that Danny turns it wrong. The bigger boy takes hold of the lead rope with nothing on the end of it and asks what is on the end of it, and asks again, louder, and the asking is all a boy like that has. What is on the end of it is Ben, in the grass under the rail, where nobody looks. The hidden come up the bigger boy’s legs in a line, and the bigger boy runs the length of the shedrow and out the end of it, and the horses put their heads over the doors one after another to watch the bigger boy go, the way heads come out along a street for a parade. At dusk the two policemen come to the trailer with the bigger boy and the bigger boy’s mother, and Danny says the bigger boy fell in the roses by the hotel patio. When the name from the newspaper comes up, Danny brings out the marionette he built in the shape of his friend and works it in the gravel on its strings, with a tremble in its walk, so the grown people will see a friend made of wood and string and nothing else. The grown people look at the wrong rat a long while. The right one watches from the hopples, through the tack room window, straight on. They leave ...

On a dark day with no racing Danny asks the rat to show him where the hidden live. Ben goes ahead, down the storm drain under the board, and the boy follows with the flashlight off, since a light in the face wakes a thing, and through the pipe that carried the fish farm’s water off before there was a board. The pipe opens into the chamber. Thousands of the hidden, and the paper, every losing half of every wish, kept to the ceiling, and under the paper the nineteen ponds, room after room, dry at the top and wet at the floor. The hidden start toward the boy. Ben turns, once, and they fall back, the way a line swings wide around the one who is new to the gap. Danny tells the rat it is a fine house. He says it the way a thing is said to somebody who built the thing. Then he goes back up the pipe on his hands, and the breath is short, and the rat goes behind him the whole way, at the pace of something small that has learned the sound of a heart ...

The boy comes up through the manhole in the middle of the infield, under the board, and sits on the grass with his back to the stand, winded, with his shirt open, and the scar is in daylight for the first time. Ben sits at the scar’s lower end, where it goes under the belt. On the far bank the woman with the folder is pacing off a wedding for a couple, water, grass, sky, in that order, and the folder takes down a boy under the tote board and a rat on a boy, and the couple notice nothing. At six the light will come copper off the far turn and stand behind the couple for eleven minutes, and nobody will remember a word they said, and the board above the boy will come on red with the odds, and the rat will have gone down the manhole before the first number ...

The radio in the tack room says the police are widening the campaign. Danny turns it down to where the sound reaches and the words do not, and the words have already reached. That night the sister lets him into the room on the fifth floor that is always made, because the sister has the key and the room is nobody’s, and he sleeps in a bed made for nobody with the curtains open on the dark oval. At the glass, five floors up, a face comes, and then another, up the siding the way Ben came in at the one window with the light on, and the boy opens the window and lets them in and tucks them under the sheet one at a time, the way anything is tucked in that is cold. Ben lies at the scar. In the morning the sister opens the door with an armful of linen and sees the bed, and the small shapes breathing under the sheet beside the boy, and the scream goes the length of the corridor and comes back. The supervisor’s radio says the room number and then says nothing ...

The two policemen are at the trailer by noon, since a thing seen on a walkway gets a sheet and a thing seen on the fifth floor gets two. They ask the boy where the rat lives. Danny is the only one on the grounds who has been shown, and the only one who will not say. He holds the lead rope with nothing on the end of it the whole time they ask. The policemen go back to the office behind the card room, where an engineer from the county has unrolled a map older than the lot, drawn for the fish farm, on paper with a giant fish printed in one corner, twenty ponds numbered and the pipes between them and the outlet that goes under what is now the board. The engineer says the men have walked what a man can walk and need several more days. Where the cars are parked, the paper has water. What the map does not have is the rat, and the rat is at that moment under the office floor, in the pipe the map draws in red, listening to the paper being flattened by four hands ...

Some mornings a race is run at noon with nothing on it. The windows are shut and the apron is empty, and a horse that broke too often has to go a mile against the clock without breaking before the judges let it back in. Today it is the one, the favorite that broke in the stretch, and the one goes the mile alone, the right way around, in the full light. Danny stands at the gap with the lead rope. Ben sits on the inside rail post at the gap, and the line goes out from him under the rail and goes the mile too, keeping pace, and none of them breaks, and the line comes back to him. The judges write the horse and the time on the sheet and let the one back in. The sheet has one line on it. The boy’s hand is on the post beside the rat the whole mile, and his heart goes the mile with the horse, and holds its gait ...

One night near the end of the meet the city station plays the song the boy made up on the harmonica, sung now by somebody else, somebody young and a long way off, with strings behind it that the tack room never had. It was written for a different boy, a famous one, who was on tour, and so it went to a boy of thirteen who wept at the microphone and went on singing. The stations would not play it at first, since it was about a rat, and the people who called in did not care. Danny turns the radio up for the length of it, and the words reach, and the rat in the hopples turns his head toward the speaker and holds it there, the way a horse holds its ears on the whistle at the test barn. The song never once says the rat’s name or what he is. Under the floor the ninth jaw keeps time with the strings. Then Danny turns it back down ...

A van comes in two barns away. Toward it walks the old horse with gray in his face, the pacer of fourteen who won more than he cost twice over, in a halter and no hopples, because the hopples are the ones on the nail, still bent to him. Danny stops with the wheelbarrow to watch. The woman at the halter has walked the horse nine years and says the crying will be done in the truck, on the way back. The ramp goes up. In the straw of the slot, under the hay net, three of the hidden go to the farm with the horse, and nobody teaches them anything, and they get one. Ben does not go. He stays in the shape of the horse on the nail, and the shape is the only thing of that horse left on the grounds, and the rat keeps it the way the hidden keep the tickets, all winter, with the boy’s lunch pail under it ...

Evening. The lights stay off over the oval and come up on the lot. Cars turn in from the road with the old name and line up in rows facing the screen, and a sign at the entrance gives the radio frequency, and on the strip of old grass at the edge a woman of sixty or so sets up a lawn chair. Tonight the screen has the two plays on one bill, the first and the second, the way the poster teamed them the year nobody made the third. In the second row Danny sits in the passenger seat of the trainer’s truck, because the trainer told him to come and see a picture, and he has never seen one. It is the first time he has sat facing the lot and not the track. The lunch pail is on his knee, and the pail is heavier than a lunch pail, and the trainer has not asked ...

At eight the screen comes on white, and the house gets its thirty seconds, its name and its horses in letters twenty feet tall. Then a card, white on black. It says the negative could not be found, and that the best print left in any vault was used in its place, and that this does not mean the picture was cleaned. Then the picture, soft, the blacks gone to gray, the color faded to the color of a thing kept in a drawer, the sound low, a copy of a copy away from whatever happened. The pail opens a crack on the boy’s knee. Ben looks at the screen. He is the first thing in the lot to look at it, ahead of the woman in the lawn chair, who is not looking at it at all ...

The song comes out of a hundred radios at a hundred volumes and lies over the gravel in pieces. A line starts in one car and finishes in another. Under the gravel the hidden hear every piece from every side and put the pieces together, and it is the first time anyone has sung to them at the pitch of a hundred radios. The rat in the truck has it whole before they do, because he is above the gravel and in it both. Danny does not know the words. He knows the tune, and the tune is his, and he puts the harmonica to his mouth in the cab, low, and plays under the hundred radios, and the trainer does not turn ...

On the screen a boy opens a lunch pail in a workroom and a face comes through a hole in the screen door, and the boy in the truck knows the face, and the face in the pail on his knee is the same face, looking at itself twenty feet tall across a lot. Neither face moves. The trainer looks at the screen and keeps looking at it, and that is the whole of what gets said. In the lawn chair the woman was here the night the devil lost, when the whole grass screamed like a horse race, and the morning after the farm paid two girls five dollars apiece to pick cups off this grass all day, and the woman has never forgotten how a field looks the morning after thousands of people have been happy on it. The boy has never seen a field like that. The rat has. The chamber is one, paper to the ceiling ...

At the reel change the picture jumps and a line of light runs down it top to bottom, and for a second the lot sees the scratch instead of the drain. A print this worn has played every small town that would have it, eighteen months of Fridays, and carries all of them in the grain, and the boy’s own town is in there somewhere, and the boy’s own night. On the screen the rat comes back wounded to the workroom and the boy gets the first aid kit from the shelf, and the rat looks straight out at the lot, and the rat on the knee looks straight out at the screen, and a hundred windshields stand between them, and the one that is wounded and the one that is not yet wounded hold each other’s eye the length of the song. Danny closes the pail. Then the screen goes white, and dark ...

The boy is watching the drain, and the old pony beside him, who has calmed forty springs of young horses and never turned his head for one of them, turns it.
