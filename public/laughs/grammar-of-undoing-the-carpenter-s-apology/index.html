From Grammar of Undoing

each particle floating upward like prayers returning to heaven embarrassed they'd been answered

She built Joy a table that existed in seventeen dimensions, its surface visible in three, its legs extending through parallel kitchens where other Angelicas built other tables.

Her workshop filled with skills that undid themselves—Spanish that made you forget English word by word, canning that preserved emptiness in jars that glowed with fullness, piano lessons that removed the ability to hear until silence became symphonic.

Angelica became an axolotl of rage. Not transformation but revelation: she'd always been amphibious with anger, regenerating fury every time they cut it away, each new rage pinker and more tender than the last.

She attended meetings with her emotional organs visible through translucent skin, her disappointment a gray liver, her hope a tiny appendix threatening to burst. When she spoke, you could watch her lungs fill with arguments that would never convince anyone but needed to be exhaled into the municipal record.

She ran for council and received exactly eleven votes from the eleven people who didn't exist yet—her unborn mother voted through séance (the medium charged by the minute but time ran backward so Angelica got paid), her tomorrow-self voted through time-loop, creating a paradox that made City Hall's clocks hiccup. The newspaper called her "The Woman Who Lost to Herself" in headlines that deleted themselves after printing.

They called her "The Backwards Carpenter" in papers written in ink made from unborn squid, words that swam off the page at night.

Highway 49 curves like God's question mark, asking why humans drive toward destinations that disappointment has already reserved.

She met Capital Monk—a pangolin made of compressed prayers, each scale a different request to God that had been marked "return to sender."

Married someone we called Storm David Who Chased Yesterday's Weather—drawing maps of storms that unhappened, tracking tornadoes that spun time counterclockwise until cows flew backward into barns.

Three children arrived in four years but in reverse order—the youngest born first at age sixteen, aging backward toward infancy, the eldest emerging as infant with adult memories, crying with the sophisticated sorrow of someone who's already lived through what's coming.

Her workshop became a temporal commune where chronology went to die. Teenagers unlearned carpentry, their hands forgetting skills they'd never mastered

David left on a Tuesday that happened on a Wednesday, temporal infidelity being grounds for divorce in most dimensions. Said he loved her but couldn't compete with her infinite capacity to care about nothing with the passion others reserved for everything.

Angelica dated in reverse chronology—a photographer who lasted three months in the future (they'd meet next year, break up last spring), a baker who survived six months in the past (their relationship ended before it began, perfect in its impossibility), a social worker who became a friend in a parallel timeline where friendship meant something different.

"I'm built for adjacency," she explained, her lovers existing in neighboring dimensions, close enough to feel their warmth through the walls of reality.

She made furniture for refugees from existence: Joy Deck needed a chair for her unbody (something to not-sit in while not-being), the Moth Council required tables that existed only at night (disappearing at dawn, reappearing at dusk with breakfast already set), Capital Monk wanted picture frames for photographs that would never be taken, holding absence in gilt borders.

A bed unmade itself so insomnia could flourish.

Sitting in it is like sitting in the idea of sitting, supportive but theoretical.
