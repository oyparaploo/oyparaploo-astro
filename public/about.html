About

We make things out of what gets thrown away.

Paraploo is the work of one person, born in 1959, who lives in the Twin Cities, Minnesota, and says "we" instead of "I". Everything here is free to read and to look at. If you open one thing first, open Start here: a few writings of every kind →

That first line is meant plainly. A chair with one short leg, a bent nail, a tail light found under a bridge, a phone text nobody was supposed to keep, a sentence half heard and almost forgotten: these are our materials. We pick them up, change them by hand, and set them where somebody might be glad to find them. The writing is made the same way the objects are. So are the drawings, which start with a pen and a blank sheet and nothing else.

We do not think of this as rescue, and it is not a way of tidying up. Things get thrown away because they stopped fitting, and now and then that happens to a person too. We would rather stay beside what stopped fitting and see what it turns into. Most of the time it turns into something useful. Sometimes it turns into something funny. Once in a while it turns into both, and those are the days we work for.

An umbrella is a roof small enough to carry, and it is easy to hold over one more person. That is the reason for it. In a writing about neighbors looking after each other through frightening days, with the food, the rides and the walk to school, the umbrella turns up like this:

How to find your way

There is a great deal here, and nobody has to see all of it. A few plain tips make the going easier, and each of them ends at a door.

Hold on to this much: everything here comes from the same habit. Something is on its way out, an object, a word, a sound, a mistake, and it gets caught and kept. The writing does it. So do the drawings, the piles and the photographs. A visitor who carries that idea along can start anywhere and not be lost.

Say it out loud.

When a piece looks strange on the page, the page is the wrong place to judge it. Hand it to the mouth, go slowly, and then give it another go. We have left instructions.

Writings picked for the voice: Read it out loud →

Trust the rough parts.

A bent nail, a crossed-out word left in plain sight, a corner nobody tidied: none of these got past us. They were kept in, the way a knot is kept in a rope.

Where the roughest things are: Placings →

Do not sort the funny from the sad.

A piece may get a laugh and then, a line later, catch in the throat. Both were meant, and nobody has to decide which kind of piece it was. We heard about this at home.

With a Mark, let the eye try.

It will go hunting for words, as it does with any handwriting, and it will come back without any. That is not a failure. The hunting is how a Mark gets seen. The words beside each drawing are its title, and we gave it. Choose a drawing and it opens large; after that the slideshow turns by itself, and all a visitor has to do is sit.

With a mirrored picture, wait.

Stay on it longer than seems polite. Sooner or later something in there looks back, an owl, a mask, a face from the old neighborhood, and whatever turns up belongs to the visitor who saw it.

Some of it is hard.

There are writings here that look straight at war, at grief and at harm done to people. They are not here to shock anybody.

For a visitor whose week is already heavy, there is a door made for that: For a hard week →

And for getting around:

the words across the top of every page each open a menu of everything in that part, and the foot of every page is the whole directory. Search finds a writing, a gallery, or a picture by its catalogue number. Every list puts the newest on top. Above each writing's title, a small line says what kind of writing it is and how long it takes to read, so nobody opens a long play expecting a short prayer. A visitor who would sooner be taken around can take a walk. The Walks page asks how much time there is, and offers what fits.
