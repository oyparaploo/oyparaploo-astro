Conduction

Otto Paraploo

Frazey Ford

In physics, conduction is the transfer of heat or electrical energy through direct contact between materials — no medium required, no conversion necessary, just the press of one body against another and the thing passes through. Touch a heated element and you receive exactly what it holds. This is not metaphor. The energy does not know it is traveling.

She stood in Royal Studios in Memphis, Tennessee, at the microphone that had belonged to Ann Peebles, and felt the history of that place coming up through the floor. Those are her words: up through the floor. Not from the walls, not from the air, not from the photographs. From the floor. The body knows what the mind is still composing sentences about, and hers reported: something is passing through the soles of your feet right now, something older than your ability to name it.

This is what conduction looks like from the inside.

Frazey Obadiah Ford was born in British Columbia in 1973 to parents who had fled across the Canadian border to avoid American political persecution. Her father, a Texan, was a conscientious objector to Vietnam, pursued by the FBI, the subject of active surveillance. Her mother was French-Canadian, from Cajun stock, knew the old songs. They took refuge first on communes, then on farms, then eventually in Toronto. The stories Frazey grew up hearing from her older brothers were stories of being on the run — of a father who was hunted, a mother who survived by singing.

This is where conduction began: her mother singing around the house to cope with what had happened to them, and then, when Frazey was five or six years old, asking her daughter to carry the lead part of songs so she could add the harmony. The mother handed the melody to the child. The child received it. The energy transferred. Neither of them could have known what was being conducted that afternoon — only that something was being held between them that required two bodies to sustain.

She grew up in the Kootenay Mountains of British Columbia, in a community where she described her voice as her contribution. She had no job, but people came to hear her sing because it made them feel better. This is an old use of a voice — communal medicine, not entertainment. She was seventeen, maybe eighteen, doing something her people had always done: converting pain into sound and offering it to whoever needed it.

Then: Asia, with her mother and sister. Guatemala. A small café. The first public performance. And then a nervous breakdown, which is the body refusing to continue conducting what it has been given without some kind of release. In the aftermath of that rupture, she encountered — fully, consciously, irreversibly — the music of Hi Records. Al Green. Ann Peebles. The Hodges brothers: Charles on organ, Leroy on bass, Teenie on guitar. Memphis soul from the 1970s, recorded in a building she would not enter for another two decades.

She heard "Love and Happiness" and thought it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard. She did not hear it as a genre. She heard it as a destination.

The years between the hearing and the arrival are the years of the apprenticeship she could not yet name. Montreal — a brutal winter alone, writing and writing and believing the writing was nothing, then discovering six months later that all of it was something, that the notebooks held the songs she would bring to the group she was about to co-found in Vancouver. A trip hop band. Then Saltwater June, a soul/folk collaboration with guitarist Trish Klein. Then an Al Green cover band, which was not a cover band — it was practice for a lineage she had not yet been invited to enter. Then The Be Good Tanyas, formed in 1999 in Vancouver with Klein and Samantha Parton, the three of them having met and scattered and reconverged after years of tree-planting in the Kootenays, of traveling in different directions across the continent. The Tanyas gave her nine years, three albums, an international audience, a reputation she would need to temporarily set aside to become who she actually was.

The country influence of the Tanyas was not her deepest influence. Al Green had always been there in the background. She fronted the folk revival, and in the background of that, Memphis was waiting.

In 2010, the year after the Tanyas' hiatus, she released Obadiah on Nettwerk — an intimate record, produced with drummer and longtime collaborator John Raham, tracked in configurations that sound like a log cabin rather than a studio. Her mother came in to sing harmony on a song about looking back at the hippie era through a mother's eyes. The neighbor contributed. The landlord. A family document as much as an album. And on it, a song called "If You Gonna Go" — which a filmmaker named Robert Gordon heard on Memphis radio, and which changed what happened next.

Robert Gordon had done this before. In 2006 he had brought Chan Marshall — recording as Cat Power — to Royal Studios to work with the Hi Rhythm Section. He recognized something in Frazey Ford's voice on the radio, tracked down her name, and made a call. He invited her to Memphis. She flew there without many songs written. This is the relay: Gordon conducting Ford toward the musicians who were already waiting for her, the same way he had conducted Morgan toward them eight years before. A lineage being passed forward through a filmmaker who recognized transmission when he heard it.

The Hi Rhythm Section — Charles, Leroy, and Teenie Hodges — initially thought they were guns for hire. They had done session work for decades. They knew how to execute what someone else had imagined. But Ford and Raham arrived without rigid plans, without prescribed roles, wanting response rather than execution: I'm interested in how people respond. I want their energy. After the first few songs, the brothers opened up. Charles Hodges later described it: when he played the first song it embraced him and he didn't want it to come off him. He said he just had a feeling about the whole project. He wanted to do it with Frazey.

This is the moment when professional transaction becomes something else. When the musicians stop being hired and start being collaborators. When the energy flows both directions.

She stood at Ann Peebles' microphone. She sang the songs she had been building toward since she first heard Hi Records in her late teens after a breakdown in Guatemala after her parents crossed a border fleeing the FBI after her mother handed her the melody at five years old so she could add the harmony. She felt the history coming up through the floor. The body knows. Indian Ocean — released in 2014, the product of two trips to Memphis and multiple Vancouver sessions with sixteen musicians total — is the record where conduction becomes fully audible. NPR's review noted that the songs sound decanted from her earlier work: denser material settling to the bottom under pressure. The voice more confident. The band brighter and louder. Obadiah was beautiful and gentle, the review said. Indian Ocean runs deep.

What runs deep is the thing she had been conducting all along. The lineage from her mother's Cajun singing to Emmylou Harris to Otis Redding at eleven to Hi Records in her late teens to the Al Green cover band to the folk revival to Memphis to the floor of Royal Studios to Teenie Hodges' guitar and Charles Hodges' organ and Ann Peebles' microphone and her own voice, which was always the instrument that mattered, which had always been able to carry the lead while someone else added the harmony.

By 2020 she was ready to follow where the rhythm wanted to take her. The third album, U Kin B the Sun, emerged from late-night improvisation sessions in Vancouver with bassist Darren Parris and drummer Leon Power — Ford deliberately releasing the melodic lead to see where Darren's bass lines would take her. I was excited to let go of control. The shift from the previous albums was not only sonic — soul and psychedelia and '70s funk where there had been folk-inflected soul — but philosophical. She described the change as being more in her body. More in touch with the wholeness of anger. Taking up space and having boundaries.

Being more in the body means letting the body conduct more completely. The body is the oldest instrument, the one that was handed a melody before it had language for what it was receiving. The body that felt Memphis history coming up through the floor. The body that carried the lead while her mother harmonized, that sang to a rural commune in the Kootenays because singing was her contribution, that broke down in Guatemala and discovered in the aftermath what it had always been moving toward.

The album opens with "Azad" — named for her sister, Azad Lotus Blythe Ford, born with a third-eye birthmark, given a Sanskrit name by hippie parents who believed in signs, described by Frazey as the wildest human she has ever known. The song is about commune childhood, about the love between siblings who came through an intense situation together. There is beauty in this world / So hold on any way that you know how. Joy and survival held in the same sentence, which is what her family had always done, which is what her mother's voice had always done, which is what her voice continues to do.

Teenie Hodges is gone now. When the Indian Ocean deluxe edition was prepared in 2024 — a vinyl reissue with three previously unreleased covers from the original Memphis sessions — Ford described feeling his ghost on her shoulder, saying: You gotta put these tracks out. She honored him by including a version of Otis Redding's "Happy Song," which Teenie had loved, and of Van Morrison's "Crazy Love," and of Ann Peebles' "Trouble, Heartaches & Sadness" — recorded in Vancouver with Charles Hodges laying his organ part down remotely from Memphis, the continent between them, the lineage unbroken.

This is conduction. The energy does not know it is traveling. It only knows contact, and what passes through.
