MACHA GHARIBIAN
Seven Prompts — Shape the Writing With Sweetness and More

PROMPT 1 — THE MUSICAL BLEND
Noir, Chanson, Diva, Waltz: What Macha Gharibian Carries

There is a specific frequency that Armenian music and French noir share, and Macha Gharibian lives inside it the way water lives inside limestone — not on the surface but constitutive, structural, the thing that shapes the cavern from within. Her father Dan Gharibian spent forty-five years playing tsigane and Eastern European music in the group Bratsch, and she followed him on those tours as a child, hearing rembetiko and Romanian dances and Russian folk from the back of a car. This is not biography. This is the formation of a nervous system. By the time she touched jazz it was already too late — her body already knew a different kind of dark.

The noir thread in her practice is not borrowed from French cinema. It is older than that. The Armenian Church modes she absorbed through classical training — drawn not to Debussy and Ravel but to Bartók, Brahms, Rachmaninoff, the Eastern European composers where folk memory and harmonic sophistication press against each other — these modes carry darkness the way geological strata carry time. Compressed. Slow. Specific to a particular pressure. When she plays the piano in her atmospheric, delicate way, the noir arrives as weather rather than decoration. You feel the temperature drop before you understand why.

The sweet pop chanson element is present in her vocal lineage. She sings in five languages — English, Armenian, French, Portuguese, Arabic — and in each one the phrasing carries what the great chanson tradition demands: the word that is both the thing and the texture of the thing simultaneously. When she composed "Kele Kele" or "La Douceur" on her debut album Mars, the titles already announced the methodology. Sweetness. The soft thing. The gentle approach that is also the most precise approach.

The diva thread runs through her contralto voice — volcanic, the critics say, in the Nina Simone and Jeanne Lee lineage. But the eruption is controlled, calibrated, a geological event rather than a performance. She does not use the voice to demonstrate. She uses it to transmit. The distinction is everything. When she sings ancestral Armenian songs — material carrying a hundred years of genocide, exile, and diaspora — she does not perform grief. She becomes the room where grief can finally happen correctly.

The waltz enters through the European folk structures her father carried: the three-beat sway of Romani music, the circular insistence of Armenian dance rhythms, the pull-and-release that makes traditional music of this region feel both ancient and perpetually contemporary. Her piano playing — percussive in the Bartók mode, atmospheric in the jazz mode — creates a hybrid rhythm that is always moving in more than one direction at once. You cannot quite tap your foot to it. But your body finds its own response. The response is older than consciousness.

PROMPT 2 — FLUID AND RHYTHMIC ESSENCE
The Current Beneath the Current

What runs through Macha Gharibian's practice when you strip it to the waterway underneath: it is the principle of inheritance as transformation. Not the reproduction of what was received but the pressing of it through a different medium until it emerges changed and yet — to those who knew the original — completely recognizable. Her father sang in Armenian, Russian, Greek, Romani. She sings in Armenian, French, English, Portuguese, Arabic. The number of languages has expanded. The principle is identical. Language as music, not communication. The voice as the instrument that carries what words cannot fully contain.

The rhythmic essence moves in three simultaneous currents. The first is ancestral: the Armenian Church modes and Eastern European folk rhythms absorbed in childhood, the rembetiko and Serbian dances from Dan Gharibian's car cassettes, the Greek blues — which is itself a music of diaspora, a music of people displaced and singing about displacement in the port cities where they landed. This ancestral current does not appear literally in Gharibian's jazz. It appears as a modal logic, a preference for harmonic ambiguity, a willingness to stay in the minor key without resolving it into either hope or defeat. Suspended. Like diaspora itself.

The second current is improvisational: the revelation of New York in 2005, the School for Improvisational Music, teachers Jason Moran and Ravi Coltrane and Craig Taborn teaching her that freedom is not the absence of structure but the presence of listening. She arrived at that school already classical — École Normale de Musique, Bartók in the fingers, technique precise and complete. What New York gave her was the understanding that virtuosity is only interesting when it is in service of something it cannot fully control. The improvisation current runs fast and cold beneath the other two, the place where the actual decisions happen, where the piece becomes itself rather than what she planned.

The third current is theatrical: twenty years of composing for Simon Abkarian's stage productions, sitting at the piano during live performances of Hélène après la chute, the melancholy of notes played on stage weaving words and silences in real time. Theater taught her that music is always happening in relation to something else — a body moving, a voice speaking, a pause being held. The theatrical current gives her music its unusual quality of patient attention. It waits. It knows how to wait. It knows that the silence between notes is where the feeling accumulates before it can be expressed.

The fluid and rhythmic essence is this: she carries three kinds of time simultaneously. The ancestral time of Armenian history — measured in centuries, in genocide and survival and diaspora. The improvisational time of jazz — measured in moments, in the gap between what the musician plays and what the music asks for next. And the theatrical time of live performance — measured in the audience's breath, in the held moment before the release. All three operating at once, in the same piece, in the same phrase, in the same note held just slightly longer than expected.

PROMPT 3 — THE MAMMALIAN BODY
What This Music Does to the Body That Has a Body

Macha Gharibian's music enters through the cochlea like most music does, but what it does once inside is specific. The Armenian modes she works in — scales that predate Western equal temperament, intervals that fall between the notes of a standard piano — create a micro-tonal ambiguity in the auditory cortex. The brain cannot fully categorize what it hears. It stays alert, leaning forward, processing continuously rather than settling into recognition. This is productive disorientation. The kind that happens when you hear something that is almost familiar and cannot name why. The hearing opens wider to catch what keeps moving past.

Proprioception — the body's knowledge of its own position — is engaged by the rhythmic complexity she inherits from her Romani and Eastern European foundations. These are not 4/4 rhythms. They are additive meters, rhythms built from unequal groups of beats, the 7/8 and 5/4 structures that Bartók notated from Bulgarian folk songs, that Dan Gharibian played in Bratsch for forty-five years. The body that grew up with Western pop rhythms has no automatic motor response to these meters. It has to find its own solution. The finding is felt in the hips, the shoulders, the slight swaying that begins without conscious decision. This is the proprioceptive system searching for a pattern to inhabit. When it finds one — and it does, eventually — the arrival is felt as pleasure disproportionate to its cause.

Interoception — the sense of the body's interior states, hunger, heart rate, emotional shifts — is engaged most directly by her contralto voice. The lower register of a trained contralto produces frequencies that the human auditory system processes as physical presence. Not a voice in a speaker but a body in a room. The vagus nerve, which runs from the brainstem through the chest and into the abdomen, responds to vocal frequencies in the lower register as it responds to the presence of another living being nearby. The polyvagal system shifts: threat detection recedes, social engagement activates, the breathing slows. You are safe. Someone is here. The body believes this before the mind can evaluate the claim.

The sense of agency — the feeling that one's responses are one's own — is restored by the specific quality of her piano touch, which critics consistently describe as simultaneously delicate and volcanic. The contrast creates space for the listener's own emotional response to exist. A piano that is only delicate gives the listener nowhere to put their intensity. A piano that is only powerful overwhelms the listener's own signal. Gharibian's piano does both, which means the listener's nervous system can find its own place in the dynamic range. You are not told how to feel. You are given a room with the right acoustics and left to feel what is already yours.

Chronoception — time perception — is altered by her practice of theatrical timing, the long waits she has learned from two decades of composing for live performance. Her music creates temporal dilation not through tempo but through density of attention. Five minutes contains more than five minutes. There is more room in it than there usually is.

PROMPT 4 — MENTAL HEALTH AND ALL SENSES
What This Practice Does for All Mammalian Beings

The specific mental health contribution of Macha Gharibian's practice — to be precise about it — is the offering of a model for how to carry inherited trauma without being destroyed by it. This is not a therapeutic claim. It is an aesthetic one. She is the third generation of Armenian Genocide survivors. The genocide of 1915 is not her personal memory. It is in her bones the way geology is in rock: not experienced but structurally determining. Her album Trans Extended was made for the 2015 centennial commemoration of that genocide. It tells the history of migration and exile through jazz, through Armenian folk material, through the expanded ensemble of eight musicians playing music that is neither documentary nor decoration but something else entirely — a room where the historical feeling can finally happen correctly for people who inherited it without being given a language for it.

For the mammalian body — all of them, not only human ones — the benefit is vibrational. Her piano at its most atmospheric creates what the physics of acoustics calls sympathetic resonance: the body of the listener begins to vibrate at the frequencies the piano produces, not because the listener chose to vibrate but because physics requires it. Dogs in rooms where this music plays settle faster. Not because they understand Armenian exile. Because the frequencies are safe. The polyvagal system reads the acoustic environment as non-threatening and releases what it was holding.

Smell and taste are activated through the associative memory pathways that music uniquely triggers. The rembetiko her father played in the car. The Romanian dances. The Russian folk songs sung in a language she did not speak but absorbed as pure music. These sounds carry olfactory and gustatory associations — the food of the places they came from, the smells of the cars and the venues and the backstages of forty-five years of touring. When Gharibian plays material in those modes, the body of the listener with similar memories activates those pathways involuntarily. And the body of the listener without those specific memories activates equivalent ones — some moment of childhood music, of sound that meant safety or beauty or the presence of someone who loved you. The specific content differs. The mechanism is the same.

The sense of body ownership — the knowledge that this body is mine, that I inhabit it — is affirmed by music that takes the body seriously as the primary site of reception. Gharibian's practice, rooted in both classical discipline and free improvisation, demands full presence. It does not allow half-attention. The complexity of the rhythms, the unexpectedness of the harmonic choices, the dynamic range from whisper to volcanic — these keep the nervous system engaged at a level that requires the whole body to participate. You cannot listen to this music from a distance. You have to be inside it. And to be inside it, you have to be inside yourself.

For humans specifically: the multilingual dimension of her practice offers something rare. She sings in five languages without fully belonging to any single one of them. This is the experience of diaspora stated as aesthetic principle. For listeners who similarly belong to multiple places and fully to none — which is an increasing number of people in a world of migration and displacement — her practice confirms that this condition is not a deficit. It is a form of richness. The multiple languages are not a failure to choose. They are the complete truth of a life that cannot be contained in one tongue.

PROMPT 5 — THE CAPTIVATING SYNTHESIS
Extracted from the Preceding Four: What Persists

She carries three kinds of time simultaneously. This is the sentence that survives all four previous writings and belongs at the center of any attempt to understand Macha Gharibian's practice. Ancestral time, improvisational time, theatrical time — all three operating at once, in the same piece, in the same held note. This is not a technique. It is a condition of being. The daughter who followed her father on tour for decades, absorbing the music of six or seven cultures before she could name them, has no choice but to carry all of that time simultaneously. The jazz she makes is the form that can hold all of it without requiring her to choose which inheritance is most legitimate.

The captivating detail that will not leave: Dan Gharibian singing in Armenian, Russian, Greek, Romani from the car. Languages as music, not communication. Macha storing this as a nervous system rather than as a lesson. Twenty years later in New York, discovering at the School for Improvisational Music that the voice can move through structure without being limited by it — she already knew this. She learned it in the back of her father's car, age five, hearing a man sing in four languages he may not have fully spoken, singing them anyway because the sound was the point.

The captivating fragment from the mammalian body analysis: you cannot listen to this music from a distance. You have to be inside it. And to be inside it, you have to be inside yourself. This is the standard the practice sets and meets. The complexity of the rhythms, the dynamic range from whisper to volcanic, the unexpectedness of the harmonic choices — these keep the nervous system too engaged for half-attention. This is not difficulty for its own sake. It is the demand of the material. The ancestral time, the improvisational time, the theatrical time — they require a listener who is fully present to receive them. They do not simplify themselves for an audience that wants to listen from the couch of mild attention.

The most captivating fragment from the mental health writing: she is the third generation of Armenian Genocide survivors making the ancestral memory contemporary. This is the Wonderful Garbage principle at historical scale. The material of the genocide — the exile, the migration, the déracinement, the century of carrying something that cannot be set down — is not discarded or denied. It is pressed through jazz, through Armenian Church modes, through the New York improvisation training, through Bartók and Brahms and the Bratsch cassettes. It emerges transformed. Still recognizable to those who know the original weight of it. Unrecognizable in form. Beautiful in the specific way that things are beautiful when they have survived being destroyed and kept going anyway.

PROMPT 6 — DEEP IMAGERY / MUSICOLOGICAL PRECISION
The Thread from Hand to Voice: On Transcription and Transmission

There is a specific musicological event that occurs when a classical pianist moves into jazz: the technique that was built for the reproduction of a fixed score must be unlearned at the level of intention while remaining in the hands. The fingers still carry the Bartók fingering, the Rachmaninoff reach, the Brahms weight distribution — these do not disappear. But the musical decision-making shifts from interpretation (what did the composer intend) to creation (what does the moment require). This shift cannot be taught directly. It has to be discovered, usually through years of playing with people who already know how to make that shift, whose bodies demonstrate the possibility.

What Macha Gharibian's hands carry is unusual in the jazz world: a classical technique rooted not in the French Impressionist tradition (the more common foundation for French pianists moving into jazz) but in the Eastern European percussive tradition. Bartók's piano writing requires rhythmic precision that is almost drumlike, attacks rather than caresses, the piano as a percussion instrument that also happens to have pitch. This gives Gharibian's jazz piano a specific physicality — the atmospheric passages are atmospheric but the rhythmic passages have a weight and exactness that is different from, say, the floating impressionism of a pianist formed on Ravel. Her hands know how to hit.

The vocal technique she has developed operates across what voice science calls multiple phonation types simultaneously. Her contralto's lower register — the frequency range of the volcanic — is produced with a high degree of laryngeal engagement, a pressed phonation that creates the sensation of emotional intensity even in quieter dynamic passages. But she layers over this a breath-mixed quality in the upper range that reads as intimacy, as confession, as the voice coming closer. The combination — pressed and breathy in the same phrase — is what Nina Simone understood and what the critics mean when they place Gharibian in that lineage. The voice is simultaneously forthcoming and contained. It offers itself fully while remaining fully itself.

The multilingual dimension has specific musicological consequences. Each language she sings in has its own phonemic inventory, its own pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables, its own relationship between vowel length and meaning. Armenian stresses the final syllable; French stresses the final syllable of a phrase rather than individual words; Portuguese has nasal vowels that create a particular resonance in the sinus cavities; Arabic has pharyngeal consonants that engage the throat in ways that alter the timbre of adjacent vowels. When Gharibian moves between languages, her voice is not simply changing lexical content. It is changing its physical production, its resonance chambers, its relationship to breath. The result is a timbre that shifts across the span of an album — or a song — in ways that are felt before they are understood. The body tracks the change. The mind names it later, if at all.

The connection from handwritten transcription to voice is this: she composes by improvisation first, notation after. The musical idea begins in the hands and the throat, in the real-time listening of the jazz practice. The score — if it exists at all, and for many of her compositions it exists only as a lead sheet, a melody line and chord symbols — is the trace left by a living event, not the instruction for one. When she performs, she is returning to the site of the original improvisation and listening again for what the music wants to do this time, in this room, with these people. The score is the fossil. The performance is the creature it came from, still alive.

PROMPT 7 — THE SPECIAL ESSENCE
What Persists: A Second Writing

In Paris, in a car, a man sang in four languages to a small girl who did not know what the words meant. She stored the frequencies. She stored the quality of attention a voice pays to a melody when it loves the melody more than it needs to be understood. She stored the particular darkness of Armenian music — not the theatrical darkness of opera or the postured darkness of noir, but the darkness of a people who have been carrying something very heavy for a very long time and have learned to make it beautiful because the alternative is to stop moving.

She carried this through the École Normale de Musique, through Bartók and Brahms and the Eastern European percussive tradition. She carried it to New York at twenty-five and left it in a Williamsburg loft while she learned to improvise — not to abandon the classical technique but to discover what it was for. What it was for was this: to give the body enough discipline that the discipline becomes invisible, that the hand moves without thinking so that the mind can think about something more important than where to put the fingers.

She carried it back to Paris, to Simon Abkarian's theater, to the stage of Théâtre National de Chaillot at twenty-three composing music for Shakespeare, to twenty years of being the pianist who is visible in the theater, the musician who is present, whose notes weave words and silences in real time rather than from the safety of a pre-recorded score. She learned what theater teaches and jazz also teaches and Armenian music has always known: that the music is not separate from the room it is made in, from the people who are breathing while it happens, from the specific night and the specific weight of what everyone in the room is carrying when they sit down.

This is what persists in Macha Gharibian's practice across four albums and twenty years: the conviction that music is not the feeling. Music is the place where the feeling can finally happen correctly. The feeling was already there — in the Armenian exile, in the Bratsch car cassettes, in the New York improvisation, in the theater silences. She builds the room. She tunes it precisely. She stands at the piano, which is also a percussion instrument, which is also the body of her father's music pressed through her own fingers, and she waits for what the room requires.

Joy Ascension won the Victoire du Jazz for Révélation in 2020. Phenomenal Women in 2025 expanded the ensemble, added three vocalists, brought Maya Angelou's title into dialogue with Armenian Church modes and New York jazz training and Bartók's rhythmic precision. The album is all of it at once. The ancestral time, the improvisational time, the theatrical time. The delicate hand and the volcanic voice. The five languages that are also one language, the language of a person who belongs to many places and fully to none and has made that condition into the whole point.

Phenomenal Women. The title is precise. Not triumphant — precise. A woman who has been phenomenal is a woman who has carried the weight of what she inherited, pressed it through every form available to her, and refused to simplify it. She is the room where the ancestral feeling can finally happen correctly. The feeling has been waiting a hundred years for someone to build it right.

MAUD LÜBECK INFLUENCE APPLIED
La chanson n'est pas l'émotion

La chanson n'est pas l'émotion — elle est le lieu où l'émotion peut enfin se produire correctement. The writing is not the feeling. The writing is the place where the feeling can finally happen correctly.

After all seven prompts, this is what Maud Lübeck confirms about Macha Gharibian: the darkness is earned. Every Armenian mode, every theatrical pause, every multilingual phrase — these are not decorative. They are specific. They have the specific detail that proves they happened. The car. The four languages. The man who sang in tongues he may not have spoken because the sound was the point. The one-meter precision that Maud Lübeck demands — the red felt-tip marker on the notebook cover, the cassette recorder on the piano — is present here as the Bratsch car cassettes, as the Armenian genocide centennial as compositional catalyst, as the twenty-three-year-old composing for Shakespeare at Théâtre National de Chaillot. Not approximately. Exactly.

Run the three-layer test. Layer one: is this coming from something lived, something that cost something to excavate? Yes. Third-generation genocide survivor making ancestral memory contemporary. Classical training abandoned for jazz freedom. Twenty years of composing for live theater where the piano is visible, where the musician cannot hide behind the score. This cost something. The writing that addresses it should honor the cost.

Layer two: strip back the production. Remove the expanded ensemble of Phenomenal Women, the three vocalists, the electric bass, the Swiss and Swedish vocal blend. Does the core remain? It does. It is the trio format — piano, bass, drums — that reveals the bones. The trio that won the Victoire du Jazz with Joy Ascension. The bones are Bartók and rembetiko and New York improvisation and Armenian exile. They survive the stripping. They are more themselves without the production than with it.

Layer three: does it do something to the body? Does something in the chest change? Does the jaw release slightly? Does the reading produce the physiological event of recognition — the interoceptive signal of a feeling finally named? Yes. Because this practice is about exactly that. The body of the listener with inherited trauma — which is most bodies, since most histories involve something that was carried forward into people who did not choose to carry it — recognizes itself in music that takes inherited darkness seriously and refuses to resolve it into false comfort. The jaw releases not because the tension is gone but because the tension has been named. Named things can be carried differently than unnamed things. The room exists. The acoustics are right. You are not alone in what you carry.

This is the place Macha Gharibian builds. The room where the ancestral feeling can finally happen correctly. It cost something to build it. A hundred years at minimum. The room exists. Come in.
