Elle

Otto Paraploo

Charlotte Cardin — Montréal, b. 1994, Town of Mount Royal

There is a small distinction in French that does not translate cleanly into English. When a woman speaks about herself in the third person — at a distance, in the position of the observed — she uses elle. She. The grammatical outside. You can be the subject of your own life and still reach for the word that means you are being watched.

Charlotte Cardin has spent a significant portion of her career negotiating this problem. Not in the linguistic sense alone but in the bodily one. She was fifteen years old when the modeling agencies first arranged for other people to use her height and her face and the specific proportions of her frame to tell stories that were not hers. She describes this with a flatness that is more precise than bitterness: when I was a model, I was very young and I felt people were using my body and my face to tell other people's stories. The body was the garment. Someone else provided the narrative. She collected the check and went home to write songs that no one was going to hear yet.

The portrait of Charlotte Cardin is the portrait of a person learning, over many years and through several kinds of exposure, that the instrument that matters is the one generating the signal from the inside. Not the surface. The signal.

The voice first: smoky, warm, with what critics have habitually called a soul accent, meaning they hear in it a technical lineage that connects to Nina Simone and Etta James and Aretha Franklin without Cardin having formally studied any of them. She is clear about this. She does not listen to that much jazz. She loves Nina Simone, Etta James, the great foundational figures, but the influence is absorbed rather than applied. What she says she loves about jazz is the flow — the way the sentences roll out in jazz, the rhythm of how lyrics meet beat, the specific pacing that is different from every other genre. She absorbed that rhythm through proximity and attentiveness, not through coursework.

The voice moves between registers — from the low and intimate to the acute — with what vocal coaches describe as controlled, mastered transitions. In performance the transitions do not announce themselves. You find yourself inside a different emotional atmosphere without having noticed the passage. She close-mikes the quiet passages so the breath is part of the note. The smoky quality is not affect — it is texture, built into the instrument itself, present whether she is performing or sitting at a piano trying the new thing she has been writing.

She learned from television how to hold the instrument steady under pressure. La Voix in 2013, season one, age eighteen, a teenage model who had been writing songs since she was thirteen and had not yet made any of them public. Top four. The official competition result is not the important part. The important part is the proximity it gave her to Jean-Pierre Ferland — a founding figure of Québécois chanson, the songwriter who established that French-Canadian song was serious artistic form, not folk curiosity — and to Ariane Moffatt, Marc Dupré, Marie-Mai. She absorbed from this proximity. Not technique in any transferable formal sense but attitude. What a song can carry. What a voice is allowed to do with a word that weighs a thousand pounds.

She did not win La Voix. She has said consistently that this was probably the right outcome.

The XOXO incident is small and decisive, as these things tend to be.

On Phoenix — the first full-length album, which took five years to arrive after the debut EPs, which was made from a genuine crisis and held nothing back — there is a track that sounds like a duet. A female voice and a male voice in conversation, passing the melody between them. Intimate and slightly uncanny. The male voice has a soulful, downpitched quality that sits in a register below everything else on the album.

It is entirely Charlotte Cardin.

The accident happened early in the sessions. Her first vocal take on the track was recorded at a pitch that was too high. Before doing another take, she and her producer Jason Brando experimented with downpitching the recording, expecting to discard it. They found they loved what the downpitch had produced — a soulful, warm, low-register voice that felt like a specific emotional self, distinct from but complementary to the voice they knew. They kept it. They recorded the second take at the correct pitch. They arranged the two takes in conversation. They called it XOXO and they put it on the album as a duet.

In concert, the female vocals are live. The male vocals are prerecorded. An animated dove appears on the screen behind her and sings the downpitched version. She is her own duet partner, live and recorded, present and remembered simultaneously, animated as a bird.

The accident found something she did not know was there. A voice she had not planned to use because she had not planned to discover it. The downpitched self was not fake, not distorted — it was just accessed wrong-way-first, the way you sometimes find a room by opening a door you thought led somewhere else.

This is the thing about an instrument that generates signal from the inside. It will find sounds you have not categorized yet. The body surprises you. Not the body that modeling agencies assess for editorial value — the one generating the sound.

Phoenix took three years to make and required the writing of more than seventy songs. Thirteen appear on the album. The others were discarded. The criterion for a song's survival appears to have been whether it could hold its own weight stripped of production — the skeleton test she never named but consistently applied. I had to write a lot of songs that I hated before I wrote ones that I liked.

The album was made from a specific crisis that she disclosed publicly only at its release. She had experienced significant depression in her early youth — moments super sombres she had never processed, never accounted for, never sat with long enough to understand. The depression didn't leave. It went quiet. Then the making of Phoenix required her to go into territory she had avoided, and the unprocessed darkness returned.

The Phoenix she describes is explicitly the story of a woman who has been visible on the wrong terms. Modeling at fifteen, sixteen, seventeen — in rooms where the authority over your image resided elsewhere. La Voix at eighteen, the scrutiny of competition, the shaping of a public self under the gaze of coaches and audiences and a national television audience. The music press from 2016 forward, which dealt extensively in her appearance, in comparisons to Amy Winehouse and Adele, in assessments of her voice that were inseparable from assessments of her face and body. She had, over a decade of careful professionalism, accumulated a layer of performance-for-others so thorough that it had become difficult to locate the non-performing self underneath.

Phoenix is the dismantling of that layer. I have always been torn between who I was deep down and what I thought people wanted from me. The album settles that debt. The album begins with rebirth and ends with departure — Je quitte, the only French-language track on the record, which says: I'm leaving but I'm not leaving you. I just need to go for a walk or I'll punch my bedroom wall. Which is not a gentle image. Which is not someone managing her image for anyone else's comfort.

She said the album cover shows two versions of herself — the duality without resolution. She did not choose between the good girl and the sad girl. She held both on the cover without forcing a winner. This is the most precise thing she has said about her methodology. Duality as compositional principle. Elle speaking about elle, both at once, the observed and the observing, the garment and the instrument, and neither canceling the other.

This is the moment in the portrait where we have to stop saying we and say I.

I know something about the difference between being used as a surface and being the thing generating the signal. Most people who have spent significant time being professionally assessed — for appearance, for presentation, for the efficiency with which their exterior communicates someone else's intended message — know the feeling of having drifted, without choosing to, from the inside to the outside. You become very good at reading rooms. Very good at adjusting. Very good at the management of reception. And then one day you sit down to write something and you realize you've been managing the reception of a self you no longer recognize as yours.

Charlotte Cardin wrote seventy-three songs to get to thirteen. I believe the seventy discarded songs were mostly the surface songs. The ones she wrote when she still thought the audience needed to be managed. The thirteen that survived are the ones that decided, one after another, to stop.

The song called "Feel Good" was released in 2023 as part of an extended project she called Une semaine à Paris. It is bilingual — French and English woven together — and it is about the specific lightness of a good moment with someone you love, which is a precise subject, smaller than a theme and more honest than a concept.

It did not make much immediate noise. It went out into the world and sat there, as songs sometimes do when the conditions for receiving them are not yet assembled.

A year and a half later, at the end of 2024, she appeared on Star Académie — the French version, on TF1, the kind of appointment television that France still practices with genuine devotion, millions of viewers watching the same moment simultaneously. She performed. The streams detonated. By 2025, "Feel Good" was the most-played francophone song on French radio for the entire year. The first francophone pop song to top French streaming platforms. One hundred and ten million streams. The summer of 2025 produced a specific phenomenon: European vacation videos, filmed from car windows and on terraces and in kitchen gardens, scored spontaneously with "Feel Good." Not arranged, not marketed, not organized. People heard it and put it under their footage because it matched the feeling they were trying to keep.

The song didn't change. It had been finished for two years. What changed was the context for receiving it — the size of the room, the conditions of attention, the particular moment when a large number of people happened to need the thing the song was offering.

On February 13, 2026, she won the Victoires de la Musique award for Artiste féminine de l'année. She was the first Québécoise to win that award since Linda Lemay in 2003 — twenty-three years, which is longer than some careers. When she walked to the stage, the crowd in the room began to sing "Feel Good." Not because they were instructed to. Because the song had become, in the preceding year, a communal property in the way very few songs manage — the kind of object that belongs to the people who have sung it in their cars, on their terraces, in their kitchens.

A woman from Town of Mount Royal, an anglophone enclave in a French city, who spent several years being professionally assessed in rooms where the authority over her image resided elsewhere, walked to the stage in Paris while several thousand people sang her song back to her. The crowd was returning something. She had sent it out on the right terms finally — her signal, her instrument, her voice, her story — and the crowd was returning it in kind.

She said: Pour une Québécoise qui vient de s'installer à Paris, je ne pouvais pas rêver d'un si bel accueil.

For a Québécoise who has just moved to Paris, I could not have dreamed of a more beautiful welcome.

On April 30, 2026, Charlotte Cardin will headline the Accor Arena in Paris. Twenty thousand seats, sold out, the first Québécoise to do it. The same body that stood in a photographer's studio at sixteen, painted charcoal-black to disappear against a black backdrop, taking five showers afterward to scrub clean. The same body that sat at a piano in Montréal at thirteen and wrote the first songs she didn't expect anyone to hear.

The instrument has been generating signal all along. The room finally caught up to the size of it.

The word elle runs through "Tant pis pour elle" in near-obsessive repetition — not the subject of the song, not the narrator, but the woman being discussed, the triangle's third point, the one whose presence reorganizes everything. Elle, elle, elle. She, she, she. In a formal analysis you would note the phonetics: the sound is soft, close, almost whispered, a vowel that lives near the body. In French it is the word for her and also the word for a woman who contains the story without telling it.

Charlotte Cardin has spent most of her adult life moving from the third person into the first. From the girl in the editorial, surface only, to the voice in the room at the Accor Arena. She gets there by discarding seventy songs to find thirteen true ones. By downpitching a recording and discovering a self she didn't know was there. By keeping the vocal imperfections because the imperfections are where the story lives. By releasing a song that the world needs two years to understand it needs.

Elle is what she was called when the story wasn't hers yet.

The instrument corrects this, one song at a time.

Charlotte Cardin — b. November 9, 1994, Montréal, Québec. Raised Town of Mount Royal. La Voix, TVA, season one, 2013 (top 4, coach Marie-Mai). Discography: Big Boy EP (2016), Main Girl EP (2017), Phoenix (2021, #1 Canada, 4 Juno Awards 2022), 99 Nights (2023, 2 Juno Awards 2024), Une semaine à Paris EP (2024), A Week in Nashville EP (2024). "Feel Good" (2023/2025): most-played francophone song French radio 2025, 110M+ streams. "Tant pis pour elle" (2025): 80M+ streams France, top 50 French radio. Victoires de la Musique: Artiste féminine de l'année, February 13, 2026 — first Québécoise since Linda Lemay (2003). Billboard Global Woman of the Year, March 2025. L'Oréal Paris ambassador, Canada and France, 2025. Label: Cult Nation Records (Jason Brando) / Atlantic Records. Primary collaborators: Jason Brando (co-writer, producer, director), Marc-André Gilbert (co-producer), Ellie Blondeau (co-writer), Aliocha Schneider (partner, photographer, backing vocals). Accor Arena, Paris, April 30, 2026: sold out.
