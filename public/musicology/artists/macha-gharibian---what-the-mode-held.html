A Musicological Witness Portrait — Macha Gharibian
Paraploo

Before equal temperament divided the Western octave into twelve identical semitones — a decision made gradually through the seventeenth century, chosen for convenience rather than truth — there were intervals that fell between. The equal-tempered system slightly mistunes every interval except the octave so that all keys are playable on a single instrument. The West chose resolution over accuracy: unlimited key change over pure interval. What this decision required was the suppression of the overtone series, the physical ratios of vibrating strings, the intervals that nature produces when a string divides itself. Modal systems that predate this choice carry those natural intervals forward: the Armenian Church modes, the Turkish maqam, the Greek Byzantine scales, the Romani modes that traveled across the southern edge of Europe and never settled into any single nation's tuning. When the auditory cortex encounters these intervals, it cannot file them using the phonemic and harmonic templates built from a lifetime of Western music. It stays alert. It leans forward. It keeps processing. This is not error. This is the ear returned to a state of genuine attention — the productive disorientation of a system that cannot recognize and move on, that must keep listening, that has encountered something the archive does not contain.

Macha Gharibian's father sang in four languages. Armenian, Russian, Greek, Romani. In the car, on cassette, on tour with Bratsch across forty-five years of playing tsigane and Eastern European music, he sang because the sound was the point and not the meaning. A small girl in the back seat stored the frequencies. She stored the quality of attention a voice pays to a melody when it loves the melody more than it needs to be understood. She stored the specific darkness of Armenian music — not performed darkness, not borrowed darkness, but the darkness of a people who have been carrying something very heavy for a very long time and have learned to make it beautiful because the alternative is to stop moving entirely. The car was the first room. The cassette was the archive. The formation happened before analysis was available, before the body knew it was being formed, before she could have named what was being laid down in her nervous system like geological sediment — layer by layer, under pressure, in the dark.

She was drawn to Bartók, Brahms, Rachmaninoff. Not to Debussy and Ravel, whom her peers at the École Normale de Musique de Paris preferred — the French Impressionists who float on the surface of Western harmony, who use color and atmosphere to suggest depths they rarely descend into. Debussy is beautiful. Bartók went into the field. He traveled to Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary, and notated what he found there: additive meters, rhythms built from unequal groupings of beats, the 7/8 and 5/4 and 9/8 structures that Bulgarian village musicians played by feel rather than by count. The body that grew up with Western 4/4 has no automatic motor response to these meters. It cannot tap its foot and call it solved. The proprioceptive system — muscle spindles and joint receptors reporting position and movement — begins searching for a pattern to inhabit, turning over different solutions, the way the tongue works at something caught between the teeth. The hips shift slightly. The shoulders try something. And when the body finds its solution — when it discovers the particular way to move inside an asymmetrical rhythm — the arrival is pleasure disproportionate to its apparent cause. The body has solved a problem it didn't know it was working on. That disproportionate pleasure is the signature of a hidden cognitive event surfacing as sensation.

She was drawn to these composers before she had words for why. The preference was structural, not aesthetic. The Armenian Church modes of her father's cassettes had already tuned her nervous system to intervals that fell between the equal-tempered notes, and Bartók's folk rhythms spoke to a body already inclined toward the asymmetrical, the unresolved, the meter that wouldn't fit into the standard counting. The car had already done its work. The École Normale gave her the technique to serve what the car had placed inside her.

At twenty-three — before she had graduated, before New York, before anything she would be recognized for — Simon Abkarian invited her to compose music for his production of Titus Andronicus at the Théâtre National de Chaillot. Abkarian: French-Armenian actor and director, veteran of Ariane Mnouchkine's Théâtre du Soleil, one of the significant figures in French-Armenian cultural life. He recognized something in her before she had finished becoming it. She plunged into the theater work and discovered her desire for composition and improvisation simultaneously — the writing and the not-knowing-what-comes-next as a single practice, inseparable, each requiring the other. Twenty years of that collaboration followed. It is still ongoing.

New York, 2005. She was twenty-five, the École Normale complete, the classical technique thorough and precise and — she had begun to understand — insufficient for what she actually needed to do. She flew to Williamsburg and enrolled in Ralph Alessi's School for Improvisational Music, and what she found there was not a method but a reversal. The teachers — Jason Moran, Ravi Coltrane, Craig Taborn, Uri Caine, Jim Black — were not teaching her how to play. They were teaching her to stop performing playing. The anterior insula and basal ganglia, the neural structures governing real-time motor sequencing, operate at speeds the frontal cortex cannot match. The improvising hand makes decisions faster than conscious evaluation can intervene. The frontal lobe's latency for deliberate choice runs two hundred to three hundred milliseconds; the motor system has already committed. What the listener hears in a genuine improvised take is not a demonstration of skill. It is the evidence of a mind at full speed, the thought arriving before the thinker could improve it or protect themselves from it. The mirror neuron system in the listener activates in recognition of a body working at its absolute limit. The recognition is felt as intimacy — not the intimacy of shared information but the intimacy of watching something real happen in real time, of being present for something that cannot be repeated.

Craig Taborn was among her teachers. Taborn works at the intersection of classical precision and electronic texture — prepared piano, extended technique, the instrument altered and opened. Jason Moran: multimedia, genre-defying, the piano as political and visual object as much as musical instrument. Ravi Coltrane: the spiritual lineage made contemporary, the modal logic his father developed carried forward by a son who had to find his own relationship to an impossibly large inheritance. She was in the right room. She was learning what inheritance looks like when it is transformed rather than reproduced.

She returned to Paris carrying the improvisation. The classical technique was now for something specific: to make the discipline invisible, so that the hand could move without thinking while the mind attended to what mattered more than fingering.

In 2015, Bratsch dissolved. Dan Gharibian and Bruno Girard announced the end of their forty-five-year collaboration — nous étions allés au bout de cette belle aventure, we had reached the end of this beautiful adventure. The group that had been her father's life, that had filled her childhood with rembetiko and Romanian dances from the back seat of a car, that had made the multilingual-singing-because-the-sound-is-the-point the water she swam in from birth — it was done.

The year after Bratsch dissolved, Macha released Trans Extended. The album had been building since 2015, when the Festival Fruits d'Arménie commissioned work commemorating the centennial of the Armenian Genocide. One hundred years since 1915. One hundred years since the systematic destruction of the Armenian people under the Ottoman Empire — the death marches into the Syrian desert, the massacres, the dispersal of survivors into diaspora across France, Lebanon, the United States, wherever they could reach. Her grandparents' generation. The weight she had been absorbing from her father's car cassettes without knowing that was what she was absorbing.

Trans Extended tells the history of migration, of exile. It does not tell it the way a documentary tells it — through information, through chronology, through the accumulation of documented facts. It tells it the way the Armenian Church modes tell it: through harmonic structures that refuse to resolve, through rhythms that carry the weight of asymmetry, through the specific darkness that is not performed but structural, laid down over generations in the nervous systems of the people who carry it. The album expanded the ensemble to an octet — saxophones, trombone, two drummers, the multilingual voice of Gharibian herself and her collaborators — and the expansion was necessary. Some weights require more people to carry.

She sings in five languages. Armenian, French, English, Portuguese, Arabic. In each one the phrasing carries what her father's singing taught before she could understand it: the word as both the thing and the texture of the thing simultaneously, the sound as the carrier of what meaning cannot fully contain. This is what the great chanson tradition demands and what Armenian music has always known and what rembetiko — Greek blues, the music of displacement sung in the port cities of Smyrna and Piraeus by people who had nowhere else to go — has always practiced. Language as music, not communication. The voice as the instrument that takes the body's knowledge and makes it transmissible to another body in another room who has never been to the place the song came from and has still, somehow, been there.

Her contralto voice sits in the bottom register of the female range — the fundamentals arriving at frequencies where the Pacinian corpuscles in bone and skin register vibration before the cochlea has named the pitch. Vibration receptors sensing low-frequency resonance, the body knowing something is in the room before the ear has identified what. The vagus nerve runs from the brainstem through the chest and into the abdomen; it responds to lower vocal frequencies the way it responds to the presence of another living being nearby, close, in the room with you. The polyvagal system shifts. Threat detection recedes. Social engagement activates. The breathing slows and deepens. Something changes in the chest before the mind has assembled a reason for the change. This is not sentiment. This is the body's fastest processing system operating at its normal speed, reporting on something real.

The critics describe her voice as volcanic. The eruption is controlled, geological — a geological event rather than a performance. She does not use the voice to demonstrate. She uses it to transmit. The distinction is everything. When she sings ancestral Armenian songs — material that has been carrying a hundred years of genocide and exile and diaspora through the bodies of everyone who has sung it since — she does not perform that weight. She becomes the room where the weight can finally be set down correctly. Not resolved. Set down. There is a difference between resolution and relief. Resolution pretends the problem is solved. Relief acknowledges the problem and offers the body a moment to breathe inside it.

The theater trained her for exactly this. Twenty years of sitting at the piano on stage, visible, present, her notes weaving words and silences in real time — not from behind a screen, not from a pre-recorded score, but there, in the room, in the specific night, with the specific weight that everyone present is carrying when they sit down. Theater taught her what jazz also teaches and Armenian music has always known: the music is always happening in relation to something else. A body moving. A pause being held. The breath taken before the difficult word. Music in relation to silence is not music interrupted. It is music revealed by what surrounds it.

There is a café near the end of the afternoon when the light is doing what it does in late February — thin and almost horizontal, arriving at an angle that makes everything briefly more specific, the grain in the wood of the table, the steam rising from the cup. The espresso machine has been running long enough that the smell is no longer coffee but something deeper — the retronasal signal rising from the soft palate, the olfactory system the only sense with direct limbic access, no thalamic relay, the scent arriving at the emotional processing structures before analysis can intervene. I was sitting with Joy Ascension, the 2020 trio album that won the Victoire du Jazz for Révélation, and the piano came through the speakers and landed somewhere in the chest before I had registered what it was. The Pacinian corpuscles registered first. Low-frequency overtones through the table surface, through the ceramic mug warm in both hands, through the sternum. The body knew the room had changed before the mind knew a song had begun.

Then the mode arrived. The interval that would not resolve — the scale step that fell between the categories my auditory cortex had built from decades of Western music — and the ear opened wider to catch what kept moving past the filing system, and something in the chest changed in the way chests change when something unnamed has finally been given a name. Not comfort. Not resolution. The specific relief of recognizing a weight you have been carrying without knowing what to call it. The jaw releases not because the tension is gone but because the tension has been precisely located. Named things can be carried differently than unnamed things. The room where the grief can happen correctly is not the room where the grief ends. It is the room where the grief stops being diffuse and becomes specific, and specific things can be held, and what can be held can be moved.

I don't have Armenian grandparents. I am not a third-generation genocide survivor building ancestral memory into contemporary music. But the mode does not require shared history to produce the physiological event of recognition. It requires only a body that has inherited something heavy and has not yet found the room with the right acoustics. Most bodies qualify. Most histories involve something carried forward into people who did not choose to carry it. The interoceptive insula — the cortical region processing the body's interior states, hunger, heart rate, the shifts that precede and produce emotion — receives the signal before the cognitive apparatus has assembled a justification. The body recognizes the room before the mind knows why.

The mode does not resolve. Armenian history has not resolved. The equal-tempered system chose convenience over truth. The car chose something else.

Phenomenal Women arrived in 2025 with an expanded ensemble: three vocalists from Switzerland and Sweden, electric bass, the quintet and beyond. Lea Maria Fries and Linda Oláh weaving harmonies that are not Western choral tradition and not Armenian Church music but something held between them — which is where all of Gharibian's practice lives, between things, in the productive space of the interval that cannot be filed. The album carries Maya Angelou's title and Armenian Church modes and Bartók's rhythmic precision and the New York improvisation and twenty years of theatrical patience and the specific darkness absorbed from a car cassette before the body had words for what it was receiving.

The title is precise, not triumphant. A phenomenal woman is not a woman who has transcended what she was given. She is a woman who has taken what she was given — the weight of it, the darkness of it, the specific pressure of a history that her body carries whether she chose to carry it or not — and pressed it through every form available to her until it emerged changed and yet, to those who knew the original, completely recognizable. The Bratsch cassette in the car and the Victoire du Jazz and Trans Extended's genocide centennial and the piano on stage in Abkarian's theater weaving words and silences in real time — all of it, at once, in the same room, the room she builds precisely so that the feeling that has been waiting can finally happen correctly.

The mode does not resolve. That is not the music's failure. That is the music's argument. The interval that refuses resolution because the history that generated it has not resolved — held open, both poles maintained, the auditory cortex still leaning forward, still processing, still unable to file it and move on. Still listening. Still in the productive disorientation that is the only honest response to what the equal-tempered system chose to suppress.

The feeling has been waiting a hundred years.

She builds the room.
