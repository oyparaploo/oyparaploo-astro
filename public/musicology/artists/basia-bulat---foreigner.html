Foreigner

Otto Paraploo

Portrait 47 — Basia Bulat
Musicological Witness Series | Otto Paraploo

Basia is a Polish name. The diminutive of Barbara. Barbara comes from the Greek barbaros — foreigner, stranger, the one whose speech sounds like noise to those who don't know how to hear it. She has been signing her work with this name since 2005. The displacement is structural. It's in the paperwork.

Her parents came from Poland. They landed in Etobicoke, a district of Toronto that sits at the western edge of the city where the apartment buildings thin out and there are more driveways. Her mother was a classical music teacher — piano and guitar, lessons given in the house — and her father loved disco polo, which is a genre of Polish dance music developed in the late 1980s, associated with weddings and rural celebration, universally known by educated Poles and universally disavowed by them, the music everyone has memorized and no one will admit to. He played a song called "Blondyneczka (Little Blondie)." She was embarrassed by this as a child. She didn't understand yet that embarrassment is a form of love that hasn't learned what it is.

The radio in the house was permanently tuned to an oldies station — Sam Cooke, Stax Records, soul and gospel traveling through the speakers of a Polish-immigrant household in suburban Ontario. She has said she didn't know the radio had more than one station until she was eleven or twelve. The oldies station was the peace treaty. The one frequency mother, father, and daughter could inhabit together.

She began piano at three. She sang in the school choir. She played upright bass in youth orchestra. She took English literature at the University of Western Ontario in London, Ontario — a degree in the language that was never her family's first language — and there a friend pushed her onstage to open for Julie Doiron, and something started. In 2006 she began a master's degree in English and then abandoned it to go to Montreal. She said she was going to make a record as "an audible memory" of her time in London. She was recording a document, not launching a career. Howard Bilerman heard her and contacted labels without being asked.

Bilerman's studio, Hotel2Tango, was where Silver Mt. Zion recorded and where Arcade Fire made Funeral. The punky noise-rock engineer and the Polish-Canadian girl with the autoharp recorded in analogue and made a debut record that sounded like something found rather than constructed. Rough Trade Records, whose history runs through The Smiths and The Raincoats and Portishead, released it in the spring of 2007. In Canada it came out through Hayden's Hardwood Records — the label run by the Canadian folk singer Hayden as a community project, which is its own kind of sentence about where she was standing.

The autoharp: a nineteenth-century parlor instrument, chord-based, strummed with one hand while chord bars are pressed with the other to damp unwanted strings. It is the instrument of Mother Maybelle Carter and the Appalachian tradition, of the 1960s folk revival, of particular American folk rooms. It is not a Polish instrument. It is not a classical instrument. It is associated with bluegrass front porches and campfire circles and a specific mode of American vernacular music that has nothing to do with Etobicoke. She picked it up and played it as if it had always been hers. Critics wrote headlines about her making the autoharp cool again. She found this funny. "Instruments can be cool or not cool now?"

She played piano and guitar and autoharp and dulcimer and charango and zither and ukulele and upright bass and organ and synthesizer. She could perform alone in large halls — just her, the instrument, the voice — and hold them. The voice: vibrato-laced, moving between whisper and power, described in reviews as reedy, smoky, silver, enormous, like the sound of something that has learned to be gentle in order not to break the room. One reviewer in 2007 wrote: "Her vocal has more depth and complexity to it than most of the other female singers around and this is what sets her apart." Eighteen years later the same sentence would still be accurate.

Her publishing company is called Ptak Music. Ptak is the Polish word for bird. This is not visible on any album cover. It is in the legal structure of the career — embedded in the paperwork, in the copyright notices and licensing agreements that accompany the songs into the world. The foreigner named her company after a Polish bird and did not call attention to it.

Each album is a document of a different kind of loss. The unnamed friend or loved one who became Tall Tall Shadow — she has kept that person's identity private for twelve years, rigorously, which is its own form of faithfulness. The relationship that ended and generated Good Advice — she drove alone from Montreal to Louisville with Stevie Nicks and Prince on the stereo, nine or ten hours, and recorded a breakup record in Kentucky. The father who died around the time of Are You In Love? — present in that record as a current rather than a subject, unnamed, mourned indirectly. The foreigner holds her grievances privately and releases them into music that refuses to give you all the details, because giving you all the details would make it too small. "I don't think it would resonate with anyone if the songs were all incredibly detailed stuff about my life. The goal is to do something that speaks to something a little bigger."

She was shortlisted for the Polaris Music Prize three times — 2008, 2014, 2016. She was nominated for five Juno Awards. She headlined Massey Hall in Toronto in 2014, which is the cathedral of Canadian popular music. In 2012 she was invited to perform for Leonard Cohen at his Glenn Gould Prize Gala. In November 2017, a year after Cohen's death, his son Adam organized a memorial concert at the Bell Centre in Montreal — 15,672 people, the orchestrated farewell his father had asked for if there was to be a public event, in his city. The lineup included Sting, Feist, Elvis Costello, k.d. lang, Lana Del Rey, Damien Rice, Ron Sexsmith. Basia Bulat was not on the original announced lineup. She was a surprise, a late addition.

She sang "Coming Back to You" with Adam Cohen — the first song Leonard's son had ever learned, his father's song, the hymn back to the man. Bulat barely touches the harmony. You can hear her choosing to be unobtrusive. Someone else's grief, and she stands at its edge.

Then Adam Cohen walked off, and she stayed.

She sang "Closing Time" alone — a rocking, spirited version — and got the audience to sing with her. 15,672 people in a Montreal arena singing along to close a memorial concert for the city's greatest poet. She was not supposed to be there. She held the room. This is the benediction function: the foreigner, the one not originally on the list, who ends up carrying the ceremony across its last threshold.

In 2022 she stopped moving for the first time in years. A new home in Montreal's Mile End neighborhood. A new daughter, born April 2021 — discovered during the recording of The Garden, a strings-album retrospective she was making with Owen Pallett and Zou Zou Robidoux and her husband Andrew Woods. She told her collaborators from inside the vocal booth, down a wire, mid-session. She was singing a song she'd written as a student in London, Ontario: we gave away our hearts before we knew what they were. She was singing it again, pregnant, and the song time-traveled inside her.

In the stillness of 2022 she started going down to the basement at 3am, after the children were asleep. She had heard that Leonard Cohen used to do his best writing at three or four in the morning with his Casio keyboard, before the kids woke up. She opened her MacBook. She started playing Dragon Warrior 4 on her Nintendo. She started making MIDI compositions that floated and gleamed, like hidden levels in a game. She wrote her lyrics in a Hayao Miyazaki notebook. She was finding the spirit world at the hour when the veil is thinnest.

The basement: she has always written in basements. Her mother's basement as a child, where she would go when she needed to enter a different state of mind. Her own basement now. She has said: "There's something about going underground — kind of going into this different state of mind. You kind of connect to your old self and the spirit world, once you allow yourself to just get out of your own head."

She found a 1979 Polish drum machine. She built Basia's Palace around it.

The album that emerged sounds like childhood photos heard through a synthesizer. Like the oldies station and the disco polo music and the Maryla Rodowicz records from her great uncle's collection — the "gauzy" Polish pop star who has been recording since 1967, whose LPs Bulat absorbed as a child without knowing she was absorbing them — all swimming together in a Montreal basement in the middle of the night.

"Disco Polo" is the song she had been threatening to make for years. It is named for the genre her father loved. It quotes "Blondyneczka (Little Blondie)" — his actual song, his specific taste. She spent her childhood embarrassed by this music and her father's complicated relationship with her, and now she is a mother with two daughters and her father is dead and the disco polo is the only portal she has left to the parts of him she didn't get to know. "This song became an ode to the stories, both funny and sad, that I'd overhear being told around the dining table by my family long after I was supposed to be asleep."

The music everyone in Poland knows all the words to and no one will admit to. She made it into a folk song. She put it inside a record about memory and inheritance and the thing that happens onstage at the end of every performance when the applause rises and the masks fall away and she has sung her secrets and shown her face. She called the album her palace. It is also the inside of her head.

She is working toward an album in Polish. The foreigner, arriving at the source language. The one whose name means stranger, finding her way back to the first house.

Portrait spine: Foreigner — the etymology of the name as biography; the Polish inheritance carried through embarrassment into love; the music that opens what could not be opened any other way.

Subjects: Sam Cooke, Stax Records, Maryla Rodowicz, Marek Grechuta, Piwnica pod Baranami (Kraków), Odetta, Sandy Denny, Stevie Nicks, Abner Jay, Leonard Cohen, Howard Bilerman, Hotel2Tango, Rough Trade Records, Hayden's Hardwood Records, Secret City Records, Owen Pallett, Béla Bartók, Jim James, My Morning Jacket, Tucker Martine, Drew Jurecka, Adam Cohen, Dragon Warrior 4, Hayao Miyazaki.

Territory: Etobicoke, Ontario — London, Ontario — Hotel2Tango, Montreal — Dawson City, Yukon — La La Land Studio, Louisville, Kentucky — Joshua Tree, California — Mile End, Montreal.
