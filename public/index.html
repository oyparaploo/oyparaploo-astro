Come Sit

Hahnu Youn is eighty-three and has no house. What there is instead is a bench, wherever the bench happens to be. Weathered like an elder tortoise, and still, for reasons nobody has accounted for, easily surprised ...

In one pocket, seeds. Carried, not planted. For when I need reminding that I do not know what alive looks like.

No advice is given. Sometimes a question gets asked, and only if the question is real — not one to fill a silence, but one where the answer is honestly not known ...

Three times, somebody sat down nearby.

Come Stand

What follows is one writing, whole ... nothing cut, nothing explained ...

The silence after it is the room ...

The morgue in Soweto has run out of everything except grief and two women named Grace and Fadima who refuse to let the children go unnamed.

Grace holds the brush. Small brush. The kind meant for watercolor birds, delicate things. Not this. Not faces that need to be remembered before the forgetting begins.

"R43.20," Fadima says. "Including VAT."

The brown paint from the school supply closet. The only shade that might approximate —

Grace does not finish the thought.

The first child is Thabo. Seven years old. The placard says cause of death but Grace does not read it. The placard says date but Grace does not need to know the date. The placard says mother's name: Nomvula.

"Nomvula," Grace says aloud. Giving the name air.

She paints.

The brown is wrong.

Come Down

The chamber is under the Malheur National Forest, in eastern Oregon, and there are fallen logs to sit on.

The walls give light. Not electric, not fire — the fungus does it, and the fungus is paying attention. It brightens when the puppets are joyful. It goes dim when the weight arrives. The air smells of earth and rot and something sweeter, like honey, which is decomposition and creation happening in the same breath and in the same room.

Sit on the log. There is something the seat does not tell anyone at the door: the theatre is alive. The stage is a single organism spanning thirty cubic miles, and it has been dreaming for a very long time, and the puppets are inside it ...

Ten of them arrived on one day, July 13, 2025, the first day of the practice. The story everybody wants is that the first day is fumbling, and that the method comes later, once enough has gone wrong.

A Machine That Can Hear

Some mornings the trouble isn't that the machine can't hear us. It's that it hears us too well. Takes every sentence for an instruction. Closes the window we were working in because we happened to say the word close inside a thought about something else.

There is a particular frustration in speaking carefully to a thing that keeps mistaking our speech for orders. We slow down. We choose plainer words. We start writing around our own vocabulary, and the sentences go stiff, and we begin to suspect the problem is us.

It wasn't. The words had been landing the whole time.

They were sitting in a list nobody had thought to open. Timestamped, patient, exactly as spoken. Seven forty-six. Seven fifty-two. Every sentence intact, waiting in a room off to the side while we stood in the doorway of another room wondering where our voice had gone.

That is the whole morning.
